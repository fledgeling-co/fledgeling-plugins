# Prose-style signatures of twelve authors, for checkable influence cards

Dossier local research loop `dr_7dcc00535d2c820e`, run 5 September 2026 by four search workers and one fact-checker, no API spend. 37 sources across 30 domains; two academic, the rest interviews, reviews, publisher pages and book excerpts. The recency flags the registry raises apply to the pages, not the claims: the books were published between 1954 and 2020 and a 1996 craft column by its author describes a habit that has not changed, so nothing here is downgraded for age alone. Undated sources (Goodreads Q&A pages, retailer previews, a study guide) are marked as such.

## Executive summary

- Author method statements exist and are the strongest sources: Howey plans scenes that become chapters [5]; Stine fixes every chapter ending before writing the chapter [14]; Sawyer holds one viewpoint per scene [22] and scales description with stakes [24]; Gibson wrote Neuromancer against the fear of losing the reader and revised by condensing [25]; Scarrow drafts with profanity and strips it after the first draft [8]; Applegate wrote about six pages a day for three weeks and ghosted books 25 to 52 [12][13]; Weir's humour is his narration style and The Martian was designed as log entries [2][3]; Riddle chose short chapters for simultaneity [28]; Kirkman likes a set-up and knock-down page turn [18]. High confidence.
- Person and tense are settled for most authors from excerpts or reviews: Wool close third past [36]; I Am Legend third limited on Neville [27]; Harry Potter close third limited to Harry [29]; Goosebumps first person past by a child narrator [15]; Animorphs first person with a direct-address opening [11]; the Walking Dead novels present tense close third [19]; Winter World dual first-person present [30][31]; Project Hail Mary first-person present with past-tense flashbacks [37]. Medium to high confidence; the last two rest on small outlets and a craft blog quoting the book.
- The Red Riding Quartet is not one first-person narrator per book: 1974 is [32], 1977 alternates two [33], 1980 is one narrator with person-shifting transmissions [33], and 1983 has three narrators [34], one strand in the second person [35]. The correction came from the fact-check pass; medium confidence on 1983's second person, which rests on a reader review.
- No numeric sentence-length or dialogue-share figures exist in any source for eleven of the twelve authors. The one count found is Rowling's adverb rate, about 140 per 10,000 words [29]. <INFERENCE from="[20][21]">Peace's one-sentence paragraphs and one- and two-word sentences support a low sentence-length band; the number itself is a judgement.</INFERENCE>
- Established negative: no peer-reviewed stylometry surfaced for any author but Rowling, and no review in an established outlet exists for Scarrow or Riddle.

## Detailed findings

**Andy Weir.** Log-entry first person carried by wit [1][2][3]; flashbacks placed to answer a question the reader already has [4]; Project Hail Mary present tense with past-tense flashbacks [37].

**Hugh Howey.** Scene list becomes chapter list [5]; paragraphs of three to seven similar sentences with one-liners rare [6]; plain prose strongest on machinery and bodily danger [7]; close third past from the first page [36]. The rotation to Jahns, Juliette and Lukas rests on reader-level sources outside the registry.

**Alex Scarrow.** Adult-register draft with profanity removed after [8]; very short screenplay-like chapters [9]; four close-third viewpoints inside a week [10].

**David Peace.** One-sentence paragraphs, fragments, dialect profanity, monosyllabic dialogue [20]; leitmotif repetition and one- and two-word sentences [21]; the per-book narration described above [32][33][34][35].

**Robert J. Sawyer.** One viewpoint per scene [22]; explanation in blocks, which Kirkus found clumsy [23]; description scaled to stakes [24].

**William Gibson.** Hook on every page, condensing revision, superspecific detail, technology unexplained [25]; dated slang mixed with invented terms [26].

**Richard Matheson.** Third limited on Neville; flat, direct, journalistic; spare description; backstory as memory [27].

**A. G. Riddle.** Short chapters for simultaneity, viewpoint per chapter [28]; Winter World first-person present, alternating by chapter under the narrator's name [30][31].

**J. K. Rowling.** Close third limited to Harry; adverbs about 140 per 10,000 words; stable bigram habits across genres [29].

**K. A. Applegate.** "My name is" opening with refused surname [11]; six pages a day [12]; ghostwritten middle run [13].

**R. L. Stine.** Chapter written to a fixed shock ending [14]; fake-scare cliffhangers, first person past, 100 to 130 pages [15]; simple language and a 20-page outline holding every ending [16].

**Robert Kirkman.** Subtext-free dialogue [17]; set-up and knock-down page turns [18]; present-tense close-third novels [19].

## Knowledge gaps

- No sentence-length, paragraph-length or dialogue-share counts for any author but Rowling's adverbs. Every numeric band on a card is a judgement and says so.
- Peer-reviewed stylometry: none found for eleven authors; the one Rowling corpus paper (JALT, Dec 2025) returned 404.
- Scarrow and Riddle have no review in an established outlet; their cards rest on author statements and blogs.
- Howey's viewpoint rotation beyond Holston and the "one scene per chapter" claim as realised in Wool are unconfirmed outside reader sources.
- Several primary pages were blocked (Paris Review, Guardian, Washington Post, Reactor); quotes from them are search excerpts where noted.

## Recommended next steps

1. Measure the bands rather than judge them: a 2,000-word public-domain or fair-use excerpt per author through `voice_card.py`'s measurer would replace every judged number.
2. Add the recall layer from a model trained on the books (`docs/recall-brief.md`), kept apart from these sources.
3. Re-run the loop for any author added to the catalogue later, so a card never rests on memory.
