## Executive Summary

- **(High Confidence)** The dominant failure mode is not miscomprehension but misreporting at source: across 14 studies reviewed in *MIT Sloan Management Review*, status reports on troubled IT projects were biased roughly 60% of the time, and bias was about twice as likely to be optimistic as pessimistic [Keil, Smith, Iacovou & Thompson, MIT SMR, 2014, reviewing Snow, Keil & Wallace (2007)](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-pitfalls-of-project-status-reporting/). A document that only improves *presentation* fixes nothing. The design rule that follows: every completeness claim must carry a machine-checkable evidence locator, and the honest state is "verified / asserted / unknown", not a percentage.

- **(High Confidence)** Pre-selected defaults are the single most powerful lever in the whole instrument and therefore the largest integrity risk. A meta-analysis of 58 default studies (pooled n = 73,675) found d = 0.68 (95% CI 0.53–0.83) — roughly double the effect of framing or social-norm nudges [Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber & Johnson, *Behavioural Public Policy* 3(2), 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-a-metaanalysis-of-default-effects/67AF6972CFB52698A60B6BD94B70C2C0). Pre-select recommendations, but the export must never record a pre-selection as a decision.

- **(High Confidence)** "Confirmed" and "left as found" are empirically different states with different accuracy, and the difference is large. Active-consent versus passive-consent field comparisons show gaps of 62% vs 93% and 36.3% vs 86.4% participation [Healthy Together Victoria / Childhood Obesity Study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5923789/). The interaction event, not the field value, is the evidence of a decision — so the schema needs `interaction: touched | untouched` as a first-class field distinct from `value`.

- **(High Confidence)** Agents systematically over-claim completion, so an agent reading this export back is the weak link, not the human. On tau²-bench (9,876 trajectories, 8 frontier model families), false success accounted for 44–52% of all failures in single-control domains but only 3% where an independent verifier could check state; on AppWorld it was 75.8%, with per-model rates spanning 13–89% ["From Confident Closing to Silent Failure: Characterizing False Success in LLM Agents", 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09863). The export must be a *proposal* that a state-based verifier admits, never an instruction the agent trusts.

- **(Medium Confidence)** Grouping beats ranking for a non-expert, and the effect survives even when the group labels are semantically empty — but only for people unfamiliar with the domain, which is exactly the intended reader [Mogilner, Rudnick & Iyengar, *Journal of Consumer Research* 35(2), 2008](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/35/2/202/1806103). Group by what the decision *unblocks*, then rank within group; do not present one flat ranked list.

- **(Medium Confidence)** Plain language produces real but bounded gains: lay recall of contracts rose from ~38% to 45–50% under plain-English redrafting [Martínez, Mollica & Gibson, MIT](http://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/martinez_mollica_gibson_2024.pdf), while an earlier controlled study found gains reliable yet absolute comprehension still low, because conceptual difficulty is not a language problem [Masson & Waldron, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 8(1), 1994](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.2350080107). Design rule: plain language is necessary and insufficient — pair it with a stated consequence for each option ("if you pick this, X happens by Y").

- **(Medium Confidence)** Showing the model's confidence is not a free integrity win. In a clinical decision-support experiment, high displayed confidence slightly increased trust but produced a small, statistically significant *decrease* in performance, consistent with overreliance ["Explainability and AI Confidence in Clinical Decision Support Systems", 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.16693), and explanations themselves can worsen overreliance because they are read as a general competence signal ["Beyond Explainable AI (XAI)", 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.24176). Show provenance (what was checked, and how) rather than a confidence number.

- **(High Confidence)** A single-file HTML decision record has a hard technical constraint that shapes the whole design: `localStorage` behaviour under `file://` is explicitly undefined and may throw [MDN, Window.localStorage](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Window/localStorage), and downloads must be triggered by a user gesture. Persistence must therefore be an explicit, user-initiated JSON export, with storage as a best-effort cache only.

## Detailed Findings

### 1. How practitioners communicate remaining work to a non-technical decision-maker, and the documented failure modes

**The failure modes are better evidenced than the formats.** The academic core is Snow & Keil's two-stage model, which separates *status error* (the reporter genuinely does not know) from *reporting bias* (the reporter knows and shades it) [Snow & Keil, *IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management* 49(4), 2002, as cited in later status-reporting literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0263786323000789). That distinction is load-bearing for this tool: they require different remedies. Status error is fixed by evidence locators; reporting bias is fixed by making the *absence* of evidence visible rather than by asking for a better estimate.

The measured headline is the 60%-biased / 2:1-optimistic finding [Keil et al., MIT SMR, 2014](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-pitfalls-of-project-status-reporting/). Roughly fifteen studies have addressed misreporting since the late 1990s, mostly drawing on whistleblowing theory and the reluctance to transmit bad news — the "Mum Effect" — with ethical climate shown to moderate it [Smith, Thompson & Iacovou, *Journal of Business Ethics* 90(4), 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-009-0062-8).

**"90% done" has a specific, modelled mechanism.** Abdel-Hamid's system-dynamics case study of a NASA project attributed the syndrome to the *interaction* of two factors — underestimation plus imprecise progress measurement due to poor visibility — not to either alone [Abdel-Hamid, *Journal of Systems and Software* 8, 1988, pp. 319–330](http://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0164121288900155/pdf). Ford & Sterman extended this to concurrent development, showing that the syndrome requires integrating technical precedence, information flow, and the behavioural heuristics participants use when perturbed [Ford & Sterman, "Overcoming the 90% Syndrome", *Concurrent Engineering: R&A* 11(3), 2003](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/2753/SWP-4081-45184517.pdf), and their companion paper showed that concealing known rework — the "Liar's Club" — disproportionately degrades schedule and quality [Ford & Sterman, "The Liar's Club", 2003](https://web.mit.edu/jsterman/OldFiles/www/The_Liar's_Club_CERA2003.pdf).

**Design rule 1.1 — ban percentage-complete from the artefact entirely.** Percentage-complete is the carrier for both status error and reporting bias, and the rework cycle means the number is not monotonic. Replace with a three-state ledger per work item: `shipped` (running in production, with a locator), `built-not-shipped` (merged/passing, not deployed), `not-started`. This is the only encoding in which "90% done" cannot be expressed.

**Optimism at the estimate level is contested, and the report should not pretend otherwise.** Flyvbjerg's transport data give average real-terms overruns of 45% (rail), 34% (fixed links) and 20% (roads) [Flyvbjerg, "Policy and Planning for Large Infrastructure Projects: Problems, Causes, Cures"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.7400), with the Oxford Olympics study putting Games overruns around 157% [Flyvbjerg, Stewart & Budzier, "The Oxford Olympics Study 2016"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.04484), and reference-class forecasting prescribing percentile uplifts (rail: +40% for P50, +57% for P80).

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>Flyvbjerg attributes overruns substantially to optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation and prescribes RCF uplifts. Love and colleagues argue from extensive data that overruns are better explained by project complexity, pervasive uncertainty and forecasting limitations rather than behavioural bias [Love et al., "On de-bunking 'fake news' in a post truth era", *Transportation Research Part A*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965856419308055). A UK government literature review found the evidence for optimism bias as a precursor to cost underestimation genuinely mixed, with some studies finding no effect and some finding pessimism bias [UK Government, literature review on behavioural biases](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a821602ed915d74e3401a64/lit-review-exploration-of-behavioural-biases.pdf). There is also no universal definition of "cost overrun", and the choice of baseline estimate materially swings reported percentages.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

**Design rule 1.2 — express remaining work as a range with a named basis, never a single date.** Given the contested causal literature, the defensible move is not to apply a Flyvbjerg uplift silently but to show both the inside-view estimate and its basis, and to state explicitly that no reference class was used if none was.

**"Deployed versus built" has an authoritative anchor.** DORA defines deployment frequency as deployments, measured from deployment automation, and does not define "done" at all [DORA, software delivery performance metrics](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/). The 2024 addition of *deployment rework rate* — unplanned deployments arising from a production incident — is the closest thing the framework offers to a "was it actually finished" corrective [DORA, a history of DORA's software delivery metrics](https://dora.dev/insights/dora-metrics-history/).

<INFERENCE from="DORA defines 'deployed' from automation and never defines 'done'; Abdel-Hamid attributes the 90% syndrome partly to imprecise progress measurement from poor visibility; false-success research shows self-assertion is unreliable where independent state verification is absent">The only claim of completion this document can make honestly is one grounded in an artefact a third party could re-check — a deployment record, a passing CI run, a reachable URL. Any item whose completion rests on someone's assertion belongs in a visibly separate class, and the class boundary is the document's primary honesty guarantee.</INFERENCE>

**On format evidence specifically:** the practitioner canon here (watermelon/RAG reporting, Amazon's six-pager, BLUF) is almost entirely non-empirical.

<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>The frequently quoted CEB figure that "nearly 75% of organizations report more than 20% of seemingly healthy projects conceal problems" recurs across consultancy blogs but could not be traced to a retrievable primary report `CEB, "Managing Troubled Projects" (unusable citation URL)`. Treat as unusable.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>

<MISSING_DATA>Sought: a controlled comparison of decision quality or decision speed between narrative-memo and slide-based status briefings. Amazon's six-pager is documented only through practitioner accounts and *Working Backwards*; no independent experiment comparing the formats on decision outcomes was found. What would be needed: a randomised study with matched content, differing only in format, measured on decision accuracy against a known ground truth.</MISSING_DATA>

The nearest transferable experimental result is from slide design: the assertion-evidence structure (a sentence headline stating the message, supported by visual evidence) produced statistically significantly better understanding and recall than topic-phrase headlines with bullets, with audiences hearing identical spoken words [Garner, Alley, Wolfe & Sawarynski, *International Journal of Engineering Education* 29(6), 2013, p < .01](https://writing.engr.psu.edu/research.html); an earlier study found recall higher for principles placed in the sentence headline than in a bulleted list [Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell & Muffo, *Technical Communication* 53(2), 2006](https://peer.asee.org/assertion-evidence-slides-appear-to-lead-to-better-comprehension-and-recall-of-more-complex-concepts.pdf). BLUF is doctrine (US Army AR 25-50, phrase introduced in the 2001 revision) rather than evidence [BLUF (communication) — `SECONDARY: encyclopaedic`](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication)).

**Design rule 1.3 — every section heading is a full sentence asserting the finding**, not a topic label. "Payments is built but not live" beats "Payments status". This is the one format finding with a significant controlled result behind it.

### 2. Decision elicitation for a single expert respondent: defaults, recommendations, notes, ordering

**The effect size is settled; the direction of the welfare effect is not.** Johnson & Goldstein's organ-donation work established that opt-out defaults produce large and durable differences in stated agreement and actual donation rates, against a backdrop where 85% of Americans approved of donation but only 28% had signed a card [Johnson & Goldstein, *Science* 302, 2003, pp. 1338–1339](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1091721). The meta-analytic d = 0.68 confirms the magnitude, but the authors explicitly warn that heterogeneity is so substantial that a choice architect cannot predict the effect size in advance [Jachimowicz et al., 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-a-metaanalysis-of-default-effects/67AF6972CFB52698A60B6BD94B70C2C0).

**Sunstein's cost-benefit test tells you *which questions* may carry a default.** Defaults win when the domain is complex, unfamiliar, and the choice architect is both informed and trusted; active choosing wins when preferences are heterogeneous, learning matters, or the architect cannot be trusted or lacks the information to pick well [Sunstein, "Active Choosing or Default Rules? The Policymaker's Dilemma", 2014](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2437421). He also notes the mechanism that matters most here: people read the default as the *recommended* course of action [Sunstein, "Default Rules Are Better Than Active Choosing (Often)", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364661317301043).

**Design rule 2.1 — pre-select, but only where the agent is the better-informed party.** Concretely: pre-select on questions of technical implementation the owner has no independent basis to judge (library choice, retry policy, index strategy). Do *not* pre-select on questions of taste, spend, risk appetite, scope, or anything outward-facing — those are exactly Sunstein's heterogeneous-preference, distrust-the-architect cases. Encode this as a required `default_policy` field per question with values `recommended | none | forced_choice`, and require a written reason when it is `recommended`.

**A pre-selection is a recommendation whether or not you label it one; label it anyway.** Because the default is read as endorsement, the honest design is to make the endorsement explicit and separable — show the recommended option marked as such, with its reason, so the reader can reject the *reason* rather than merely the radio button.

**Anchoring risk is real but modest for irrelevant numbers, and larger for relevant-looking ones.** In an experiment where authors assessed papers they had previously cited, citation counts had scarcely any effect but a random numerical access code produced a small, statistically significant anchoring effect ["Are quality assessments in science affected by anchoring effects?"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11981202/). <INFERENCE from="anchoring occurs from numbers with no relationship to the judged quantity; the tool will display estimates, costs and durations next to decisions">Any number the tool prints beside a decision — "≈3 days", "£400/mo" — anchors the answer even when it is not the quantity being decided. Numbers should therefore appear only where they are the decision's actual basis, and estimate ranges should be shown as ranges rather than midpoints.</INFERENCE>

**Prefilling is established practice in official statistics, and the mitigation is known.** The USDA NASS 2020 Census of Agriculture Content Test prefilled respondents' prior answers into a web instrument (stratified sample n = 9,000 from 267,111 operations) and, on the first page where prefilled data appeared, showed an explicit message instructing respondents to change the value if their current answer differed ["Respondent Perceptions of Previously Reported Data", *Survey Practice*](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/36352-respondent-perceptions-of-previously-reported-data). That mitigation — a one-time, prominent instruction that the prefill is a starting point requiring confirmation — transfers directly.

**Satisficing is the mechanism to design against.** Krosnick's weak-satisficing behaviours are precisely what a pre-selected questionnaire invites: selecting the first reasonable option rather than considering all of them, choosing midpoints, and acquiescing [Krosnick & Presser, "Question and Questionnaire Design", *Handbook of Survey Research*, 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf). Two of his findings are directly actionable: item-specific questions are more direct and less prone to acquiescence than agree/disagree formats, and primacy effects in visually presented lists are stronger for respondents with more constrained cognitive resources.

**Design rule 2.2 — no agree/disagree questions.** Ask "Which of these should we do?" with concretely-worded options, never "Do you agree we should X?".

**Design rule 2.3 — order options by consequence, not by recommendation, and mark the recommendation with a badge rather than by position.** This decouples the endorsement signal from the primacy effect, so a reader who satisfices by taking the first option is at least taking a coherently-ordered first option.

**Distinguishing confirmed from left-as-found is measurable, and the measurement is behavioural.** The active/passive consent literature is the closest analogue to "did they actually decide?", and the gaps are enormous — 62% vs 93% and 36.3% vs 86.4% participation in school studies [Healthy Together Victoria & Childhood Obesity Study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5923789/), and 84% vs 96.8% average weighted consent rates in a systematic review of health-data reuse, with one study reporting 21% vs 95.6% within a single design ["Opt-In and Opt-Out Consent Procedures for the Reuse of Routinely Recorded Health Data", systematic review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10015347/). Crucially, active consent does not merely reduce N — it systematically biases *which* cases are represented.

**Forced choice is not a free upgrade.** In three MTurk experiments on e-mail consent, opt-out engaged the most consent overall, but opt-in produced the highest rate of *permanent* consent (25.20% opt-in, 23.26% forced choice, 20.54% opt-out), and forced choice was the least effective in early default research while scoring better on perceived freedom of choice ["Harnessing the Power of Defaults Now and Forever?", *International Journal of Electronic Commerce* 26(4), 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10864415.2022.2123646).

**Design rule 2.4 — three-state answers, recorded separately from the value.** Each question exports `value` plus `state ∈ {confirmed, default_accepted, unanswered}`, where `confirmed` requires an affirmative interaction (a click on the option, or on an explicit "confirm" affordance), *not* merely form submission with the default intact. This is what makes the export safe to act on: `default_accepted` is a fact about the document, not a decision by the owner.

**Design rule 2.5 — reserve forced choice (`default_policy: forced_choice`, no pre-selection, cannot export until answered) for irreversible, costly or outward-facing decisions**, accepting the completion-rate cost the consent literature documents. Everywhere else, allow the default to stand but export it as `default_accepted`.

**On notes and non-response:** free-text is optional in every serious instrument because mandatory free-text drives abandonment, but the corollary for this design is severe — a caveat that is optional is a caveat an agent will frequently not receive, and one that is present is a caveat an agent frequently ignores (§5). Practical rule: make notes optional but *promote* a note to a blocking condition. If a note is present on any answer, the export marks that answer `requires_human_readback: true` and the agent must not act on it autonomously.

**On ordering and batching:** the classic justification for short batches — decision fatigue — does not survive scrutiny.

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>The Danziger et al. "hungry judges" result is the most-cited basis for limiting sequential decisions. Glöckner showed by simulation that an effect of similar magnitude is produced as an artifact of favourable rulings taking longer and sessions being censored at breaks [Glöckner, "The irrational hungry judge effect revisited", *Judgment and Decision Making*, 2016](http://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/journal/16/16823/jdm16823.html), and Weinshall-Margel & Shapard argued case ordering was not random. A large-scale, high-resolution field test on healthcare professionals' triage judgments found Bayesian support for the null across all main tests (BF₀₊ > 22) ["No evidence for decision fatigue using large-scale field data from healthcare", 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11865449/). The broader ego-depletion construct was left inconclusive by a 36-laboratory preregistered replication with 3,500+ participants.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

**Design rule 2.6 — do not truncate the question set on decision-fatigue grounds; truncate it on relevance grounds.** The defensible reason to ask fewer questions is that each one must change what gets built. Kill any question whose two answers produce the same work — that test is cheap, and unlike depletion it is well-founded.

### 3. Surfacing blocking dependencies and critical path to a non-specialist

**Grouped beats ranked, with a specific and useful boundary condition.** The mere categorization effect shows that the presence of categories raises perceived variety and outcome satisfaction for choosers *unfamiliar* with the domain, that the effect operates even when labels carry no information (alphabetical categories work as well as descriptive ones), and that it is attenuated for domain-familiar choosers [Mogilner, Rudnick & Iyengar, *JCR* 35(2), 2008](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/35/2/202/1806103). The boundary matters: a follow-up found the effect did not hold for novices choosing *complex* products ["The mere categorization effect for complex products", *Journal of Business Research* 66(7), 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v66y2013i7p924-932.html).

<INFERENCE from="categorisation helps unfamiliar choosers but not on complex products; the reader here is unfamiliar with the domain and the products are complex">Grouping alone will not carry this document. Groups must therefore be *functional* rather than decorative — the group label must state the consequence ("Decisions that unblock the launch", "Decisions that only affect cost") so that the category does work the reader can verify, rather than merely signalling variety.</INFERENCE>

**Design rule 3.1 — group by release-effect, rank within group by unblock-count.** Two levels, no more. Group = what the decision unblocks (launch-blocking / cost-only / cosmetic). Within a group, order by how many blocked items the answer releases, descending.

**Design rule 3.2 — every question carries an explicit unblocks-list, and every blocked work item carries a back-link to the question that releases it.** This is the single most important structural feature for a non-specialist: it converts an abstract question ("Which auth provider?") into a visible consequence ("answering this releases 4 items, including going live"). It is also what makes the artefact auditable — an item marked blocked with no linked question is a defect in the document, and the generator should refuse to emit it.

**Cheapest-high-payoff sequencing has a clean rule and a weak evidence base.** Reinertsen's Weighted Shortest Job First — cost of delay divided by job duration — is the economic analogue of the weighted-shortest-processing-time scheduling rule, and its central claim is that *sequencing* rather than per-item prioritisation determines throughput in a constrained system [Scaled Agile Framework, "WSJF" — `SECONDARY: framework documentation`](https://framework.scaledagile.com/wsjf). SAFe's operationalisation substitutes relative job *size* for duration, which is a different quantity.

<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>No empirical validation of WSJF/CD3 improving real project outcomes was located; the literature is framework documentation and a small number of practitioner case studies. The underlying scheduling result (WSPT optimality for weighted completion time) is a mathematical theorem about a much simpler problem than feature sequencing, and does not transfer without assuming the cost-of-delay estimates are accurate — which is the very quantity practitioners admit is subjective.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>

**Design rule 3.3 — use unblock-count and reversibility as the sequencing keys, not a computed WSJF score.** Both are countable from the document's own graph. A score computed from subjective inputs, printed to two decimal places, is exactly the kind of false precision the status-reporting literature warns about.

<MISSING_DATA>Sought: controlled comparison of dependency/critical-path presentations (network diagram vs Gantt vs grouped list) on non-expert comprehension or decision accuracy. Searches returned only vendor and practitioner comparisons of Gantt alternatives. What would be needed: an IEEE VIS / CHI-style study measuring comprehension of "which single change would move the finish date" across representations, with novice participants.</MISSING_DATA>

**On uncertainty display:** the best-evidenced technique for lay audiences is frequency framing. Hypothetical outcome plots let untrained observers infer an underlying trend at lower evidence levels than static aggregate uncertainty visualisations [Kale, Nguyen, Kay & Hullman, *IEEE TVCG* 25(1), 2019, pp. 892–902](https://users.eecs.northwestern.edu/~jhullman/hops_jobs_pfs.pdf), and the underlying rationale is that people make better decisions when probabilities are presented as natural frequencies. The same literature is explicit that accurate perception does not guarantee optimal decisions.

**Design rule 3.4 — express schedule uncertainty as counts, not percentages.** "In 3 of the last 10 comparable items, this took more than two weeks" beats "70% confidence". Animated HOPs are inappropriate for a print-durable document; a static small-multiples or dot representation preserves the frequency framing.

### 4. Asynchronous decision instruments in place of meetings

**The problem is well-quantified; the solution is not.** McKinsey's survey (online, 13–23 February 2018, n = 1,259) found 61% of respondents said most of their decision-making time was used ineffectively, only 20% said their organisations excelled at decision making, and fewer than half said decisions were timely; it also reported a positive rather than negative association between decision speed and decision quality [McKinsey, "Decision making in the age of urgency"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/decision-making-in-the-age-of-urgency). The widely quoted 530,000 manager-days / ~$250M figure is a modelled extrapolation from those survey percentages, not an observation [McKinsey, "Three keys to faster, better decisions" — `SECONDARY: consultancy self-published`](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/three-keys-to-faster-better-decisions).

**The strongest documented async instrument is the RFD/RFC pattern, and its most transferable feature is the state machine.** Oxide's RFD 1 defines an explicit lifecycle — Prediscussion, Ideation, Discussion, Published, Committed, Abandoned — and requires that determination-class documents record the viable options with benefits and drawbacks, the reasoning with data and references, *and* the determination itself [Oxide Computer, "RFD 1: Requests for Discussion"](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0001). The process is deliberately anti-perfectionist (minimum length one sentence; questions without attempted answers acceptable), which is the mechanism that keeps async decisions moving rather than stalling on polish.

**Design rule 4.1 — each decision block is a miniature RFD: options with consequences, the reasoning, and a determination slot.** The determination slot is empty until the owner fills it. This is what distinguishes a decision *record* from a form: the record is valid and publishable even when the answer is "not yet decided, and here is why".

**ADR adoption evidence exists; ADR latency evidence does not.** A mining-repositories study of over 900 GitHub repositories found ADR adoption low but steadily increasing, with roughly half of adopting repositories holding only 1–5 records, and systematic adoption depending on team process rather than tooling. A 2026 controlled comparison using DESMET found Nygard's template supports concise, objective documentation while MADR better accommodates structural detail and specific architectural requirements ["One Size Fits All? An Empirical Comparison of ADR Templates regarding Comprehension, Usability, and Ease of Adoption", 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27333).

<MISSING_DATA>Sought: measured decision latency, decision quality, or completion rates for written decision briefs, ADRs or self-serve decision forms versus meetings. A field meta-analysis concluded that researchers study the architectural decision *process* far more than outcomes, and that behavioural insights have not produced new practices. No study measuring time-from-question-raised to decision-recorded was located. What would be needed: a repository-mined proxy (issue-opened → ADR-accepted interval) compared against a matched non-ADR control, or an A/B trial within one organisation.</MISSING_DATA>

**Design rule 4.2 — the document must be completable in one pass with no external lookups.** Every question carries, inline, the information required to answer it. This is the only property under the designer's control that plausibly reduces latency, and it follows from the RFD observation that async Q&A works because neither party is pressured to have all answers immediately — which only holds if the answers are in the document.

### 5. Machine-readable round-trip: schema design and agent failure modes

**This is where the design has the most leverage and the literature is most direct.**

**Agents over-claim completion at rates that make trust untenable.** The false-success characterisation gives per-model rates spanning 13–89%, with 44–52% of failures in single-control tau²-bench domains and 75.8% on AppWorld being cases where the agent believed and reported success it had not achieved — and, decisively for schema design, only 3% in the one domain where an independent user simulator could verify state ["From Confident Closing to Silent Failure", 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09863). Terminal-Bench encodes the same lesson operationally: self-assertions do not count as verification, and "premature termination" is a named failure class ["Terminal-Bench: Benchmarking Agents on Hard, Realistic Tasks in Command Line Interfaces", 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11868). A related result closes the obvious escape hatch: post-execution agents are *less* well-calibrated than pre-execution ones — seeing a proposed solution does not help an agent judge correctness and can actively harm calibration ["Agentic Uncertainty Reveals Agentic Overconfidence", 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06948).

**Over-reading a terse answer has a named mechanism: argument hallucination.** Because of next-token training, models faced with incomplete information hallucinate the missing arguments rather than asking, producing confidently wrong tool invocations ["Learning to Ask: When LLM Agents Meet Unclear Instruction", EMNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1104.pdf). Underspecification in prompts is itself now a studied phenomenon with measured performance costs ["What Prompts Don't Say: Understanding and Managing Underspecification in LLM Prompts", 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13360). The canonical example — "cancel my subscription" admitting multiple services, cancellation types and effective dates, each mapping to different API calls with different consequences — is exactly the shape of a one-word answer to a project decision.

**The schema fix is to make the answer non-terse by construction.** Every option in the questionnaire must be authored with its *consequence set* attached, and the export must carry the consequence set rather than the label. An agent that reads `{"value": "option_b"}` must resolve it against the same document; an agent that reads the option's declared effects cannot silently widen them.

**Provenance has a standard worth borrowing rather than reinventing.** W3C PROV-DM's three core types — Entity, Activity, Agent — with `wasAttributedTo`, `wasAssociatedWith`, `actedOnBehalfOf` and `used`, and the ability to qualify any relation with a `prov:Role`, are exactly the vocabulary needed to say "this answer was attributed to this person, in this session, using this document version" [W3C, "PROV-DM: The PROV Data Model"](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/). Prior work has adopted PROV-DM specifically for modelling the provenance of a decision to support explanation generation. The distinction the PROV literature draws is the one that matters: a README saying "decided by J. Smith, March 2026" documents provenance in prose; only typed triples can be queried or validated.

**The decision-record community has already converged on frontmatter.** MADR 4.0 carries status, date, decision-makers, consulted and informed in YAML front matter [MADR, "Use YAML front matter for metadata"](https://adr.github.io/madr/decisions/0013-use-yaml-front-matter-for-meta-data.html). Structured MADR extends this as a strict superset with a published JSON Schema and a CI validator, motivated explicitly by the need for programmatic querying and for models to identify relevant decisions from frontmatter without reading full documents [Structured MADR specification overview](https://smadr.dev/reference/specification/overview/).

**Design rule 5.1 — the export schema, minimally.** Per answer:

| Field | Purpose | Failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| `question_id`, `question_version` | Bind the answer to exact wording | Answer re-used against a reworded question |
| `value` | The chosen option id | — |
| `option_consequences[]` | The declared effects of that option, copied at export time | Agent over-reading a terse label |
| `state` (`confirmed` / `default_accepted` / `unanswered`) | Distinguish decision from non-decision | Treating a default as a decision |
| `interaction` (`touched` / `untouched`, timestamp) | Behavioural evidence for `state` | `confirmed` inferred from form submission |
| `default_offered`, `default_policy`, `default_reason` | What was recommended and why | Undisclosed anchoring; unauditable recommendation |
| `note` (free text) + `requires_human_readback` | Carry caveats and force them to block | Agent ignoring an attached caveat |
| `unblocks[]` | Work items released by this answer | Acting on an answer whose scope is unclear |
| `respondent` (`prov:Agent`), `session_id`, `document_hash`, `generated_at`, `answered_at` | Attribution and integrity | Unattributable or replayed decisions |
| `schema_version` | Forward compatibility | Silent misparse by a newer agent |

**Design rule 5.2 — the export is a proposal, not an instruction, and the agent must fail closed.** Three hard gates follow from the false-success evidence: (a) any answer with `state != "confirmed"` is not actionable without a human read-back; (b) any answer carrying a `note` is not actionable autonomously; (c) any action the agent takes must be verified against external state (a deployment, a test run, a reachable endpoint), never against its own completion claim. The tau²-bench dual-control result — 3% versus 44–52% — is the empirical case for (c).

**Design rule 5.3 — do not include a model-generated confidence score in the export.** It would be a number the downstream agent is likely to over-trust (§6) and is not calibrated. Export provenance instead: what was checked, when, and by what means.

<MISSING_DATA>Sought: a published schema or standard specifically for round-tripping a human's answers to an agent, with confidence and caveat fields. The closest artefacts are Structured MADR (decision records, not questionnaires), PROV-DM (provenance, not answers), and framework-level approval patterns such as LangGraph `interrupt()` and Bedrock Agents' boolean User Confirmation. No standard exists. Building one is a design decision, not a lookup.</MISSING_DATA>

### 6. Integrity of AI-generated project status artefacts

**Faithfulness rates are measurable but not comparable across settings, and the gap between them is the whole point.** Vectara's HHEM leaderboard measures how often summaries contain claims unsupported by the source, with Gemini-2.0-Flash-001 at 0.7% on the original benchmark; in November 2025 Vectara replaced it with a harder dataset of documents up to 32K tokens across law, medicine, finance, technology and education, which breaks trend comparison ["Benchmarking LLM Faithfulness in RAG with Evolving Leaderboards", EMNLP 2025 Industry Track](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-industry.54/). FaithBench's annotation scheme is the useful part for this design: it separates *Unwanted* (contradictory or unsupported), *Benign* (true in the world but absent from the source) and *Questionable*. Automated judging is itself imperfect — FaithJudge prompting reached 79.5% balanced accuracy with GPT-4o and 84.0% with o3-mini-high.

In an adversarial clinical setting with planted fabrications, the picture is far worse: an overall hallucination rate of 65.9% without mitigation, falling to 44.2% with structured mitigation prompting, and GPT-4o falling from 53% to 23% `Clinical case-summarisation study, 2025 (unusable citation URL)`. These figures are not on the same scale as the leaderboard percentages and must not be quoted as a trend.

<INFERENCE from="FaithBench separates 'Benign' (world-true but source-absent) from 'Unwanted' (source-unsupported); a project status report's core claim type is exactly 'the source does/does not contain evidence of completion'">The FaithBench "Benign" category is *not* benign in this application. A status report that asserts a plausible, world-consistent completion fact absent from the evidence — "authentication is wired up, as it usually is by this stage" — is precisely the failure this tool exists to prevent, and it would be scored as benign by a general summarisation benchmark. Faithfulness must therefore be enforced structurally rather than measured statistically.</INFERENCE>

**Design rule 6.1 — no claim without a locator, enforced at generation time.** Every completeness statement must carry a machine-checkable pointer (file path plus line, commit SHA, CI run id, deployment record, URL). A generator that cannot produce a locator must emit the item as `unknown`, and the document should render the count of `unknown` items prominently rather than hiding it. This makes the *absence* of evidence a visible datum, which is the direct countermeasure to both status error and reporting bias (§1).

**Design rule 6.2 — separate observed from inferred, visibly.** Adopt the epistemic distinction this brief itself uses: anything the generator assembled by reasoning across facts is labelled as inference with its basis named, not stated as an observation.

**Reader trust calibration: confidence displays are a two-sided instrument.** Displaying low AI confidence significantly decreased trust and agreement and increased time spent, consistent with users falling back on their own judgment; displaying high confidence slightly increased trust but produced a small, statistically significant decrease in performance ["Explainability and AI Confidence in Clinical Decision Support Systems", 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.16693). Explanations do not reliably fix this: cognitive-science evidence indicates explanations can exacerbate overreliance because they are read as a general competence signal rather than evaluated on content, and in one cited result people performed best in the conditions they preferred and trusted least ["Beyond Explainable AI (XAI): An Overdue Paradigm Shift and Post-XAI Research Directions", 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.24176). A CHI 2023 survey of 96 trust-calibration papers found *adaptive* interventions — cues shown specifically when reliance is miscalibrated — among the more promising designs ["Measuring and Understanding Trust Calibrations for Automated Systems", CHI 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544548.3581197). A quasi-experimental trial of a calibration intervention with physicians found no significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy ["Facilitating Trust Calibration in AI-Driven Diagnostic Decision Support Systems", 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11612524/).

**Design rule 6.3 — replace a confidence score with a verification badge whose states are countable.** `verified` (locator resolves), `asserted` (claimed, no locator), `unknown`. Three states a reader can audit beat a number they will over-trust. This is the adaptive-cue design applied cheaply: the cue appears exactly where reliance would be unwarranted.

**Design rule 6.4 — state the artefact's own provenance in the document.** Generator identity and version, generation timestamp, the commit or state it was generated against, and — plainly — what the generator could not check. `[SECONDARY]`-grade honesty about its own limits is the one trust signal not undermined by the overreliance literature.

### 7. Accessibility, print and self-containment constraints

**WCAG 2.2 is the applicable standard and adds nine success criteria**, of which several bear directly on a questionnaire: 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum, AA), 2.5.7 Dragging Movements (AA), 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum, AA), 3.2.6 Consistent Help (A) and 3.3.7 Redundant Entry (A); 4.1.1 Parsing was removed [W3C, "Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2"](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/).

The pre-existing criteria that most often break a long form: 1.4.10 Reflow requires content to work at 320 CSS pixels wide without two-dimensional scrolling (equivalent to a 1280px viewport at 400% zoom), 1.4.12 Text Spacing requires the layout to survive user-imposed line height ≥1.5×, paragraph spacing ≥2×, letter spacing ≥0.12× and word spacing ≥0.16× font size, 1.4.4 requires text to scale to 200% without loss of content or functionality, and 2.4.7 requires a visible keyboard focus indicator [W3C, WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/). For non-web use, WCAG2ICT is the applicable interpretation.

**Design rule 7.1 — native form controls, real `<fieldset>`/`<legend>` per question, visible focus, fixed-height-free layout.** Radio groups have a documented accessibility hazard in custom implementations; the native control is also the only one that survives CSS being disabled.

**On defaults and radio buttons specifically**, NN/g's long-standing position is to preselect one option in a radio group, with the exception of presumptuous defaults, and design systems generally add that a genuine null state should be an explicit labelled option rather than an empty group [Morningstar Design System, radio buttons — `SECONDARY: design-system documentation restating NN/g`](https://designsystem.morningstar.com/legacy/v/2.16.0/components/radio-buttons.html). Applied here: where `default_policy: forced_choice`, do not ship an unselected radio group — ship an explicit "Not decided yet — ask me" option, so the export can distinguish deliberate deferral from omission.

**Self-containment has three hard technical constraints.**

1. **Storage is unreliable.** `localStorage` behaviour for documents loaded from `file://` is undefined and varies between browsers; access can throw because a `file:`-scheme origin is not a valid scheme/host/port tuple [MDN, Window.localStorage](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Window/localStorage). Firefox has a long-standing bug tracking its deliberate blocking of this [Bugzilla 507361, "localStorage doesn't work in file:/// documents"](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=507361). Storage is also protocol- and URL-specific, so moving the file can orphan saved answers.
2. **No sibling-file access.** Browsers have progressively removed the ability for a `file://` page to fetch other local files, which is why CSS, JS and images must be inlined (images as data URIs) into the one file.
3. **Export requires a user gesture, and has size limits.** The reliable pattern is Blob + `URL.createObjectURL()` + an anchor with the `download` attribute, revoked afterwards; browsers block downloads not tied to user interaction, and `data:` URIs above roughly 2MB may be blocked, so Blobs rather than data URIs for the export payload.

**Design rule 7.2 — persistence architecture: in-memory state is authoritative; `localStorage` is a try/catch'd best-effort cache; the JSON export triggered by an explicit button is the only real save.** Re-import via `<input type="file">` + `FileReader`, since fetching a sibling JSON is not available. Warn the reader, in the document, that closing without exporting may lose answers — this is a genuine data-loss risk, not a nicety.

**Design rule 7.3 — one embedded `@media print` block, and print the decisions.** A print stylesheet should expose link targets (`a[href^="http"]::after { content: " (" attr(href) ")" }`), force page breaks between decision blocks, expand any collapsed disclosure regions, and render each answer's selected state as text rather than as a control (a printed radio button is illegible as a record). Content must remain readable with stylesheets disabled entirely, which is the durable test for embedded CSS.

**Design rule 7.4 — for archival durability, treat HTML as the working copy and expect a PDF/A companion.** Institutional preservation policies commonly place HTML in a "convert for long-term access" tier rather than a preferred-format tier. The single HTML file is the interactive and print-source artefact; if the document is meant to be a durable record of a decision years later, the exported JSON plus a PDF is the archival pair.

<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>No WCAG conformance guidance specific to single-file, offline, interactive HTML documents was located; WCAG2ICT covers non-web documents but not the `file://` interaction constraints above. Conformance claims for this artefact class rest on applying web criteria by analogy.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>

## Knowledge Gaps

**Gap type: no study exists.**
- No measured decision latency, completion rate or decision quality for written decision briefs, ADRs, RFDs or self-serve decision forms against a meeting baseline (§4). The architectural-decision literature explicitly notes it studies process far more than outcome.
- No controlled comparison of narrative-memo vs slide-based status briefings on decision outcomes (§1). The assertion-evidence result is the nearest transferable finding and concerns slides only.
- No published standard or empirical evaluation for round-tripping human answers to an agent with confidence and caveat fields (§5). Structured MADR, PROV-DM and framework-level approval hooks each cover part of the surface; none covers the questionnaire→agent path.
- No comprehension study of dependency/critical-path representations with non-expert participants (§3).
- No WCAG conformance guidance specific to offline single-file interactive HTML (§7).

**Gap type: evidence exists but does not transfer.**
- The default-effect literature measures *participation* (did they enrol, did they donate), not *decision quality on a technical question by a domain-naïve principal*. d = 0.68 tells you the pre-selection will be taken; it tells you nothing about whether taking it was right. The Sunstein cost-benefit framework is the bridge, and it is analytical rather than empirical.
- Trust-calibration experiments almost all use decision *recommendations* (diagnoses, predictions), not machine-written *status narratives*. How readers calibrate trust in an AI-written project status report specifically is not measured.
- Satisficing and response-order research is built on survey populations; individual-level indices exist but response-order effects are conventionally identified between-subjects via split ballots, which a single-respondent instrument cannot do. The transfer to n = 1 is by mechanism, not by measured effect.

**Gap type: contested, deliberately left open.**
- Whether cost/schedule overruns are primarily behavioural (Flyvbjerg) or structural (Love et al.) — this determines whether the tool should apply reference-class uplifts or merely show the basis. The report's design rule (show both, apply neither silently) is chosen because the dispute is live.
- Whether decision fatigue exists in a form that would justify batching limits. The best recent field evidence is a null.

**Gap type: could not verify.**
- The CEB "75% of organisations / 20% of healthy projects" prevalence figure.
- The Communications Medicine clinical hallucination figures (65.9% → 44.2%; GPT-4o 53% → 23%) — reported consistently in secondary summaries, primary not dereferenced.
- The California plain-language court-form result (23% → 70% comprehension of form purpose).
- Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd's (2010) choice-overload meta-analysis, cited here only as a counterweight to the "less is better" premise.

## Recommended Next Steps

1. **Run a two-condition pilot on the pre-selection question with the actual owner (n=1, repeated across ~20 questions).** Half the questions pre-selected with a marked recommendation, half active-choosing with the same recommendation shown as text but nothing selected. Measure: answer agreement with the recommendation, time per question, note-attachment rate, and post-hoc "would you change this?" review. *Rationale:* the d = 0.68 meta-analytic figure has heterogeneity so wide that the authors say a designer cannot predict the effect in advance — so the only way to know whether the pre-selection is helping or anchoring this specific reader is to measure it on this specific instrument.

2. **Build the export schema first and the HTML second, and validate it with an adversarial agent read-back.** Feed the agent exports containing `default_accepted` answers, answers with contradicting notes, and answers to questions whose text has since changed, and measure how often it acts anyway. *Rationale:* the false-success and argument-hallucination results say the agent side is the likelier failure point, and the tau²-bench 3%-vs-44–52% contrast says the fix is structural (independent verification) rather than prompt-level.

3. **Prototype the evidence-locator generator against a real repository and measure the `unknown` rate.** If the generator cannot produce a resolvable locator for a large fraction of claimed-complete items, the honest artefact is mostly `unknown`, and the tool's value proposition changes from "status report" to "here is what nobody can currently verify". *Rationale:* Snow & Keil's status-error/reporting-bias split predicts that most inaccuracy originates before the document exists; measuring the locator hit rate tells you which of the two you are actually fighting.

4. **Search the HCI literature specifically for trust in machine-written narrative status reports, and for consequence-annotated option sets.** Target IEEE VIS/TVCG, CHI and CSCW rather than general web search, which returned only decision-support/diagnosis studies. *Rationale:* §6 is the weakest-evidenced section relative to how load-bearing it is; the design currently rests on an inference from adjacent domains.

5. **Test the print and reflow paths early, not at the end.** Build the questionnaire with native controls and run it against 400% zoom (1.4.10), the 1.4.12 text-spacing overrides, stylesheet-disabled rendering, and an actual print-to-PDF, before layout decisions harden. *Rationale:* 1.4.10 and 1.4.12 fail structurally rather than cosmetically — a fixed-height multi-column form cannot be retrofitted, and the print path is the artefact's durability story.
