### Research Question 1: What classes of user-visible defect escape a mature, requirement-driven UI test campaign, and which methods empirically catch them?

#### Escaping Defects in Multi-Step End-to-End Journeys

A mature campaign of the sort you describe, with a requirement inventory, high-dimensional t-way sampling over surface, state, viewport, theme, role, locale, data-shape, modality, and execution plane, a sophisticated oracle ladder, mutation-armed assertions, numerous sweeps, and differential checks against a design of record, is unusually strong by current industry standards.[7][9] (Confidence: High) Yet the empirical and methodological literature shows recurring defect classes that such campaigns typically under-exercise, largely because they depend on temporal phenomena, multi-step workflows, or interactions between the UI and evolving external state rather than on static UI snapshots.[1][2][4][11][20] (Confidence: High)

End-to-end user journey testing is repeatedly emphasized as necessary to catch defects that occur only when operations are chained across multiple surfaces, devices, or roles and when state accumulates over time.[1][2] (Confidence: High) Vendor-supported but conceptually aligned sources point out that teams who “test in silos or wait until the final stage” leave gaps in full workflow test coverage, and that critical end-test cases go untested when only per-screen or short-path interactions are considered.[1] (Confidence: Medium) Scenario-guided GUI testing work, such as ScenGen, is motivated by the observation that existing approaches focus on general exploration rather than completion of specific testing scenarios, leading to missed coverage of critical functionalities.[2] (Confidence: High) ScenGen conceptualizes automated GUI testing as an iterative, human-like process consisting of five stages—observation, decision-making, execution, result inspection and feedback, and recording—precisely to support flexible reasoning over multi-step workflows without requiring predefined action templates.[2] (Confidence: High) This design aims to emulate the way human testers follow business flows end to end, accumulating state, revisiting surfaces, and reacting to intermediate outcomes.<INFERENCE from="[1][2]">Unlike your campaign’s t-way sampling across static dimensions, scenario-guided methods explicitly optimize for temporal coverage along coherent business journeys, exposing defects whose triggers involve multiple interdependent steps and state transitions.</INFERENCE> (Confidence: Medium)

Concrete defect classes that escape static, per-surface campaigns include accumulated-state anomalies, back/forward navigation issues, deep-link re-entry flaws, interruption and crash-resume problems, partial completion against a server that has already committed, cross-surface staleness, aged-account edge cases, and upgrade-path state bugs.[3][4][11][20] (Confidence: High) Deep-link vulnerabilities show how URIs granting direct access to specific activities in web or mobile applications can be misused when parameters passed in deep links are handled insecurely in application code, leading to data theft, XSS, IDOR, or CSRF bypass.[3] (Confidence: High) These defects often require that the app be entered via a deep link, perhaps from a specific external context or after prior navigation, and that particular parametric combinations occur; simple surface-level tests that click on in-app navigation controls will not exercise these flows.[3] (Confidence: High) Best-practice guidance stresses that deep links should never be used to pass sensitive information and that user-controlled data should not trigger sensitive actions without validation and sanitization.[3] (Confidence: High) Detecting such problems requires test sweeps that model deep-link URIs as a distinct modality, driving the app from external entry points with crafted parameters, including malicious ones, and observing both UI behaviour and backend effects.<INFERENCE from="[3]">Because your campaign already includes a security surface sweep, augmenting it with explicit deep-link entry paths and malicious parameter injection would directly target this class of defects.</INFERENCE> (Confidence: Medium)

### Research Question 3: What are the contrasting viewpoints or competing evidence?

#### Deterministic Versus LLM-Based Oracles

One contrast in the literature and practice arises between deterministic or rule-based oracles and LLM-based or AI-powered oracles. ## Conclusion

The literature and practice reviewed here indicate that even an advanced, requirement-driven UI test campaign like yours leaves several important defect classes under-tested, particularly those arising from multi-step end-to-end journeys, temporal event ordering, cross-version state transitions, asynchronous concurrency, localization and accessibility nuances, and instrumentation correctness.[1][2][5][7][11][14][17][20] (Confidence: High) The strongest empirical evidence supports adding scenario-guided end-to-end testing, race-detection sweeps, upgrade-path checks, offline–reconnect state-machine validation, and pseudo-localization-based visual regression, each targeting defect types that static combinatorial sampling and per-surface assertions miss.[2][5][11][14][17][20] (Confidence: High) LLM-based oracles and planners offer promising capabilities for interpreting textual artefacts and generating complex test sequences, but their performance is not yet quantified robustly enough to justify fully mechanical gating; they are best introduced as advisory augmentation to deterministic methods.[2][10] (Confidence: Medium)

Given the knowledge gaps, your immediate decisions should be guided by a combination of available empirical evidence and domain inference. Mechanically gated sweeps are most justified where defect classes are frequent and high-impact, and detection methods have demonstrated strong performance with manageable triage, as in race detection, upgrade-path testing, and offline–reconnect checks.[11][14][20] (Confidence: High) Advisory sweeps are appropriate where evidence is emerging or vendor-driven, such as AI visual testing, and where testing methods yield fewer but still valuable defects, such as soak testing, screen-reader-driven accessibility, temporal edge cases, and telemetry correctness.<INFERENCE from="[8][17][18][20]">By progressively integrating and measuring these methods in your portfolio, you can convert current qualitative rankings into quantitative ROI assessments and refine your gating logic accordingly.</INFERENCE> (Confidence: Medium)
