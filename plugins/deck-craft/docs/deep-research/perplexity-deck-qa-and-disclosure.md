# Designing Machine-Gated Investor Decks: Evidence on Slide Legibility, Regulated Disclosure Norms, Automated Quality Assurance, and Chart Integrity

The available evidence from empirical studies, regulatory guidance, and visualization research between 2019 and the present converges on a set of defensible, machine-enforceable rules for investor and board decks, while also clarifying where human judgment remains irreducible. Visual design research shows that comprehension and retention improve when slides use large fonts calibrated to viewing distance, high-contrast text, controlled cognitive load, and dual coding of words with relevant visuals, with quantitative thresholds such as a minimum apparent text size of roughly 15–20 arc minutes and practical projector guidelines of titles around 36–44 points and body text 18–24 points for typical rooms.[3][4][9][14][19] Regulatory sources from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission’s Regulation G and Item 10(e) of Regulation S‑K, along with updated Compliance & Disclosure Interpretations, provide detailed, enforceable requirements on non‑GAAP measures, reconciliation, labelling, and the relative prominence of GAAP versus non‑GAAP figures, which can be operationalized into automated checks for font weights, ordering, and presence of reconciliations.[11][12][20] Data visualization research documents specific distortion mechanisms—most prominently truncated y‑axes, dual axes, and non‑meaningful area encodings—that measurably bias perception, and recent work shows how axis metadata can be used to automatically flag truncated or inverted axes and dual scales in rendered charts.[5][6][16][17][18] At the same time, both visualization and regulatory domains emphasize that many aspects of meaning, such as whether a customized performance metric is genuinely informative or misleading, still require human interpretation even if structural red flags can be detected mechanically.[8][11][12][17] Finally, the literature on cognitive load, dual coding, and typography, taken together with accessibility standards like WCAG, supports an evidence-based “preflight gate” that can transfer several defect classes—illegible text at distance, ## Presentation Design for Comprehension and Retention at Viewing Distance

### Cognitive Load, Dual Coding, and Slide-Based Learning

Modern theories of learning provide the conceptual backbone for evidence-based slide design rules. Cognitive Load Theory, building on the limitation of working memory, holds that instructional materials should minimize extraneous load to free capacity for schema formation.[4] This theory frames visual design not as decoration but as a determinant of how much mental “bandwidth” is consumed by decoding the slide versus understanding the content.[4] When visual design is poorly executed—cluttered layouts, non-standard icons, low contrast, dense text—learners allocate cognitive resources to deciphering the slide itself, impairing comprehension and retention. Conversely, well-structured visuals can reduce cognitive load, making it easier for viewers to construct robust mental models of the information being presented.[4]

Dual Coding Theory, advanced by Paivio and extended by Mayer’s Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, adds that memory traces are stronger when information is encoded both verbally and visually.[19] Empirical syntheses show that pairing clear verbal explanation with relevant diagrams or sketches yields significantly better comprehension and retention than text-only or image-only formats, because learners have both a verbal and a visual retrieval route.[19] Clark and Lyons report that well-designed instructional graphics can reduce cognitive load by 30–50% relative to text-only materials, which is a substantial effect size for practical teaching and presentation contexts.[19] The dual coding literature emphasizes that visuals must be directly relevant and conceptually aligned with the verbal content; decorative or tangential images can backfire by increasing extraneous cognitive load and distracting attention from core messages.[19]

In the specific context of slide presentations, the interaction between visual aids and verbal delivery has been studied in classroom settings. Savoy, Proctor, and Salvendy analyzed information retention when lectures were delivered with traditional methods versus PowerPoint-supported methods, finding that students retained approximately 15% less information that was delivered verbally during PowerPoint presentations, even though they preferred PowerPoint to traditional lectures.[1] This counterintuitive result suggests that slides can inadvertently compete with the spoken explanation, especially when they are text-heavy or visually complex. Dual coding theory would predict that redundancy or split attention—where learners are forced to toggle between reading dense text and listening—undermines retention.[19] The implication for investor and board decks is that slides should be designed to provide succinct visual anchors rather than full textual transcripts, allowing verbal explanation to carry nuance.

Recent practitioner-oriented guidance, grounded in cognitive load principles, advocates a “three-second rule”: each slide should be understandable in less than three seconds so that viewers can reallocate attention to the speaker.[4] This rule is not a formal experimental result but expresses, in operational terms, the need to minimize the time spent parsing visual structure. If content cannot be grasped within a few seconds, the visual design is likely imposing excess cognitive load. For machine gating, this principle can be approximated by thresholds on the amount of text per slide, the number of distinct visual elements, and the complexity of layouts, even though the precise three-second metric itself is not directly measurable in static preflight analysis.[4]

The training literature further cautions against overuse of typographic emphasis. Bold and italics are effective for signaling emphasis when used sparingly, but their overuse quickly dilutes their function, making it harder for viewers to discern which elements truly matter.[4] For cognitive load, excessive emphasis styles create a noisy visual field that demands more effort for visual parsing. This insight supports the idea of an “accent budget” for slides: only a small fraction of text should be bold or italic, and accent usage can be enforced through automated checks that flag slides where, for example, more than a certain percentage of characters are bold. While precise numeric thresholds are not standardized in the literature, the qualitative guidance aligns with a shift toward machine-enforceable limits on typographic emphasis.

### Typography, Text Size, and Legibility at Distance

For investor and board decks, a central design constraint is legibility at distance. Empirical and engineering guidance converge on quantitative rules relating text size to viewing distance. Extron’s videowall legibility guidelines synthesize vision science and ergonomics to recommend that text occupy at least 15–20 arc minutes of visual angle for comfortable reading, with 10 arc minutes as an absolute minimum.[3] They provide a trigonometric formula to compute the visual angle in arc minutes given the physical text height and viewing distance: 

\[
\text{Arc Minutes} = 60 \times \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Text Height}}{\text{Viewing Distance}}\right)
\][3]

Using this formula, Extron demonstrates that doubling text height from 1 inch to 2 inches at a fixed distance substantially increases the visual angle, improving legibility and reducing eyestrain.[3] From this analysis, they derive a practical rule of thumb: provide a minimum text height of 1 inch on screen for every 15 feet of maximum viewing distance.[3] In a room where the furthest viewer sits 30 feet from the display, text should therefore be at least 2 inches tall on the screen, which roughly corresponds to 74 pixels in height on typical displays.[3] They note that, as a general rule, the pixel height of a font is about 30–35% larger than its point size, so a 74-pixel tall font equates to approximately 58-point type in authoring tools like PowerPoint.[3] This gives an actionable numeric floor: at 30 feet, text that viewers must read should be at least around 58 points.

Practitioner guidance for standard conference and classroom settings refines these raw legibility numbers into recommended font size ranges. A widely cited presentation design guide suggests that titles work best at 36–44 points, subtitles at 28–32 points, body text at 18–24 points, and captions at 12–16 points for typical rooms using projectors or large monitors.[9] The same guidance recommends never going below 18 points for any text that the audience is expected to read from the screen; text smaller than that belongs in handouts or notes rather than on slides.[9] Moreover, it proposes a simple ratio: titles roughly twice the size of body text, and body text around 1.5 times the size of captions.[9] While these numbers are not derived from controlled experiments, they are consistent with the Extron arc-minute analysis for moderate room sizes, especially when the furthest viewer is no more than eight times the screen height away, a common heuristic for room layout.[9] Taken together, the arc-minute formula and these practical ranges support machine-enforceable minimum font sizes keyed to declared room geometry or maximum viewing distance.

Accessibility standards further constrain legibility requirements through contrast and spacing metrics. WebAIM’s contrast checker, based on the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.0 and 2.1, states that normal text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background, while large text—defined as 14-point bold or greater, or at least 18-point regular—must have at least 3:1.[14] For AAA-level accessibility, normal text must reach a contrast ratio of 7:1 and large text at least 4.5:1.[14] These ratios can be computed programmatically from the RGB values of text and background, making them ideal candidates for automation. In an investor deck preflight gate, any text below these ratios could be flagged, with stricter thresholds for materials expected to be scrutinized by diverse audiences or regulators.

WCAG 2.2’s Success Criterion 1.4.12 adds numeric guidance on text spacing to ensure readability when users adjust spacing. It requires that content remain readable and operable when line height is set to at least 1.5 times the font size, spacing after paragraphs to at least 2 times the font size, letter spacing to at least 0.12 times the font size, and word spacing to at least 0.16 times the font size.[10] These metrics were informed by the McLeish study on reading speed, which found increased reading speed up to about 0.20 em for letter spacing before the benefit flattened, with earlier studies failing to detect improvements because they started at much higher spacing values around 0.5 em.[10] For slide design, the implication is that excessively tight line and letter spacing can impair readability, and designs should be robust when spacing is increased to the specified thresholds. Automated gates can check that line height in CSS or equivalent settings meets at least 1.2–1.3 times the font size by default and does not break layout at 1.5 times, though slides themselves often encode spacing at the template level rather than the content level.[10]

Typography research also points to the impact of letter shape variance on word recognition. The Training Industry article cites cognitive researchers who find that the visual variance between tall letters (such as d, h, k, l), descending letters (g, y, p, q), and short letters (a, e, n, s) helps readers recognize words more easily.[4] Using all capitalized letters removes this variance and slows word recognition, increasing cognitive load.[4] As a result, the article recommends avoiding all caps except for limited, short labels, again providing a qualitative rule that can be translated into machine checks that flag lines where nearly all alphabetic characters are uppercase. All-caps headlines spanning many words are especially problematic for at-distance reading in investor decks, where viewers must quickly parse information during meetings.[4]

Finally, guidance for virtual presentations acknowledges that viewers often consume slides on smaller screens, including laptops and mobile devices, which necessitates larger relative font sizes. The same presentation guide recommends pushing body text to at least 24 points and maintaining titles around 40 points for virtual decks, emphasizing the need to avoid long text blocks altogether.[9] These numbers are pragmatic, acknowledging reduced screen real estate and scaling issues in screen-sharing, and they can be adopted as higher minimums for slides intended primarily for remote investors and board members.[9]

### Visual Structure, Iconography, and Cognitive Load Management

Beyond text metrics, slide design must manage visual complexity to maintain comprehension and retention. Visual design can either elevate content by clarifying structure or detract from it by consuming cognitive resources.[4] Cognitive Load Theory suggests that visual design choices should aim to reduce extraneous load, focusing viewer attention on germane processing of the core message.[4] Poorly chosen design elements—such as arbitrary icons, ornamental graphics, and complex backgrounds—add extraneous load without contributing to comprehension.

The Training Industry guidance underscores that icons should have learned meaning to reduce cognitive load.[4] When viewers already associate an icon with a concept, the icon can efficiently convey information and serve as a mnemonic. However, icons without learned meaning force viewers to spend time inferring what the icon might represent, which consumes bandwidth that should be used to understand the underlying message.[4] This aligns with dual coding theory’s insistence on semantically aligned visuals: images that do not directly answer a question posed by the text, or that do not illustrate a concept under discussion, are considered extraneous and potentially harmful.[19] A practical design test proposed in dual coding guidance is that if removing an image does not cause the explanation to lose essential information, the image is likely decorative and should be removed.[19] For a machine gate, semantic alignment is harder to judge, but structural signals—such as unlabeled photographs adjacent to dense text, or a proliferation of icons without accompanying labels—can be treated as red flags that warrant human review.

Dual coding practice emphasizes spatial contiguity: placing text near the corresponding part of a diagram and using arrows or color-coding to link labels to visual features.[19] Mayer’s contiguity principle indicates that when explanatory text is physically close to the diagram it describes, learners process the information more efficiently, reducing the need to hold separate representations in working memory.[19] In slides, this means that captions, callouts, and labels should be integrated with graphs and diagrams rather than relegated to distant footnotes. Automated gates can examine layout geometry to ensure that text boxes associated with charts are within a certain proximity, although this may require heuristic thresholds given the variability of slide templates.

Modern instructional design also advocates for a limited “accent budget” in visual design. Because bold, italics, and color accents convey emphasis, their overuse overwhelms visual hierarchy and makes it difficult for viewers to discern which elements are truly important.[4][9] The Training Industry guidance explicitly warns that overusing bold and italics will deemphasize the things one is trying to emphasize.[4] Similarly, presentation guides recommend pairing a single display font for titles with one clean sans-serif body font, and using bold sparingly.[9] Automated gates can quantify the proportion of text styled with bold or italics and flag slides that exceed a certain threshold, encouraging authors to consolidate emphasis.

Finally, textual density and layout play a crucial role in cognitive load. While quantitative thresholds such as the “6x6 rule”—no more than six lines per slide and six words per line—are popular in practice, they are heuristic rather than empirically validated rules.[9] Nonetheless, they capture the insight that dense, paragraph-style text belongs in handouts, not projected slides. Savoy et al.’s finding that students retained significantly less verbally delivered information when slides were used suggests that dense text may shift attention toward reading and away from listening.[1] For investor decks, where the spoken narrative is essential, these heuristics can be operationalized by measuring characters per slide and line counts, with gates flagging slides whose textual density exceeds defined thresholds.

### Empirical Outcomes and Failure Modes in Slide-Based Communication

The empirical literature and practical guidance together suggest several design techniques that measurably improve comprehension and retention, and they also surface recurring failure modes. The primary empirically documented failure mode is the interference between slide content and verbal explanation. Savoy et al. showed that while students preferred PowerPoint-supported lectures, they retained 15% less of the information delivered verbally when slides were present, indicating that the visual channel can interfere with the verbal channel when slides are not optimally designed.[1] This interference likely stems from slides that demand significant reading and decoding, leading students to miss verbal nuances. For investor decks, this implies that slides should be intentionally lean, providing visual anchors—graphs, diagrams, short phrases—rather than full prose explanations.

Another failure mode is illegible text at viewing distance. Extron warns that text occupying only 10 arc minutes of visual angle is barely legible and may cause eyestrain over extended periods.[3] They recommend at least 15–20 arc minutes to ensure comfortable reading.[3] Slides with small fonts relative to room size thereby risk not only comprehension but also physical discomfort, especially in boardrooms and auditoriums. Presentation guides emphasize that text below 18 points for on-screen reading is unsuitable for live presentations.[9] Automated gates that enforce minimum font sizes and compute apparent text size given declared room geometry can eliminate this failure mode in machine-gated decks.

Accessibility-based failure modes include insufficient contrast and cramped spacing. WebAIM’s contrast thresholds exist precisely because low-contrast text becomes difficult to read, particularly for viewers with visual impairments.[14] A slide with light grey text on a white background may look aesthetically pleasing but fails legibility standards. The WCAG text spacing criterion addresses layouts that break when users increase spacing for readability, revealing designs that are brittle and not inclusive.[10] Investor decks that ignore these constraints can unintentionally exclude segments of their audience or attract regulatory scrutiny if used in regulated disclosures.

Finally, typographic choices such as all caps and excessive emphasis create subtle but real comprehension costs. All-caps headings reduce the visual distinctiveness of words and slow recognition.[4] Overuse of bold and italics dilutes emphasis and makes slide hierarchies ambiguous.[4][9] These design mistakes rarely trigger explicit criticism but collectively erode the effectiveness of presentations. Because they are structurally simple—count upper-case characters, measure bold coverage—these defects are excellent candidates for machine detection and remediation recommendations.

## Regulatory and Investor-Disclosure Conventions for Financial Content

### SEC Regulation G and Item 10(e): Non-GAAP Measure Governance

In investor and board decks that present financial information, regulatory norms surrounding non-GAAP measures are central. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, through Regulation G and Item 10 of Regulation S‑K, defines non-GAAP financial measures as numerical measures of historical or future financial performance, position, or cash flows that exclude or include amounts that are not so excluded or included in the most directly comparable GAAP measure.[11][20] Importantly, this definition extends beyond formal financial statements to disclosures in earnings releases, investor presentations, and proxy statements when they include performance metrics not required by GAAP.[11][20] Thus, many customized metrics common in investor decks—such as adjusted EBITDA, annual recurring revenue modified for billings, or “funds from operations” outside the specific NAREIT definition—qualify as non-GAAP measures and must comply with SEC rules when used by U.S. registrants.[11][20]

Regulation G and Item 10(e) require that any non-GAAP financial measure be accompanied by a presentation of the most directly comparable GAAP measure with equal or greater prominence, along with a reconciliation of the differences between the two measures.[11][20] The reconciliation must be quantitative, specifying the adjustments made to arrive at the non-GAAP measure from the GAAP measure, unless a narrow exception applies for forward-looking measures where such reconciliation is impracticable; in those cases, the registrant must disclose the unavailable information and its probable significance.[11][12] This requirement directly supports machine-enforceable checks: any slide that presents a non-GAAP metric should also contain, in the same or a nearby slide, the GAAP metric and a reconciliation table or narrative, and the visual styling should not emphasize the non-GAAP figure over the GAAP figure.

Updated Compliance & Disclosure Interpretations (CDIs) issued in December 2022 clarify the SEC staff’s expectations and expand examples of practices considered misleading.[11][12] One CDI elaborates that the exclusion of “normal, recurring, cash operating expenses” from performance measures may render the non-GAAP measure misleading, depending on the nature and effect of the adjustment and how it relates to the company’s operations and revenue-generating activities.[11][12] Another CDI, Question 100.04, introduces a general principle that non-GAAP measures can be misleading if adjustments alter the recognition and measurement principles required under GAAP, such as accelerating revenue recognition as though earned when billed instead of over time, presenting revenue on a gross basis where GAAP requires net, or adopting cash-basis accounting for a performance measure where GAAP uses accrual.[12] While such substantive accounting judgments are not directly machine-checkable, the presence of individually tailored adjustments can be structurally flagged, especially when narrative cues indicate departures from GAAP recognition.

Question 100.05 reiterates that non-GAAP measures can be misleading when they are not appropriately labeled and clearly described.[12] The CDI provides examples of failures, such as using labels like “pro forma” for measures not calculated in accordance with Regulation S‑X Article 11 or failing to identify a measure as non-GAAP.[11][12] Question 100.06 further notes that a non-GAAP measure can be misleading even where detailed disclosure is provided, emphasizing that misalignment between label and substance is itself a problem.[12] For a preflight gate, labelling defects—where a metric is styled and named in a way that suggests GAAP while being non-GAAP—can be detected by cross-referencing metric names against known GAAP line items and flagging inconsistencies.

CDI Question 102.10, expanded into subparts, addresses the “equal or greater prominence” requirement in detail.[11][12] The staff considers non-GAAP measures more prominent than GAAP when they are presented before the comparable GAAP measure, when the GAAP measure is omitted altogether, when non-GAAP measures are highlighted in headlines or captions without equivalent GAAP characterization, or when charts, tables, or graphs are used for non-GAAP measures without corresponding visuals for GAAP measures.[11][12] The CDI also notes that presenting a non-GAAP income statement—an income statement comprising non-GAAP measures and replicating GAAP line items and subtotals—is an example of undue prominence.[12] It warns that presenting a non-GAAP measure using styling such as bold or larger fonts compared to the GAAP measure, describing non-GAAP results as “record performance” or “exceptional” without equal descriptive characterization of GAAP measures, and providing extensive discussion of non-GAAP figures without similar analysis of GAAP figures are all indicators of non-GAAP measures being given greater prominence.[11][12]

These rules are particularly amenable to automation. A preflight gate can analyze typography (font size, weight, color) to ensure that GAAP measures are not visually demoted relative to non-GAAP measures.[11][12] It can check ordering of metrics on slides and within narratives, flagging cases where non-GAAP measures appear without GAAP counterparts. It can inspect for the presence of charts showing non-GAAP measures and verify that GAAP measures receive similar visual treatment. While nuanced narrative descriptions may still require human judgment, the structural features cited in the CDIs are precisely those that machines can detect with high reliability.

### Comment Letter Trends and MD&A Expectations

Beyond the formal rules, patterns in SEC comment letters highlight enforcement priorities. Analyses of SEC comment letter trends show that, year after year, the two most commented areas are Management’s Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) and non-GAAP financial measures.[15] Comment letter volume has declined since the 2019–2021 period, but the letters have become more targeted, focusing on specific issues rather than broad deficiencies.[15] This suggests a regulatory environment where companies may receive fewer comment letters overall but are still likely to be scrutinized when they push the boundaries on MD&A clarity or non-GAAP usage.[15]

For MD&A, a recurring comment theme is whether material period-over-period changes in significant line items are tied to specific, quantified drivers.[15] Regulators ask whether each driver identified is accompanied by a statement of its dollar or basis point contribution to the change.[15] While this is more relevant for formal filings than investor decks, the expectation of quantified explanations echoes the need for clarity in performance narratives presented to investors and boards. Machine gates can assist by checking whether MD&A-style narratives in decks include quantification when discussing significant changes, although the interpretation of “significance” and “drivers” remains largely human.

The persistence of non-GAAP measures as a top comment letter topic reinforces the need for robust gating around such metrics in investor decks used by registrants. Because Regulation G applies broadly to any public disclosure containing non-GAAP measures, the same principles must extend to slide presentations used in investor days, earnings calls, and board meetings when they are filed or furnished with the Commission.[11][12] The equal prominence, reconciliation, and labelling rules, as clarified in the CDIs, provide a concrete basis for machine enforcement in these contexts.

### Chart Axis Rules, Perception Bias, and Figure Provenance

Investor decks frequently use charts to communicate trends, and regulatory bodies are increasingly aware of the potential for misleading visualizations. While SEC rules do not yet codify granular visualization standards such as y-axis truncation or dual axes in the way they regulate non-GAAP measures, scholarship in the life sciences and visualization communities provides guidance that can be adopted as best practice and used in internal gating.

The article “Choice of y-axis can mislead readers” by Schober et al. uses examples from non-scientific literature to show how unit of measure and y-axis scaling can create biased perceptions of data, a phenomenon they term “perception bias.”[17] In one example, a newspaper graph plots the proportion of diesel-engine cars over time with a y-axis starting at 40%, creating a regression line that visually suggests diesel engines will soon disappear.[17] When the same data are plotted with a y-axis starting at 0%, the downward trend remains clear but appears much less steep, conveying a more realistic impression of persistence.[17] Schober et al. recommend that axis scaling cover the full range of data and be biologically or clinically meaningful, and they propose that for ratio variables, the default should be to start the y-axis at 0 unless a justified exception exists.[17] They emphasize that authors should pre-specify units of measure and axis scaling rules in the study protocol, or at least specify how these will be determined, to guard against bias.[17]

Related work by Yang et al. and subsequent analyses quantify the y-axis truncation effect: bar graphs with lower truncated scales—where the y-axis starting point is above zero—lead observers to perceive larger differences between the values depicted.[5] They find that graphs with truncation cause observers to overestimate differences compared to graphs without truncation, whereas graphs with expanded scales at either end can cause observers to perceive smaller differences.[5] The effect exists even when the truncated range is not extreme, although the effect size varies.[5] Crowd-sourced experiments summarized in “Truncating the Y-Axis: Threat or Menace?” further explore how truncation influences perception, showing that while viewers can sometimes adjust their interpretations when axis labels are clearly visible, truncated baselines still systematically bias judgments, especially in bar charts where area and height act as anchors.[6]

The Misviz dataset introduced in recent visualization research operationalizes these concerns by defining truncated axis as any visualization where one or more sorted vertical axes start with a numerical value strictly higher than 0.[16] It also defines inverted axes—where the axis order is reversed from the default—and dual axes, where a chart has two independent and parallel numerical axes with different scales.[16] Misviz relies on axis metadata rather than underlying data tables to detect misleaders, allowing automated systems to identify truncated axes, inverted axes, dual axes, inconsistent tick intervals, inconsistent binning sizes, and inappropriate item order in temporal axes.[16] For investor decks, a gate that extracts axis metadata from charts can therefore automatically flag truncated baselines, reversed y-axis orientation, or dual axes that might confuse or mislead viewers.

Best practice guidance on data visualization from the biomedical literature further stresses the importance of figure provenance and captions. A set of ten principles for effective data visualization emphasizes that authors should determine their main message before designing a visual and use an effective geometry with a high data-ink ratio, minimizing non-data elements such as heavy gridlines, decorative backgrounds, and redundant labels.[8] The guidance encourages authors to “show the data” by including raw datapoints alongside summary geometries where possible, and to represent uncertainty explicitly through confidence intervals or variability markers.[8] Captions should be standalone, fully explaining everything in the figure so that the major point can be understood even if the figure and caption are read in isolation from the rest of the study.[8] This concept of standalone captions and explicit uncertainty aligns with investor deck design: charts showing performance metrics or projections should disclose what they depict, how they were constructed, and what uncertainty or variance they carry.

While these visualization principles are not codified in SEC or ASIC regulations in the same way as non-GAAP rules, they can be adopted as internal standards and enforced by a preflight gate. Automated checks can compute data-ink ratios, detect non-data elements occupying space, and verify that captions mention key contextual elements such as units, time ranges, and whether data are actuals or forecasts. Moreover, they can detect charts omitting uncertainty indicators in contexts where variability is expected, prompting human review rather than automatic rejection.

## Automated Document and Presentation Quality Assurance

### Mechanically Detectable Defect Classes in Rendered Documents

The question of which defect classes can be mechanically detected in rendered decks is central to designing a preflight gate that reliably upgrades investor communications. Many of the constraints discussed above—typography, contrast, spacing, axis integrity, and non-GAAP prominence—are structurally encoded in the document and can be extracted in a headless browser environment.

Font size and text height relative to viewing distance are mechanically measurable. Given a slide’s resolution and layout in a headless browser, the gate can identify all text elements, compute their pixel heights, and, using the Extron guideline that pixel height is approximately 30–35% larger than point size, infer effective font sizes.[3] With declared or assumed maximum viewing distances, the gate can then apply the arc-minute formula \(\text{Arc Minutes} = 60 \times \arctan(\text{Text Height} / \text{Viewing Distance})\) to ensure text occupies at least 15–20 arc minutes.[3] It can flag any text below 10 arc minutes as effectively illegible and any text below 18 points for body content as violating practical guidelines.[9] Titles and subtitles can be checked against recommended ranges such as 36–44 and 28–32 points, respectively, with adjustments for large auditoriums where even larger fonts may be necessary.[9]

Contrast ratios are directly computable from RGB values of text and background. When a headless browser renders the slide, the gate can sample colors for each text element and background region, calculate luminance differences, and compute contrast ratios. It can then enforce WCAG’s minimum thresholds of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, and optionally the AAA thresholds of 7:1 and 4.5:1 for critical content.[14] Text failing these thresholds can be flagged, with suggestions for color adjustments.

Text spacing and layout robustness can also be assessed. The gate can read CSS or slide template properties controlling line height, paragraph spacing, and letter spacing, comparing them to WCAG’s text spacing requirements of line height at least 1.5 times the font size, paragraph spacing at least 2 times, letter spacing 0.12 times, and word spacing 0.16 times.[10] It can verify that content does not overlap or become illegible when spacing is increased to these values, though this may require dynamic layout simulation rather than static inspection.[10] Slides whose design breaks under these conditions are likely brittle and non-inclusive.

Cognitive load-related metrics such as text density and icon usage can be approximated. The gate can count words per slide, lines of text, and the number of distinct visual elements, identifying slides where textual density exceeds heuristic thresholds like a maximum of roughly six lines and short phrases per line, indicative of excessive reading load.[9] It can similarly count icons and check whether they are accompanied by labels, flagging unlabeled icons whose meaning is not evident. While it cannot judge semantic alignment between icons and text, structural anomalies such as many icons with no explanatory text are warning signs.

Typographic emphasis can be quantified by measuring the proportion of text styled as bold, italic, underlined, or in all caps. The gate can flag slides where more than a certain percentage of text is bold or where entire paragraphs are in all caps, aligning with cognitive evidence that overuse of emphasis dilutes its effectiveness and that all caps hinder efficient word recognition.[4][9] It can also infer whether fonts are serif or sans-serif, recommending clean sans-serif fonts like Arial or Helvetica for on-screen body text.[9]

Non-GAAP structural checks rely on textual analysis integrated with formatting. The gate can scan for known non-GAAP metric names, such as “Adjusted EBITDA” or “Funds From Operations,” and identify slides where such measures appear.[11][20] It can then search for the presence of reconciliations—tables or text describing adjustments from GAAP measures—and for nearby mentions of the GAAP counterpart, such as “Net income” or “Revenue.” If non-GAAP measures appear without GAAP measures or reconciliations, the gate can flag them as violations of Regulation G and Item 10(e).[11][12] It can also compare font sizes, weights, and colors between GAAP and non-GAAP metrics to ensure GAAP figures are not visually less prominent, following CDI examples where non-GAAP metrics in bold or larger font are considered problematic.[11][12]

Chart integrity checks are particularly suited to mechanical detection. Using the Misviz approach, the gate can parse axis metadata from chart images or embedded objects, identifying truncated axes where sorted vertical axes start above zero, inverted axes where axis order is reversed, and dual axes where two vertical scales with different tick labels appear.[16] It can detect inconsistent tick intervals that might mislead viewers about uniformity of changes, and inappropriate item order in temporal axes where dates are shuffled.[16] Y-axis truncation, as defined in anchor-and-ratio research, can be explicitly flagged, with warnings that truncated baselines exaggerate perceived differences between bars.[5][6][17] It can also detect charts using line geometries for categorical data, a misuse that Misviz tags as inappropriate use of line charts.[16]

Finally, data visualization principles such as data-ink ratio and caption completeness can be approximated. The gate can compute the proportion of pixels representing data elements (bars, points, lines) versus non-data elements (gridlines, backgrounds, decorative icons), estimating whether data-ink ratios are reasonably high.[8] It can check whether captions mention key components such as units, sample size, time period, and whether data are actual or forecast, though this requires natural language understanding rather than purely structural analysis.[8] Charts lacking any caption or label are straightforward to flag.

### Irreducibly Human-Judged Defects

Despite the breadth of mechanically detectable defects, some classes require human judgment because they involve semantic interpretation, contextual awareness, or subjective weighing of trade-offs. One such class is the substantive appropriateness of non-GAAP adjustments. While a gate can detect that a metric excludes certain expenses or accelerates revenue recognition, only human experts can assess whether these adjustments meaningfully convey operational performance or mislead investors by smoothing volatility or masking recurring costs.[11][12] CDI Question 100.01 describes the staff’s evaluation of whether the exclusion of “normal, recurring, cash operating expenses” is misleading, taking into account the nature and effect of the adjustment and how it relates to the company’s operations.[12] Operationalizing this evaluation requires domain knowledge about the business and industry, beyond the scope of static document analysis.

Forward-looking statement risk disclosures and qualitative cautionary language also resist pure mechanical judgment. While a gate can check for the presence of forward-looking disclaimers and identify text labeled as “guidance” or “outlook,” the sufficiency and specificity of risk factors cannot be reliably scored by automated methods. Similarly, the clarity of MD&A narratives in explaining drivers of performance changes, and whether each material driver is quantified, is partly structural but largely interpretive.[15] Human reviewers must read narratives with an understanding of the business model and macro environment.

Visual semantics present another irreducibly human domain. Dual coding theory insists on conceptual alignment between words and visuals, but machines cannot yet robustly judge whether a diagram truly illustrates the concept described or whether an image is decorative and misleading.[19] An image of a rocket next to a chart showing incremental revenue growth may be motivationally appealing but semantically questionable; machine analysis of shape and style cannot evaluate this rhetorical function. Human reviewers must decide whether such choices are appropriate for the specific audience and context.

Perception bias in visualization, while structurally detectable through axis scaling and geometry choice, also involves questions of intent and reasonableness. Y-axis truncation is sometimes justified to prevent overlapping lines or to focus on small variations in a parameter that is biologically or economically meaningful within a narrow range.[17] Schober et al. acknowledge that exceptions to the default axis starting at 0 can be adequate, such as when displaying human body temperature or heart rate, and suggest that authors should explain why axis scaling does not start at 0.[17] Whether an exception is appropriate thus depends on the nature of the variable and the magnitude of differences relative to normal variability, which machines cannot fully evaluate without domain-specific thresholds.

Finally, presentation tone, narrative coherence, and rhetorical emphasis are inherently human. A gate can flag overuse of bold or superlatives such as “record performance,” but it cannot assess the overall tone of a deck or whether the narrative appropriately balances optimism and realism. Regulatory guidance warns against describing non-GAAP measures as “exceptional” without equally prominent characterization of comparable GAAP measures.[11][12] Determining what constitutes “equally prominent” descriptive characterization, however, requires holistic reading of the document.

### Failure Modes in Automated Gates: Silent Passes and False Security

Automated gates introduce their own failure modes, particularly when checks silently fail or do not run. One risk is conflating an absence of detected defects with evidence of correctness. For example, if a gate attempts to compute a ratio such as margin percentage and encounters a zero denominator or missing numerator, it may skip the calculation rather than raising an error. Without explicit logging and reporting, this could be misinterpreted as a clean pass, creating “zero-over-nothing” scenarios where defects go unnoticed. Similarly, if the gate is configured to check for non-GAAP reconciliations but fails to identify a metric as non-GAAP due to an unrecognized label, it may not execute the reconciliation check at all. Unless the gate reports that certain checks were not run due to classification uncertainty, users may infer that all checks passed.

Another common failure mode is partial coverage. A gate might robustly analyze text and charts created within a specific slide authoring application but fail to parse embedded images or externally linked content. For instance, a chart pasted as a static image from another tool may not expose axis metadata, preventing detection of truncated axes or dual scales.[16] If the gate does not surface this limitation, decks containing such images may be treated as fully vetted when in fact key visuals have escaped scrutiny.

There is also the risk of brittle rule implementation. If font size thresholds are hard-coded based on generic guidelines without accommodating variability in room geometry or projection size, the gate may incorrectly flag compliant decks or miss non-compliant ones. The Extron guideline of 1 inch text per 15 feet of viewing distance provides a more robust rule anchored in visual angle, but implementing this requires capturing viewing distance and screen size parameters.[3] If those inputs are missing or incorrect, the gate’s judgments about legibility become unreliable.

To mitigate these failure modes, a preflight gate should explicitly distinguish between three states: passed checks (where specific rules were executed and no defects found), failed checks (where rules detected defects), and unexecuted checks (where rules could not run due to missing inputs or unsupported content types). A machine-readable and human-friendly report should enumerate which checks were executed and their outcomes, and which were skipped, so that a “clean pass” is not conflated with “no checks run” or “some checks run.” Adopting conventions such as per-check status codes and aggregated coverage metrics can help maintain transparency.

Automated gates also risk over-reliance, where human reviewers assume that any deck passing the gate is free of substantive issues. To counter this, gates should emphasize that they cover structural and formal defects, not economic substance or narrative framing. For example, the gate can assert that non-GAAP measures appear with reconciliations and equal prominence relative to GAAP measures, but it cannot judge whether the adjustments themselves are meaningful or whether the projections are realistic.[11][12] Clear documentation of the gate’s scope and limitations is essential.

## Chart Integrity and Misleading Visualization Detection

### Truncated Baselines and Perceptual Distortion

Empirical research on chart perception provides detailed evidence on how visualization choices distort judgment. The y-axis truncation effect is one of the best-documented phenomena. Yang et al. and follow-up studies show that observers perceive larger differences between values in bar graphs when the y-axis starting point is above zero than when it starts at the data origin.[5] This occurs because truncated axes compress the visual representation of lower values, exaggerating relative differences in bar heights. Anchors—visible aspects of the graph such as bar height, area, and axis ticks—guide viewers’ ratings of differences, and truncation modifies these anchors away from a neutral representation.[5]

Yang et al. confirm the existence of the truncation effect even when the magnitude of truncation is moderate, though the effect size is smaller than in more extreme cases.[5] They also find that different distortion types—lower truncation, upper truncation, and both ends—affect perceived differences in different ways. Truncation tends to amplify perceived differences between values, while expansion at either end of the axis can compress perceived differences.[5] In addition, they observe that black bars biased observers more when truncated, because reduced area modifies the visual cue of magnitude.[5] This suggests that color intensity interacts with truncation, potentially amplifying distortion.

Crowd-sourced experiments summarized in the ACM article “Truncating the Y-Axis: Threat or Menace?” further explore how truncation impacts perception across varying conditions.[6] While some viewers can adjust their interpretations when axis labels clearly indicate the truncated range, truncated baselines nonetheless systematically bias judgments, particularly for lay audiences and in contexts where viewers make rapid assessments.[6] The article reviews debate in the visualization community, noting that truncation can be appropriate in certain contexts but is widely misused to exaggerate trends.[6]

Schober et al.’s life-sciences examples reinforce this point, illustrating how axis scaling can create perception bias even among scientists accustomed to reading graphs.[17] Their diesel engine example shows that a y-axis starting at 40% creates the impression of a steep decline approaching zero, whereas a y-axis starting at 0% conveys a milder decline.[17] They conclude that presentation of data impacts what findings convey and can cause misleading impressions, proposing pre-specification of axis scaling and unit of measure to guard against bias.[17] They recommend that axis scaling cover the full range of data, be biologically or clinically meaningful, default to a 0 baseline for ratio variables, and explicitly justify departures from this default.[17]

Automated detection of truncated baselines, as implemented in Misviz, centers on axis metadata: a chart is considered to have a truncated axis if one or more sorted vertical axes start at a numerical value strictly higher than 0.[16] This definition is straightforward to apply in machine gates when axis tick labels are accessible. The gate can compute the minimum axis value and compare it to the data origin (typically 0 for ratio variables), flagging any positive minimum as truncated. It can also inspect labels for units and variable types, distinguishing cases where non-zero baselines may be justified, such as human body temperature.

The empirical evidence thus supports a strong presumptive rule against truncated baselines in investor charts depicting ratio variables (percentages, rates, financial quantities) unless the deck explicitly explains the justification and the chart is clearly labeled as magnifying small variations. A preflight gate can enforce a default rule that any bar chart with a truncated y-axis must carry visible annotation noting the truncation and its rationale, or it must be flagged for human review. For machine-grade compliance, charts depicting revenue, profit, growth rates, and similar metrics should default to y-axis starting at 0, preventing exaggerated impressions of changes.

### Dual Axes, Inverted Scales, and Area versus Length Encoding

Dual axes and inverted scales introduce additional avenues for misinterpretation. Misviz defines dual axes as visualizations with two independent and parallel numerical axes with different scales.[16] Such charts often plot two series with distinct units (e.g., revenue and customer count) on left and right y-axes, using overlapping lines or bars. While dual axes can succinctly show relationships between variables, they easily mislead when scale choices or styling make one series appear more volatile or aligned with the other than it truly is. Viewers may infer causal relationships that are not supported by the underlying data.

Inverted axes—where one or more axes are reversed from the default order—also disrupt intuitive interpretation.[16] A descending y-axis where higher values appear lower on the chart or a reversed x-axis where time runs from right to left can confuse viewers and invert relationships. Misviz flags such cases by detecting axis order metadata.[16] Automated gates can use similar logic to flag inverted axes, requiring explicit annotations or rejecting charts where inversion lacks justification.

Area versus length encoding is another source of distortion. Lu’s work on modeling Just Noticeable Differences (JNDs) in charts examines bar charts, bubble charts, and pie charts, focusing on how distance between elements and visual intensity affect perceptual discrimination.[18] Their empirical study shows that in bar charts, the distance between bars has a main effect on JND: closer bars make small differences harder to perceive, whereas more spread-out bars improve discrimination.[18] In pie charts, intensity (e.g., color saturation) plays a primary role in JND, while in bubble charts both distance and intensity matter.[18] These findings underscore that visual encoding choices, such as spacing and color, influence the minimum difference that viewers can reliably perceive. Small differences encoded in area (bubble size or pie slice angle) are harder to detect than those encoded in length, especially when area differences are subtle.[18]

Visualization researchers have long debated the appropriateness of pie charts, with studies showing that area is a less precise cue than length for comparing magnitudes and part-to-whole relationships.[18] The cost of pie charts, explored in prior work, highlights that viewers struggle to visually differentiate small wedges and accurately estimate angles.[18] Bubble charts similarly rely on area encoding, and their JND characteristics suggest that small differences in bubble radius may not be noticeable.[18] Misviz’s inclusion of bubble charts and pie charts in its misleader categories reflects this sensitivity.[16][18]

For investor decks, these findings support preferring bar and line charts for comparisons and trends. Efficacy studies comparing information extraction from bar, line, circular, bubble, radial, and radar charts find that bar and line graphs yield lower search times and more efficient information retrieval than circular, bubble, radial, or radar graphs.[7] Legend compatibility in bar graphs improves when color coding aligns with categorical groupings, making them easier to read.[7] Thus, bar and line charts should be the default geometries for performance metrics, while pie and bubble charts should be used sparingly and only when part-to-whole relationships or distributions are central.

Automated gates can detect chart types by geometry and flag uses of pie, bubble, radial, and radar charts in contexts where precise value comparisons are needed, recommending replacements with bar or line charts. They can also assess spacing between elements and highlight bar charts with very narrow spacing or overlapping bars, which reduce discrimination according to JND findings.[18] For dual axes, gates can require explicit legend entries clarifying units and encourage alternative chart designs, such as faceted charts or normalized scales.

### Derived Ratios, Disclosed Figures, and Visual Misrepresentation

Derived ratios presented as disclosed figures combine visualization and regulatory concerns. When investor decks display ratios such as adjusted operating margin, lifetime value to customer acquisition cost, or customized efficiency metrics, they often derive these figures from underlying data with adjustments. If these derived ratios are presented visually as primary performance indicators without clear reconciliation to GAAP or standard metrics, they may mislead viewers about the underlying economics.

Regulation G and Item 10(e) treat such derived ratios as non-GAAP measures when they alter the inclusion or exclusion of amounts relative to GAAP metrics.[11][20] CDIs warn that individually tailored revenue recognition or measurement methods are particularly problematic, including acceleration of revenue recognition as though earned when billed, gross presentation where GAAP requires net, or cash-basis accounting in performance measures where GAAP is accrual-based.[12] When these adjustments are embedded in derived ratios and visualized in investor charts without explicit explanation, they risk creating a perception of strong performance that is not grounded in GAAP.

Visualization research on perception bias reinforces that the choice of unit of measure and scaling affects interpretation.[17] Absolute changes describe something different than relative changes; depending on the scientific or business question, either can be justified, but the choice should not be driven by the desirability of the outcome.[17] When in doubt, authors should present both absolute and relative changes.[17] For investor decks, derived ratios such as percentage growth or efficiency metrics should be accompanied by absolute values, allowing viewers to assess magnitude in real terms.

Automated gates can inspect ratios for denominators and numerators, verifying that they correspond to disclosed GAAP measures or appropriately labeled non-GAAP measures. They can flag ratios derived from non-GAAP measures that lack reconciliation back to GAAP, aligning with Regulation G requirements.[11][12] They can also detect charts that present only ratios without underlying scale, recommending inclusion of absolute values in captions or accompanying tables.

However, the substantive choice of which ratios to highlight and whether their adjustments produce meaningful insight remains a human responsibility. Gates can enforce transparency, but they cannot determine whether a novel metric is misleading by design.

## Synthesis: Designing a Deterministic Preflight Gate for Investor Decks

### Defect Classes Suitable for Machine Gating

The evidence above supports transferring several defect classes from human judgment to deterministic machine gating in a headless browser environment. These include typography and legibility defects, contrast and spacing violations, structural non-GAAP and reconciliation issues, chart axis misconfigurations, and certain visual design excesses.

Typography defects are among the most straightforward. Fonts below 18 points for body text, titles below recommended ranges for the declared room size, and text that subtends fewer than 15 arc minutes at the furthest viewing distance can be mechanically detected and enforced.[3][9] Gates can require authors to specify room geometry or use conservative defaults for typical boardrooms, computing text height in pixels and visual angle to ensure legibility.[3] All-caps passages beyond short labels can be flagged based on cognitive evidence that they impede word recognition.[4]

Contrast and spacing defects are similarly amenable to automation. Gates can calculate contrast ratios for all text against background colors using WebAIM’s formulas and enforce WCAG thresholds.[14] They can check line height, paragraph spacing, letter spacing, and word spacing values, ensuring that content remains readable when spacing is increased to WCAG-specified values.[10] Slides with tiny line heights or cramped letter spacing can be flagged as promoting cognitive strain.

Structural non-GAAP defects—unreconciled metrics, GAAP prominence violations, and mislabeled measures—can be enforced using text analysis and styling inspection.[11][12][20] Gates can detect non-GAAP metrics by name, search for reconciliations in tables or narratives, and verify that GAAP measures appear with equal or greater prominence (font size, weight, color, position).[11][12] They can flag non-GAAP income statements and highlight slides where metrics labeled “pro forma” are not calculated under Regulation S‑X Article 11 definitions.[11][12][20]

Chart axis defects are particularly well-suited to machine detection. Gates can parse axis metadata from embedded charts or programmatically rendered visualizations, identifying truncated y-axes, inverted axes, dual axes, inconsistent tick intervals, and inappropriate item orders for temporal data.[16] They can apply Schober et al.’s guidance to default to y-axis starting at 0 for ratio variables and require explicit justification when baselines deviate.[17] They can also detect misuse of line charts for categorical data, as defined by Misviz, and flag them for remediation.[16]

Visual design excesses, such as overuse of bold or italics and high non-data-ink ratios, can be quantified and enforced. Gates can measure the proportion of text styled as bold or italic, flagging slides that exceed internal accent budgets inspired by cognitive load guidance.[4][9] They can compute data-ink ratios by comparing the area occupied by data elements to total visual area, encouraging removal of decorative backgrounds and non-informative graphics.[8]

### Defect Classes Requiring Human Judgment

Despite extensive automation potential, certain defect classes must remain under human review. Substantive accounting judgments about non-GAAP adjustments, risk factor sufficiency for forward-looking statements, narrative coherence in MD&A-like sections, and semantic alignment between visuals and concepts are all domains where machines can only provide partial support.

Machines cannot judge whether excluding a particular expense from adjusted EBITDA is misleading or a reasonable reflection of one-time items; regulators consider such questions in light of the company’s operations, industry norms, and the nature of recurring expenses.[11][12] Similarly, machines cannot evaluate whether a forward-looking statement’s risk disclosures are adequate or whether the narrative fairly conveys uncertainty and downside risks. They can check for the presence of disclaimers but not their completeness.

Narrative coherence and rhetorical tone are also inherently human. Whether a deck appropriately balances discussion of challenges and risks against achievements, whether the story is internally consistent, and whether comparisons are fair all require substantive reading. Machines can flag superlatives applied to non-GAAP measures without similar characterization of GAAP measures, following CDI guidance, but cannot fully assess fairness.[11][12]

Visual semantics—the question of whether an image meaningfully contributes to understanding or merely decorates—remains beyond current automated capabilities. Dual coding theory suggests that images should be removed if they do not carry essential information.[19] Machines can detect unlabeled images and photos but cannot determine whether their presence is pedagogically justified.

Consequently, a well-designed preflight gate must be explicit about its scope: it enforces structural and formal compliance while flagging areas where human interpretation remains necessary. Its role is to catch mechanical defects and structural misleaders, not to replace expert judgment.

### Numeric Floors and Evidence-Based Rules for the Gate

To make the gate’s rules defensible and evidence-backed, numeric floors should be drawn from the strongest available sources rather than asserted ad hoc. For typography and legibility, the Extron guidelines and WCAG provide the backbone. Text intended for at-distance reading should subtend at least 15–20 arc minutes of visual angle for comfortable legibility, with 10 arc minutes as an absolute minimum.[3] Using the formula \(\text{Arc Minutes} = 60 \times \arctan(\text{Text Height} / \text{Viewing Distance})\), the gate can compute whether a given font size meets the threshold for a declared viewing distance.[3] For typical projector setups in medium-sized rooms, practical font size floors of 18 points for body text and 36 points for titles, as suggested by presentation guides, are consistent with these geometric constraints.[9]

Contrast floors should follow WCAG 2.1: at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, with AAA thresholds adopted for critical content where necessary.[14] Spacing thresholds should follow WCAG text spacing: line height at least 1.5× font size, paragraph spacing at least 2×, letter spacing 0.12×, and word spacing 0.16×.[10] The gate should test robustness by simulating these adjustments and checking layout integrity.

Axis rules should adopt Schober et al.’s default of starting y-axis at 0 for ratio variables, with exceptions requiring explanation.[17] Any truncated y-axis should be flagged, and charts of financial metrics such as revenue, profit, growth rates, and margins should either use full-range axes or include evident annotation explaining the chosen scale.[5][6][17] Dual axes and inverted axes should be flagged as potentially misleading and restricted to contexts where domain experts approve their usage.[16]

Accent budgets can be set based on cognitive load guidance that emphasizes sparing use of bold and italics.[4][9] While the literature does not specify numeric limits, internal standards might cap the proportion of text styled with emphasis at, for example, 20–30% of total characters, with gates flagging slides that exceed this. All-caps usage can be limited to short labels (e.g., fewer than three words per label), reflecting research that all caps slow word recognition.[4]

Non-GAAP rules can be directly imported from Regulation G and Item 10(e), as interpreted by the CDIs.[11][12][20] The gate should require that any detected non-GAAP measure be accompanied by the most directly comparable GAAP measure, presented with equal or greater prominence, and a quantitative reconciliation.[11][12] It should flag adjustments that appear individually tailored in ways described in CDI 100.04, such as revenue acceleration or gross presentation, for human review.[12] Labels such as “pro forma” should be restricted to metrics calculated in accordance with Regulation S‑X, Article 11, and the gate should flag any mismatch.[11][12][20]

### Distinguishing Failed, Passed, and Unexecuted Checks

A crucial design requirement for the preflight gate is to distinguish checks that failed, checks that passed, and checks that did not run. To achieve this, each rule should be implemented as a discrete module that returns a status code: success, failure, or not executed. For instance, the font size module might return failure when any body text is below 18 points, success when all text meets thresholds, and not executed when slide resolution or viewing distance information is unavailable. The axis integrity module might return not executed if a chart is embedded as a static image without accessible axis metadata.[16]

Gate reports should enumerate each module and its status, preventing a global “pass” label from obscuring unexecuted checks. Users should be able to see that while typography and contrast checks passed, axis integrity checks did not run on certain slides due to content type limitations. This transparency prevents silent failures where the absence of detected defects is misinterpreted as comprehensive vetting.

Additionally, the gate should log and expose exceptions such as division by zero or missing data when computing derived ratios. Rather than silently skipping calculations, it should flag them as defects or at least as unresolved checks requiring human attention. For regulated disclosures, anything that prevents a check from running on non-GAAP metrics or MD&A narratives should be treated as a gating issue, not as a benign skip.

By combining explicit per-check status reporting with robust structural detection grounded in empirical and regulatory evidence, the gate can serve as a reliable preflight mechanism for investor and board decks, elevating mechanical defects from human review to automated enforcement while clearly signalling where human judgment remains indispensable.

## Conclusion

The body of evidence from cognitive psychology, visual design, accessibility standards, data visualization research, and securities regulation provides a solid foundation for designing a deterministic preflight gate for investor and board decks. On the presentation side, cognitive load theory and dual coding research underscore that slides should minimize extraneous visual complexity and pair concise verbal explanations with relevant, semantically aligned visuals.[4][19] Typography and legibility can be governed by numeric floors derived from visual angle calculations, practical presentation guidelines, and WCAG contrast and spacing standards, ensuring that fixed-size content remains readable at distance.[3][9][10][14] These constraints are straightforward to implement in a headless browser environment and can be enforced without human intervention.

Regulatory norms around non-GAAP measures and MD&A disclosures, particularly under SEC Regulation G and Item 10(e), yield clear rules about reconciliation, labelling, and prominence that can be codified in automated checks.[11][12][20] Updated CDIs provide examples of misleading practices—such as individually tailored revenue recognition and overemphasis of non-GAAP metrics—that gates can structurally detect or at least flag for human review.[11][12] Visualization research on y-axis truncation, dual axes, and JNDs shows how chart choices distort perception, providing quantitative evidence for rules restricting truncated baselines, discouraging dual axes, and preferring bar and line charts over area-based encodings.[5][6][7][16][17][18]

Automated quality assurance can confidently handle structural defects—illegible fonts, poor contrast, cramped spacing, unreconciled non-GAAP metrics, truncated or inverted axes, misused chart types, and excessive typographic emphasis—while deferring substantive judgments about economic meaning, risk disclosure sufficiency, narrative coherence, and visual semantics to human reviewers.[4][8][11][12][17] To avoid false security, gates must explicitly distinguish between passed, failed, and unexecuted checks, ensuring that a clean report reflects actual coverage rather than silent failures.

In designing such a gate, teams should ground every numeric threshold and structural rule in cited evidence, from Extron’s arc-minute analysis and WCAG guidelines to SEC regulations and visualization research, building an “evidence file” that documents why each check exists and how it relates to observed comprehension, retention, or regulatory risk.[3][5][10][11][12][14][17][19] By doing so, they can justify the transfer of specific defect classes from human to machine gating, improve the reliability and integrity of investor and board decks, and reduce the cognitive and regulatory burden on presenters and reviewers alike.
