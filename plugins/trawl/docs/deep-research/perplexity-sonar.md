# Maximizing Idea Diversity and Quality in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Brainstorming Systems

Across the last three years, empirical work on large language models has converged on a nuanced picture of creativity and diversity: aligned LLMs are prone to homogenization and mode collapse under naive prompting, yet with carefully designed personas, structured reasoning, evolutionary search, and robust evaluation pipelines, their idea diversity can rival or surpass human groups, while maintaining practical feasibility.[1][2][6][15][17] At the same time, extensive studies of LLM-as-judge systems reveal systematic biases in novelty scoring, verbosity, and self-preference, which, if left unaddressed, cause ideation systems to reward flashy but unrealizable ideas and converge prematurely on conventional patterns.[5][13][14] Synthesizing results from creativity benchmarks, graph- and evolution-based reasoning architectures, and multi-agent collaboration research, one can derive a defensible design pattern for systems like your five-agent “adhd” Claude Code skill: use diverse, theory-grounded cognitive frames and personas to sample different regions of the semantic space; structure agent interactions as a shallow quality-diversity search rather than a single-pass filter; incorporate semantic de-duplication and cluster-then-select procedures; separate generation from evaluation with calibrated judge ensembles; and anchor convergence on a joint notion of effective diversity, novelty, feasibility, and contextual fit.[1][4][6][11][15][17]  

## Conceptual and Empirical Background: LLM Creativity and Divergent Ideation

A growing body of literature examines how LLMs perform on classic creativity constructs such as divergent thinking, originality, and associative distance, often by adapting psychological tests originally developed for humans.[2][7][15] Divergent thinking refers to the capacity to generate varied, non-obvious responses to open-ended prompts, contrasting with convergent thinking, which seeks a single correct answer.[2] In the context of LLMs, divergent ideation typically means producing many distinct candidate ideas, solutions, or perspectives for a given problem, often in brainstorming or innovation support systems. This is directly relevant to your multi-agent “adhd” skill, whose purpose is to explore a broad idea space before converging on a few refined options.

Bellemare-Pepin and colleagues benchmarked several LLMs against a large human sample of around 100,000 participants on the Divergent Association Task (DAT), where subjects must generate a list of words that are maximally semantically distant from each other.[2] Using computational measures of semantic distance derived from word embeddings, they found that GPT‑4 outperformed average human participants on DAT scores, while other models such as Gemini Pro were comparable to humans and some smaller open models performed surprisingly well.[2] This result indicates that, at least on a narrow measure of associative diversity in word lists, state-of-the-art LLMs can be highly competitive with human creativity. However, this metric focuses on semantic dispersion at the word level rather than the originality, usefulness, and depth of multi-sentence ideas, which are more relevant to your use case.

Complementary work by Zhao and colleagues adapted the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT) to evaluate LLMs across seven creativity tasks, measuring fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration.[7][15] Their findings suggest that LLMs tend to excel in elaboration and fluency, generating many detailed responses, but fall short in originality relative to human baselines.[7] These results highlight a core tension for LLM brainstorming systems: models are capable of producing many coherent, detailed suggestions, but without careful design, those suggestions cluster around typical, high-probability patterns rather than truly novel ideas.

The emerging research on human–LLM collaboration provides further context. Chang and co-authors proposed a framework for integrating an LLM tool into human brainstorming sessions, finding that such collaboration can enhance both the creativity and usefulness of ideas when the system is structured to complement human thinking rather than simply replace it.[8] Similarly, multi-agent creativity surveys describe pipelines in which multiple LLM agents with different roles or personas engage in planning, iterative refinement, and decision making, often yielding richer idea sets than single-agent prompts.[15][10] Your current design, with five parallel agents under different cognitive frames, is directionally aligned with these multi-agent strategies, but the literature suggests several evidence-based refinements in how these agents are framed, how they interact, and how their outputs are evaluated.

Taken together, these empirical strands show that LLMs have significant latent creative capacity, yet their realized creativity in practical systems is highly sensitive to prompt design, sampling strategies, and evaluation mechanisms.[1][2][6][7][15][17] The rest of this report distills the most robust techniques for maximizing idea diversity and quality in such systems, with explicit attention to failure modes you will need to defend against when evolving the “adhd” skill.

## Homogenization and Mode Collapse in LLM Idea Generation

### Evidence for Homogenization and Reduced Diversity after Alignment

Multiple studies have documented that LLMs, especially those tuned with human preference data and alignment methods like RLHF or DPO, can suffer from a reduction in output diversity, often referred to as **mode collapse**.[1][6] Mode collapse in this context means that the model disproportionately favors a narrow set of high-likelihood responses, neglecting other plausible continuations that existed in the pre-aligned base model. This phenomenon is particularly concerning for brainstorming tools whose value depends on sampling broadly from the model’s latent knowledge.

The work “How to Mitigate Mode Collapse and Unlock LLM Diversity” offers one of the clearest empirical investigations of this effect.[1] The authors show that post-training alignment leads to a substantial drop in diversity across creative writing tasks such as stories, poems, and jokes when using standard direct prompting.[1] For example, they report that a model’s diversity on creative writing prompts declines from around 20.8 percent after supervised fine-tuning to approximately 10.8 percent after DPO, indicating that the range of distinct outputs narrows markedly as alignment progresses.[1] This empirically quantifies what many practitioners observe informally: aligned models are safer and more polite, yet also more generic and repetitive in their ideation.

However, a more recent study by Liu and colleagues complicates this narrative by distinguishing between raw diversity and **effective semantic diversity**, defined as diversity among outputs that meet a minimum quality threshold.[6] Using open-ended tasks that can be evaluated automatically, such as code generation and creative writing, they find that while preference-tuned models may exhibit lower diversity under naive metrics that ignore quality, they actually achieve higher effective semantic diversity than base models when one considers only valid or high-quality outputs.[6] RL-tuned models, in particular, show substantial improvements over purely supervised models in producing multiple high-quality, semantically distinct responses.[6] This result suggests that alignment does not necessarily harm the practically useful diversity of outputs and may even improve it, provided that one measures diversity appropriately.

This yields a nuanced picture. On the one hand, aligned models clearly risk collapsing onto high-typicality patterns that satisfy human preferences, especially in direct, single-sample prompting regimes.[1] On the other hand, these same models can be more effective at generating multiple distinct, high-quality ideas when sampled appropriately and evaluated with quality-aware metrics.[6] For your “adhd” skill, this implies that the challenge is not simply to “undo” alignment, but rather to design prompts, sampling procedures, and evaluation loops that unlock the model’s latent diversity without sacrificing the higher quality that alignment provides.

### Mechanisms: Typicality Bias, Fixation, and Knowledge Aggregation

The mode collapse phenomena documented for aligned LLMs can be traced back, in part, to **typicality bias** in human preference data.[1] Typicality bias refers to the cognitive tendency of human annotators to prefer familiar, conventional text over atypical or surprising content, a bias that is well documented in cognitive psychology and now observed in LLM preference datasets.[1] When reinforcement learning or other preference optimization methods train models to maximize these biased human scores, the models learn to prioritize typical responses and underweight rare but potentially creative variations. The study by the authors of the Verbalized Sampling method formalizes typicality bias theoretically, validates it empirically on preference datasets, and shows that it plays a central role in post-alignment mode collapse.[1] This is a critical mechanism for you because any scoring procedure that uses LLM-as-judge aligned to human preferences risks reintroducing the same bias at inference time.

Beyond typicality bias, there is evidence that LLMs exhibit **fixation** similar to humans: early outputs constrain subsequent ideation and cause the system to stay near its initial suggestions.[17] A recent paper on LLM idea diversity describes two key mechanisms. At the individual level, an LLM can become fixated on early generated ideas within a session, which then shape subsequent outputs in ways that reduce diversity.[17] At the collective level, a single LLM aggregates knowledge into a unified distribution, lacking the natural partitioning of knowledge across individuals that characterizes human populations, where each person occupies a distinct region of the knowledge space.[17] This explains why naive multi-sampling from a single prompt often produces variations on a theme rather than genuinely different conceptual directions.

These mechanisms are directly relevant to your current “adhd” architecture. Spawning five parallel agents under different cognitive frames is intended to counteract knowledge aggregation and fixation by forcing the system to adopt distinct perspectives. However, if the frames are not sufficiently divergent in their instructions and if all agents share identical underlying model parameters and context, the system may still sample from overlapping high-density regions of the same distribution, yielding less diversity than expected.[17] Additionally, your current practice of banning the first three obvious answers partially addresses typicality bias by suppressing high-probability responses, but without a more structured approach, the system may still gravitate toward typical variants of the next-most-obvious ideas.

### Mitigation Techniques: Sampling, Personas, Semantic De-Duplication, and Novelty Search

Several lines of research provide actionable mitigations for homogenization and mode collapse, many of which you can incorporate into your multi-agent design.

One key contribution is **Verbalized Sampling (VS)**.[1] Rather than prompting the model to generate a single response to a query, VS asks the model to explicitly verbalize a distribution over multiple candidate responses and their probabilities. For example, instead of “Tell me a joke about coffee,” the prompt becomes “Generate five jokes about coffee and their corresponding probabilities.”[1] This simple, training-free prompting strategy encourages the model to surface a broader range of plausible outputs and to explore the tail of its internal distribution. The authors report that VS significantly improves diversity in creative writing tasks, increasing diversity by approximately 1.6 to 2.1 times over direct prompting, and, importantly, that VS is robust to post-training alignment: it maintains relatively stable diversity across alignment stages, unlike traditional prompting methods whose diversity collapses after DPO.[1] Incorporating a VS-style step into each “adhd” agent’s initial ideation phase would likely yield a richer candidate pool before your ban-on-obvious filter is applied.

Another robust set of interventions involves **persona and frame diversity**, particularly when combined with structured reasoning. Research on LLM idea diversity demonstrates that ordinary personas, such as “a middle-school teacher” or “a skeptical engineer,” can serve as diverse sampling cues that anchor generation in distinct regions of semantic space, enhancing knowledge partitioning.[17] Chain-of-thought prompting that asks the model first to produce initial ideas, then to explicitly revise them to be bolder and more distinct, reduces fixation and increases idea diversity, at least in LLMs.[17] Combining persona modifiers with chain-of-thought reasoning yields the highest idea diversity in their experiments, surpassing human group brainstorming.[17] Your current “adhd” frames should be re-examined in light of these findings to ensure they correspond to genuinely distinct, realistic personas rather than abstract cognitive labels, and they should be paired with explicit, multi-step reasoning instructions that encourage exploration and revision.

Sampling strategies also matter. Raising temperature can increase diversity, but empirical work finds that higher temperatures tend to introduce more nonsensical or low-quality outputs while yielding only modest gains in idea diversity.[17] Similarly, work on effective semantic diversity emphasizes that diversity without quality is trivial; random token sequences are maximally diverse but useless.[6] Therefore, temperature should be tuned in conjunction with quality-aware filtering rather than simply increased. In practice, for an architecture like yours, a more effective approach is to keep temperature moderate and rely on multi-agent personas, VS-style prompting, and semantic de-duplication to drive diversity, followed by scoring and pruning based on novelty and feasibility.

Semantic de-duplication is another important tool. Studies of LLM creativity metrics introduce techniques for computing semantic similarity between ideas using embeddings, then counting the number of unique, valid ideas to derive effective semantic diversity.[6][15] For instance, Liu and colleagues define effective semantic diversity as the proportion of semantically unique valid generations in a set, using an LLM judge or embedding-based similarity to cluster similar outputs and collapse near-duplicates.[6] Applying such clustering or near-duplicate detection between your five agents’ outputs would prevent the system from over-counting minor variations as distinct ideas and prompt under-represented regions of the space to be explored further.

Finally, **novelty search** and quality-diversity methods from evolutionary computation, such as MAP-Elites, provide a principled way to maintain diversity while optimizing for quality.[11][16] Santos and coauthors use a MAP-Elites grid over prompt structures to explore diverse prompt phenotypes that yield high performance on BigBench Lite tasks, demonstrating that quality-diversity search can discover both high-performing and structurally varied prompts.[11] Zammit and colleagues propose MEliTA, a variant of MAP-Elites tailored to multimodal creative tasks, which decouples modalities and promotes cross-pollination between elites, improving text-to-image mappings in a creative game-design testbed.[16] These approaches suggest that your “adhd” system should not merely sample five agents once but could evolve idea populations over several generations, maintaining elites in different behavioral niches (for example, radical novelty, conservative practicality, edge-case scenarios) and encouraging exploration.

In sum, the literature indicates that homogenization and mode collapse can be mitigated through a combination of VS-style distribution verbalization, persona and frame diversification with chain-of-thought reasoning, careful temperature tuning, semantic clustering and de-duplication, and evolutionary quality-diversity search.[1][6][11][16][17] Your existing ban on obvious answers is a step toward combating typicality bias, but the evidence suggests it should be embedded in a more structured, multi-step pipeline that explicitly optimizes for effective semantic diversity rather than raw variation.

## Structured Multi-Agent Ideation Architectures

### Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thought, and Graph-of-Thought

Reasoning-oriented prompting strategies such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Tree-of-Thought (ToT), and Graph-of-Thought (GoT) have been extensively studied in problem solving and are increasingly applied to ideation tasks.[3][12] While their origins lie in tasks with clearer notions of correctness, the architectural patterns they embody are directly transferable to structured brainstorming systems.

Chain-of-Thought prompting instructs a single model instance to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer, often improving performance on complex tasks by encouraging the model to decompose problems.[3] In ideation, CoT can be used to ask the model to articulate its reasoning for why a particular idea is novel, feasible, or well-fitted to constraints, and to revise ideas based on that reasoning. The idea diversity paper cited earlier shows that a multi-stage CoT prompt, where the model first proposes brief ideas and then explicitly revises them to be bolder and more distinct, significantly increases idea diversity in LLMs.[17] This suggests that even without a full multi-agent architecture, you can gain diversity by structuring each agent’s internal process as a mini tree of alternatives.

Tree-of-Thought extends CoT by allowing the model to branch into multiple partial solutions, then evaluate and backtrack across branches.[3] In one ToT design, the LLM generates several candidate next steps and a separate evaluator decides whether any partial solution is satisfactory or whether to continue exploring the tree.[3] Yao and colleagues propose a form of ToT where the LLM both generates and evaluates candidate steps, using voting or scoring among intermediate states to choose the most promising branch.[3] For brainstorming, a ToT-like architecture corresponds to having each agent expand an initial idea into several variants, evaluate those variants for novelty or feasibility, and then either refine promising branches or backtrack to alternate starting ideas.

Graph-of-Thought generalizes these structures by allowing arbitrary directed graphs of intermediate thoughts, including both branching and aggregation.[3][12] In GoT, each thought node can have multiple child thoughts (branching) and multiple parent thoughts (aggregation), enabling reasoning patterns akin to dynamic programming where subsolutions are combined.[3] Auto Graph-of-Thought extends this concept by encoding an entire GoT as a sequence of tokens that can be decoded, enabling hands-free and cost-effective orchestration of complex reasoning graphs.[12] For ideation, GoT architectures naturally model multi-agent brainstorming: different agents generate sub-ideas or perspectives that can be merged into composite concepts at later nodes, and feedback or critique from one agent can influence branches created by another.

Empirical comparisons indicate that while graph-based prompting can improve performance on certain tasks, chain-based schemes are often more cost-effective, especially where evaluation is expensive.[3] This trade-off is relevant for your stack: full GoT orchestration around each brainstorming query may be computationally heavy, but a shallow tree structure per agent, combined with cross-agent aggregation, could offer many of the benefits with manageable cost. A practical approach is to let each of your five agents run a limited-depth ToT process, perhaps two levels of branching, then feed their mid-level ideas into an aggregation step where a separate module (or judge agent) synthesizes cross-branch combinations in a GoT-like fashion.

### Debate, Multi-Agent Interaction, and Human-in-the-Loop Variants

Beyond structured reasoning graphs, multi-agent debate and collaboration architectures are increasingly used to augment both correctness and creativity in LLM systems.[9][10][15] Debate-style setups involve agents taking different stances or roles and critiquing each other’s outputs, with the goal of refining arguments or ideas. While much of the early literature focuses on improving factual accuracy, there is emerging work on using multi-agent systems to support collaborative brainstorming.

One study investigates whether LLM-powered multi-agent systems can augment human creativity in brainstorming tasks, finding that such systems can indeed enhance creative output when designed with appropriate interaction protocols and evaluation mechanisms.[10] Agents may play roles such as proposer, critic, refiner, and summarizer, each contributing complementary perspectives.[15] A survey of creativity in LLM-based multi-agent systems describes common three-stage pipelines: planning, process, and decision making, often with dedicated evaluator agents scoring outputs on creativity and quality and only forwarding those that exceed thresholds.[15] These evaluator agents effectively act as inner judges that gate the progression of ideas, much like the scoring step in your “adhd” skill.

Human intervention can further improve outcomes in multi-agent debate. A recent paper on human intervention in LLM multi-agent debate shows that inserting human judgments into the loop can steer conversations, mitigate failure modes, and improve alignment with user values.[9] In a brainstorming context, this suggests that your system could optionally expose interim idea clusters or rationales to a human user who can upvote, downvote, or annotate them, thereby guiding subsequent agent iterations. However, given your desire to keep context tight for the skill, human-in-the-loop steps might be optional or batched.

For divergent ideation, an important lesson from these multi-agent studies is that interaction patterns matter: simply having multiple agents generate ideas independently may not be enough to unlock synergistic creativity.[15] Agents that only operate in parallel risk producing redundant ideas, while agents that engage in structured critique, cross-pollination, and synthesis can generate higher-quality, more diverse ideas. For your system, one could redesign the five-agent structure so that some agents are tasked explicitly with proposing unconventional ideas under certain constraints, while others critique feasibility or fit, and yet another agent plays the role of synthesizer, combining promising elements into new concepts.

### Evolutionary and Quality-Diversity Architectures: FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, MAP-Elites, and MEliTA

Evolutionary methods that pair LLMs with automated evaluators have shown striking success in discovering novel algorithms and creative artifacts, and they are particularly relevant to your interest in novelty, viability, and fit scoring.[4][11][16] FunSearch, introduced by Google DeepMind, is a method where a code-trained LLM proposes candidate programs to solve mathematical problems, and an automated evaluator runs these programs and scores them based on correctness and quality.[4] The best-performing programs are stored in a database and used as seeds for future proposals, creating an evolutionary loop that steadily improves solutions.[4] Crucially, FunSearch balances creativity and correctness by offloading verification to an external evaluator, allowing the LLM to generate bold ideas without the risk of deploying incorrect ones.

AlphaEvolve extends this paradigm to general-purpose algorithm discovery using an evolutionary coding agent.[4] It uses a prompt sampler that composes rich context from previously discovered solutions, an ensemble of LLMs (Gemini Flash for high-throughput candidate generation and Gemini Pro for deeper insights), an evaluator pool that executes and scores programs, and a program database inspired by MAP-Elites and island models to maintain diversity.[4] The database preserves high-performing solutions across different behavioral niches while continuing to explore new regions of the solution space.[4] This multi-component architecture provides a powerful template for ideation systems: LLMs propose candidates, automated or semi-automated evaluators score them along relevant dimensions, and a quality-diversity database preserves the best candidates per niche.

MAP-Elites itself is a quality-diversity algorithm that partitions a behavioral descriptor space into a grid and seeks to fill each cell with a high-quality individual.[11][16] Santos and coauthors apply MAP-Elites to the prompt space, using context-free grammars to generate prompts with varying structural traits and mapping them to performance and diversity metrics on BigBench Lite tasks.[11] Their results show that MAP-Elites can generate high-performing, structurally diverse prompts and reveal how structural variations influence LLM performance.[11] Zammit and colleagues adapt MAP-Elites to multimodal creative tasks with MEliTA, decoupling modalities and promoting cross-pollination between elites across niches, leading to improved text-to-image mappings for video-game concept art.[16] In both cases, quality-diversity methods preserve a repertoire of diverse, high-performing solutions rather than a single optimum.

These evolutionary and quality-diversity architectures translate naturally to multi-agent brainstorming. Instead of programs or prompts, the individuals in the population are candidate ideas or idea clusters, and the behavioral descriptors correspond to dimensions like problem subdomain, degree of novelty, risk level, or resource requirements. A simple yet powerful adaptation for your “adhd” system would be to store ideas in a grid indexed by, for example, novelty score and feasibility score, then preserve the top idea or cluster in each cell across iterations. Agents can then be instructed to mutate these elites or explore underpopulated regions of the grid. This prevents convergence on a single “best” idea and encourages the system to maintain a portfolio of options spanning a range of risk and originality.

### Island Models and Population-Based Brainstorming

Island models, used in evolutionary computation, divide populations into subpopulations (islands) that evolve semi-independently with occasional migration of individuals between islands.[4][16] AlphaEvolve’s evolutionary database is inspired in part by island-based models, using separate islands or niches to maintain diversity.[4] For brainstorming, each island can correspond to a distinct cognitive frame or persona, with local selection optimizing ideas within that frame and occasional cross-island exchanges promoting creative recombination.

Your current architecture, where five agents operate under different cognitive frames, is analogous to a five-island model without migration or generational evolution. Each agent generates ideas once, they are clustered and scored, and the top three ideas are deepened. The literature suggests that you can significantly improve this by adding inter-agent migration and iterative evolution. For example, top ideas from one agent could be passed as seeds to another agent with a very different persona, asking that agent to transform or extend them from its perspective. This cross-pollination aligns with MEliTA’s decoupling and cross-pollination across modalities and is likely to yield hybrids that combine novelty from one frame with feasibility or fit from another.[16] Implementing such an island model would transform your pipeline from a single-shot parallel sampler into a multi-generation, quality-diversity search process that systematically explores and refines ideas.

## Scoring, LLM-as-Judge Biases, and Convergence Strategies

### Known Biases in LLM-As-Judge Systems

Many ideation pipelines, including your “adhd” skill, rely on LLM-based scoring of ideas along dimensions such as novelty, feasibility, and fit. Recent work, however, has documented substantial biases in LLM judges that must be explicitly mitigated to ensure reliable scoring and convergence.

A comprehensive study titled “Justice or Prejudice? Quantifying Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge” introduces a framework called Calm to analyze various judge biases, including position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and others.[13] Position bias refers to the tendency of an LLM judge to favor responses based on their position in the input sequence; for example, preferring the first or last answer consistently.[13] The authors show that all judge models they examined, including models like GPT-4 and Claude 3.5, are significantly impacted by position bias, with robustness rates often below 0.5 when evaluating multiple options.[13] Verbosity bias denotes the preference for longer responses even when shorter ones are clearer or more accurate.[13] Experiments demonstrate that judge models frequently rate more verbose answers higher than concise but correct ones.[13]

Self-enhancement bias is particularly relevant if your generation and judge models are the same or closely related. It describes the tendency of an LLM to rate its own outputs more favorably than those generated by other models, even when the source of each answer is anonymized.[13] The Calm study finds strong evidence for self-enhancement bias across several judge models, quantifying how much more favorably they score their own responses compared to external ones.[13] This implies that if your “adhd” system ever compares ideas generated by different models or by humans, judges tied to a specific model will systematically skew evaluations.

These biases interact with structuring decisions in your system. For instance, if your scoring step always presents ideas in the same order (for example, Agent 1’s ideas first and Agent 5’s last), position bias will distort scores. If some frames are instructed to write more detail than others, verbosity bias will inflate their scores irrespective of underlying idea quality. And if you use the same model for both idea generation and scoring, self-enhancement bias may cause the system to overvalue its own style and typicality, reinforcing mode collapse.

### Limits of LLM Novelty and Impact Judgments

Beyond these generic biases, specialized studies on LLM assessment of research ideas reveal deeper limitations in novelty and impact judgment. The HindSight framework evaluates LLM-generated research ideas by matching them against real future publications and measuring citation impact and venue acceptance over a 30-month window.[5] Using a temporal cutoff to restrict accessible literature, the authors compared a vanilla idea generation system to a retrieval-augmented system and found, via HindSight, that retrieval-augmented ideas achieve 2.5 times higher scores in terms of future impact and alignment with subsequent research trends.[5] However, when they used LLM-as-judge evaluations to score the same ideas on novelty, feasibility, and expected impact, the LLM judges showed no significant difference between the two systems.[5] This exposes a disconnect between LLM-judged quality and real-world impact.

Even more strikingly, HindSight scores are negatively correlated with LLM-judged novelty, with a correlation coefficient around \(\rho = -0.29\) and \(p < 0.01\).[5] This means that ideas the LLM judge considers more novel are, on average, less likely to correspond to actual future research that gets published and cited.[5] The authors interpret this as evidence that LLM judges overvalue “novel-sounding” framing, such as ambitious, broad statements, while undervaluing practically grounded ideas that anticipate real trends.[5] At the same time, HindSight scores correlate positively with LLM-judged feasibility, indicating that more feasible ideas are more likely to materialize in future work.[5]

Parallel work on the limits of LLM-as-judge for scientific novelty evaluation reinforces these concerns, arguing that full novelty evaluation is particularly challenging and that LLM judges are prone to conflating linguistic originality with substantive novelty.[14] Together, these studies suggest that naive reliance on LLM-judged novelty scores in your system could systematically mis-rank ideas, favoring flashy but unrealistic ones over grounded, high-impact concepts.

### Designing More Reliable Scoring Pipelines

Given these biases and limitations, a robust scoring and convergence mechanism for your “adhd” system should incorporate several design principles derived from the literature.

First, separation of generation and evaluation is critical. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve achieve reliability by pairing a pre-trained LLM with an automated evaluator that executes and tests candidate programs objectively, rather than relying on LLM self-judgment.[4] For ideation tasks where automated execution is not possible, a partial analogue is to use a different model or a different configuration of the same model as the judge, reducing self-enhancement bias.[13] Where feasible, including retrieval-augmented context during judging can help ground feasibility and fit assessments in existing knowledge, as the HindSight study shows that retrieval-augmented ideas align better with future trends.[5]

Second, process-level mitigations can reduce position and verbosity biases. Randomizing the order in which ideas are presented to judges helps counter position bias, as recommended in the Calm study.[13] Explicitly instructing judges to ignore superficial length and to focus on clarity and substance, and enforcing length normalization (for example, trimming or summarizing ideas to a comparable length before scoring) can mitigate verbosity bias.[13] Additionally, scoring multiple criteria separately and then combining them with weights informed by empirical evidence can help manage trade-offs. Given the negative correlation between judged novelty and real impact, it is prudent to assign relatively lower weight to raw novelty scores and higher weight to feasibility and fit, at least in domains where practical implementation matters.[5]

Third, clustering before selection is advantageous. Studies of LLM creativity metrics propose using embedding-based similarity to cluster outputs, then scoring clusters rather than individual ideas.[6][15] This helps ensure that redundancy is minimized and that selection captures diverse conceptual regions. For example, your system can embed all candidate ideas from the five agents, perform clustering into, say, 8–12 clusters, then select representative ideas from each cluster for scoring. Judges then compare cluster representatives rather than a large, redundant list. Cluster-level selection can better approximate quality-diversity criteria, preserving at least one high-quality idea per distinct conceptual category.

Fourth, multi-dimensional scoring and Pareto-style convergence are preferable to single-composite scores. Creativity surveys emphasize that creativity is multi-faceted, typically characterized by fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration, as in the Torrance framework.[7][15] Your current scoring dimensions of novelty, viability, and fit map reasonably well to originality, feasibility, and contextual appropriateness. Rather than collapsing these into a single scalar score upfront, you can maintain a Pareto frontier of ideas that are non-dominated across dimensions and only make scalar trade-offs at the final convergence step. This aligns with quality-diversity methods that maintain elites across multiple descriptors rather than a single objective.[11][16]

Finally, incremental convergence over several rounds, with feedback loops, can stabilize scoring. Multi-agent creativity systems often use iterative evaluation where evaluator agents provide feedback that proposer agents use to refine ideas.[15] Your system could adopt a similar pattern: after an initial scoring, top ideas are fed back to agents for refinement, with explicit instructions to address feasibility concerns or clarify fit, and then re-scored. This mirrors the multi-stage prompts that increased idea diversity in the persona-plus-CoT experiments.[17] Importantly, this iterative process should be bounded and monitored to avoid convergence to a single narrow idea; maintaining cluster-level diversity and quality-diversity niches can provide such bounds.

### Example Scoring and Convergence Adaptation for the “adhd” Skill

To make these principles concrete for your architecture, consider the following design. Each of the five agents generates a set of ideas using VS-style distribution prompts and chain-of-thought reasoning within its persona. All ideas are pooled and embedded, and semantic clustering identifies distinct conceptual groups. For each cluster, a representative idea or synthesized summary is chosen. A separate judge model, or at least the same base model with different system prompts and no access to generation context, evaluates each cluster representative on novelty, feasibility, and fit, with ideas presented in randomized order and length-normalized summaries. Scores are recorded, but instead of selecting a single top-three list by scalarizing scores, the system identifies a Pareto set of clusters as potential candidates for deepening.

Subsequently, one or two agents specializing in synthesis and refinement are tasked with deepening these cluster-level ideas, explicitly addressing feasibility and fit issues identified in judge rationales. The final convergence step presents the user with a small set of diverse, high-quality ideas along with transparent rationales and uncertainties. Throughout this pipeline, measures such as the number of semantically unique valid ideas per cluster can be tracked to monitor effective diversity.[6] This design directly incorporates evidence from studies of LLM biases, quality-diversity methods, and multi-agent creativity systems, and it represents a concrete, defensible improvement over a single-pass scoring and top-three selection process.

## Creativity Benchmarks and Metrics for LLM Ideation

### Human Creativity Tests Adapted to LLMs

A significant portion of recent research has focused on adapting human creativity benchmarks to LLMs to measure their divergent thinking and originality systematically. These benchmarks are informative not only as evaluation tools but also as inspirations for prompt and architecture design.

The Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (TTCT) are among the most widely used human creativity assessments, scoring responses along four main dimensions: fluency (number of responses), flexibility (variety of categories), originality (statistical rarity), and elaboration (level of detail).[7][15] Zhao and colleagues created a dataset of 700 questions based on modified TTCT criteria and used LLM-based evaluation methods to assess creativity across seven tasks.[7] Their findings indicate that LLMs often show strong elaboration and fluency but weaker originality compared with human baselines.[7] This suggests that brainstorming systems should deliberately emphasize mechanisms that promote flexibility and originality rather than relying solely on the LLM’s natural tendency toward detailed elaboration.

The Alternative Uses Test (AUT) is another classic divergent thinking test in which participants list as many novel uses as possible for common objects.[2][15] Recent studies have used AUT prompts to probe LLM creativity, with mixed results: overall performance may be comparable to human baselines on some items, but differences emerge in specific cases.[2] Automated scoring methods based on semantic distance or statistical rarity can be used to quantify the uniqueness of LLM responses.[2][15] Similarly, the Remote Associates Test (RAT) evaluates associative creativity by asking participants to find a word connecting several seemingly unrelated words; while less directly ideational, it measures a component of creative association that is relevant to LLM internal representations.[15]

The Divergent Association Task (DAT), used in the large-scale study of 100,000 humans and multiple LLMs, specifically measures semantic distance across a list of words.[2] Semantic distance is computed as the mean cosine distance between word embeddings, so higher DAT scores indicate that the words are further apart semantically.[2] GPT‑4 was found to surpass human scores statistically, whereas other LLMs such as Gemini Pro were roughly on par with humans.[2] Although DAT concerns word lists rather than multi-sentence ideas, its methodology illustrates how semantic embeddings can be used to quantify divergent thinking. Similar embedding-based measures underlie more complex metrics like Divergent Semantic Integration (DSI), which measures semantic distance across sentence-level narratives.[2]

These benchmarks highlight an important pattern: when creativity is defined purely in terms of associative spread or semantic distance, LLMs can match or surpass human performance, but when originality is assessed relative to human norms or when practical feasibility is considered, LLM performance exhibits specific weaknesses.[2][7][15] This reinforces the need for ideation systems to combine measures of semantic diversity with domain-specific evaluations of feasibility and value.

### Diversity and Originality Metrics for Text Outputs

Beyond adapted psychological tests, researchers have proposed a variety of automated metrics to evaluate the diversity and originality of LLM-generated text, many of which are directly applicable to your system’s internal monitoring.

Surface-level diversity metrics include Distinct-n and Entropy-n, which compute the proportion of unique n-grams and the entropy over n-gram distributions, respectively.[15] Distinct-n measures lexical richness, with higher values indicating more unique phrases, while Entropy-n measures how evenly distributed those phrases are across outputs.[15] These metrics are useful proxies for variety but do not necessarily capture semantic differences; an LLM could achieve high Distinct-n by minor paraphrasing while remaining conceptually redundant.

Self-BLEU is a sentence-level internal diversity metric that treats each generated sentence as a hypothesis and the rest as references, computing BLEU scores to quantify redundancy.[15] Lower Self-BLEU scores indicate greater internal diversity.
