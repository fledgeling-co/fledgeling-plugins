# Mapping Eligibility of Software and SaaS Operating Expenditure under the Australian R&D Tax Incentive

The Australian Research and Development Tax Incentive (R&DTI) is deliberately structured to reward experimental activities that generate new knowledge, but it only allows notional deductions for expenditure to the extent that those costs are genuinely incurred on eligible core or supporting R&D activities rather than routine operations.[1][9][13] For a small Australian software company relying heavily on cloud services and SaaS tools, the key compliance challenge is therefore not whether the business is “innovative” in a broad sense, but whether each subscription or software expense can be shown to have a direct nexus to registered R&D activities and, where relevant, to have been incurred for the dominant purpose of supporting those experimental activities.[1][2][7] This report synthesises statutory requirements, AusIndustry and ATO guidance, and specialist commentary to map which categories of software and SaaS operating expenditure are typically eligible, how mixed-use tools should be apportioned, and what records an accountant will need to substantiate each line item in a claim schedule. It also examines overseas-supplied services, GST treatment, and common review triggers in software R&D claims, to support decisive classification of each expense line as inside or outside the R&DTI.

## The Statutory Tests for Core and Supporting R&D Activities

### The Object and Structure of Division 355 ITAA 1997

Division 355 of the *Income Tax Assessment Act 1997* establishes the R&D Tax Incentive regime and explicitly states that its object is to encourage industry to conduct research and development activities.[9] The statutory design is activity-based: eligibility is assessed against particular activities rather than entire projects or business models, and only expenditure linked to those eligible activities can be notionally deducted at enhanced rates.[9][16] This activity focus is critical for software companies where a single project may mix genuinely experimental work with routine development, maintenance, or commercial deployment; the law requires that the experimental components be separated and described as core R&D activities, while related but non-experimental work may only be included as supporting R&D activities if specific tests are met.[1][11][16]

Division 355 distinguishes between “R&D activities” broadly and the subsets of “core” and “supporting” R&D activities that attract the tax incentive.[9] R&D activities must be conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge, including new or improved materials, products, processes or services, and this encompasses software, SaaS platforms, and AI systems.[1][3][12] However, not all business development or product delivery falls within this definition; a key statutory filter is that core R&D activities must be experimental in nature and follow a systematic progression of work based on principles of established science.[1][11][16] Supporting activities sit conceptually downstream: they need not be experimental themselves, but must be directly related to core R&D activities and, in many cases, conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting those activities.[1][2]

In practice, the statutory object and structure are operationalised through joint guidance from AusIndustry (Department of Industry, Science and Resources) and the ATO, which emphasises that the regime is intended to support genuine experimental activities, not routine software implementation, compliance work, or purely commercial optimisation.[1][3][16] This means that when analysing software subscriptions, the legal team and accountants must first understand whether the underlying work being carried out (for example, experiments with novel architectures, AI model training, or performance-scale uncertainty) can be characterised as core R&D activities before assessing whether each cost is sufficiently connected to those activities. The classification of expenditure cannot be done in isolation from the activity descriptions registered with AusIndustry.[7][13][17]

### Core R&D Activities: Experimental Work with Unknown Outcomes

Core R&D activities are defined as experimental activities conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge where the outcome cannot be known or determined in advance and can only be determined by applying a systematic progression of work involving hypothesis, experiment, observation, evaluation, and logical conclusions.[1][11][16] This definition contains several cumulative elements that must all be satisfied. First, the activity must be experimental rather than merely demonstrative or routine; there needs to be a structured attempt to test a technical hypothesis in order to resolve a specific technical uncertainty.[11][16] Second, the outcome must not be reasonably determinable in advance by a competent professional in the field using publicly available or reasonably accessible knowledge; if existing methods, tools, or patterns can predict the outcome, the activity will generally not qualify as core R&D.[11][16]

For software development, AusIndustry’s sector guidance clarifies that core R&D activities arise when a particular technical hurdle prevents progress and cannot be resolved using existing knowledge or tools, even for experienced professionals.[3] Examples include integrating a novel AI agent into a platform where no known method can guarantee performance within bounded latency, or developing a new algorithm for large-scale data processing with unknown scaling behaviour that must be experimentally tested.[3][12] In these cases, the team formulates a hypothesis about a proposed solution or architecture, designs experiments (such as prototype builds, benchmarking, or simulation), and evaluates results to determine whether the hypothesis is confirmed or refuted.[3][11] By contrast, routine software tasks such as applying documented configuration options, integrating third-party APIs using vendor documentation, or assembling user interfaces with known components and patterns are explicitly described as generally ineligible as core R&D because their outcomes are predictable.[3]

The statutory requirement for a systematic progression of work is not satisfied by ad hoc trial-and-error or informal tinkering; AusIndustry expects to see evidence of planned experiments, hypotheses, and evaluation recorded contemporaneously in engineering artefacts such as tickets, design notes, test plans, and performance logs.[3][7][11] For scale-up activities, where experimental work moves from prototype to larger deployment, the same requirements apply: the scale-up must address a continuing technical uncertainty that cannot be resolved with existing knowledge, including knowledge generated at smaller scale, and must involve hypothesis-driven experiments.[11] If scale-up simply applies known results from earlier experiments, it will not qualify as core R&D, though it may in some circumstances be treated as supporting.[11] This framework establishes the baseline against which all subsequent expenditure analysis must be carried out.

### Supporting R&D Activities and the Dominant Purpose Test

Supporting R&D activities under Division 355 are activities that are directly related to core R&D activities and, where specific conditions apply, conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting those core activities.[1][2] “Directly related” is a nexus concept: there must be a close operational connection between the supporting activity and the experimental work, such that the supporting activity is integral to, or necessary for, conducting, refining, or evaluating the core experiments.[1][7][13] Supporting activities may include tasks such as data collection, setting up test environments, configuring monitoring tools used in experiments, or software maintenance performed to enable ongoing experiments, provided there is clear linkage to identified core R&D activities.[1][3][7]

The “dominant purpose” test is triggered in two situations. First, where the activity falls within the list of excluded core R&D activities in the legislation or guidance, it cannot be treated as core R&D and may only be treated as supporting R&D if conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting core R&D activities.[1][2][3] Excluded activities relevant to software include market research, management studies, activities associated with complying with statutory requirements or standards, and developing or modifying software for internal administration of business functions.[1][3][16] Second, where the supporting activity is directly related to the production of goods or services, such as production trials in a manufacturing or SaaS deployment context, the dominant purpose test also applies.[1][2] PwC’s commentary, reflecting case law, explains that “dominant purpose” means the ruling, prevailing, or most influential purpose, and that merely having some commercial objective does not preclude an activity from qualifying if the most influential purpose is to support core R&D.[2]

Importantly, case law and ATO guidance emphasise that the fact an activity is necessary for core R&D to occur is not sufficient by itself to show that the activity was undertaken for the dominant purpose of supporting core R&D.[2][13] For example, general IT administration, email systems, or standard development tools may be necessary for engineers to work, but their dominant purpose is often broader business operation rather than supporting specific experiments.[13][14][18] Conversely, a cloud compute environment spun up exclusively for running benchmark experiments as part of a registered core activity may satisfy the dominant purpose test where production hosting is separated.[5][14][18] The burden falls on the taxpayer to demonstrate, with contemporaneous documentation, that each supporting activity subject to the dominant purpose test was actually conducted with the main objective of facilitating the experimental work rather than simply delivering products or services.[2][7][13]

### Activity-Based Eligibility and Software Development Risk

ATO Taxpayer Alert TA 2017/5 highlights common risk patterns in software R&D claims, underscoring that eligibility is determined at the level of activities rather than entire software development projects.[16] The alert notes that many companies have claimed the R&DTI for projects involving developing new software, modifying or upgrading existing software, or acquiring and customising off-the-shelf software, but where significant portions of the work involve applying existing software knowledge and tools to achieve required outcomes without real technical uncertainty.[16] It warns that activities characterised by business risk rather than technical risk, activities that simply automate manual processes using readily available technologies, and activities using existing software technologies as intended are not eligible unless they are being conducted as experiments to test hypotheses about specific technical issues.[16]

The alert also clarifies that testing activities such as bug testing, beta testing, system testing, user acceptance testing, data mapping and migration testing, and testing the efficiency of already-known algorithms are not eligible as core R&D unless they form part of experiments designed to prove or disprove technical hypotheses.[16] They may, however, be eligible as supporting activities if directly related to core R&D experiments.[16] Critically, the alert underscores that software development projects undertaken for the sole or dominant purpose of internal use by the company or its affiliates, for internal administration of business functions, are not eligible for the R&DTI.[16][3] This has direct implications when classifying software and SaaS expenditure: subscriptions used primarily for internal administration, such as HR platforms or general accounting tools, will typically sit outside the claim, even if R&D is undertaken elsewhere in the business.

In summary, the statutory tests for core and supporting R&D activities require that each claimed activity be either experimental work with unknown outcomes and systematic hypotheses, or directly related work conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting such experiments.[1][2][11][16] For software and SaaS expenditure, this means that eligibility depends on whether the use of each tool is sufficiently bound up with core or supporting R&D activities as defined above, rather than simply being part of day-to-day operations or routine development. The next sections translate these activity-based tests into expenditure nexus and category-level analysis.

## Nexus and “Directly Related” Tests for Software and SaaS Expenditure

### Expenditure Must Be Incurred on Registered R&D Activities

The ATO’s guidance on keeping records and calculating notional deductions emphasises that R&D expenditure can only be claimed to the extent that it has been incurred on R&D activities that are registered with AusIndustry.[13][4] This creates a two-step nexus requirement. First, the company must register eligible core and supporting R&D activities with the Department of Industry, Science and Resources for each income year.[1][7][17] Second, the company must show that the amounts it is claiming in the tax return relate to those registered activities, not to other non-registered or ordinary business activities.[13][17] Expenditure that would have been incurred regardless of whether R&D activities occurred, such as routine marketing or general corporate overhead, generally lacks the necessary nexus.[13][14]

The ATO states that to work out the notional R&D deduction and support claims, taxpayers must keep records showing how each item of expenditure relates to registered R&D activities, how expenditure has been apportioned between eligible and ineligible activities, that the taxpayer received the major benefit from the R&D activities, and when amounts were paid to associates.[13] It notes that administrative costs and overheads may be incurred on R&D activities if there is a direct link between the R&D activities and the expenditure, but ineligible expenses are those without sufficient link, particularly if they relate to general company operations or marketing expenditure that would be incurred regardless of R&D.[13] This framework applies directly to recurring software subscriptions and cloud services, many of which fall within overhead or operational cost categories.

AusIndustry’s record-keeping guidance complements this by requiring that records link R&D activities to related expenditure and show what was done, how it was done, when, by whom, with what resources, and at what cost.[7] This includes showing which facilities, equipment, tools, and materials were used in R&D activities, and which expenditure items relate to those uses.[7] For software-specific R&D, AusIndustry suggests capturing digital boards, workshop notes, issue tracking systems, emails, chat messages, technical reports, and logs that demonstrate technical hurdles, hypotheses, experiments, and evaluation.[3][7] When a cloud subscription or SaaS tool is used in experiments, contemporaneous records should show how that tool was configured and used in the context of registered activities, thereby evidencing the nexus required for claiming its cost.

### Treatment of Overheads and Software Subscriptions as R&D Expenditure

Specialist expenditure guides emphasise that operational and administrative costs associated with R&D activities may be claimable under the R&DTI, provided they can be clearly linked to R&D activities and are properly documented.[14][18] Examples include office rent, utilities, certain insurance costs, office consumables, software subscriptions, and relevant training costs, but not all indirect costs qualify.[14] Non-claimable overheads typically include accounting and legal fees, advertising, bank charges, employee benefits, facility maintenance, director fees, donations, and entertainment.[14][13] In addition, capital expenditure on depreciating assets and building costs, interest, non-risk expenditure where the business does not bear financial exposure, and expenditure on acquiring technology or intellectual property are specifically excluded categories.[14]

With respect to software subscriptions and cloud hosting, commentary indicates that these can be claimable as overheads or directly incurred expenditure to the extent they are used for R&D activities.[14][18] For example, Rand Advisory notes that software subscriptions and cloud hosting can be claimed as R&D expenditure, either as overheads or directly incurred costs, but only to the extent they are used in conducting registered R&D activities.[18] This implies that cloud compute costs for environments running experimental builds, test harnesses, or data-processing prototypes will generally have a stronger nexus than costs for production hosting of a commercial SaaS platform serving customers, even if both environments run on the same provider. Similarly, subscriptions for issue tracking, source control, observability, or AI tools can be claimable where those tools are used by staff engaged in core or supporting R&D, but must be apportioned where they also support non-R&D tasks.[13][14][18]

The ATO emphasises accurate apportionment in salary and overhead claims, especially where staff and tools are used across both R&D and non-R&D activities.[13][14] While this guidance is framed in terms of wages and general overheads, the principle applies equally to software subscriptions. If a Slack subscription is used by the entire company for general communication, only the portion reasonably attributable to R&D activities (for example, usage by the R&D team in coordinating experiments) could be claimed, and even then only if a direct link can be demonstrated through records.[13][14] By contrast, a specialised monitoring tool configured exclusively for an experimental environment might be more comfortably treated as fully incurred on a supporting R&D activity directly related to that core experiment.[1][2][3] The differentiation rests on both factual use and documented linkage.

### AusIndustry and ATO Positions on Software R&D and Expenditure Nexus

AusIndustry’s software development sector guide directly addresses the eligibility of software-related activities and provides nuanced guidance relevant to expenditure nexus.[3] It clarifies that applying a particular software development methodology (agile, waterfall, rapid) does not in itself make an activity eligible or ineligible; individual activities must be assessed against core or supporting criteria.[3] It emphasises that routine activities such as configuration, standard integration, UI assembly, dashboard building, and data migration are generally routine and unlikely to be eligible as core R&D activities because they involve applying known tools with predictable outcomes, though they may be eligible as supporting R&D activities if directly related to a core activity.[3] This distinction has expenditure implications: subscriptions used solely for routine work without technical uncertainty would not support core R&D, while those used during experiments might.

The guide also reiterates statutory exclusions, including an explicit exclusion for developing, modifying, or customising computer software for the dominant purpose of use by the developer or its affiliates for internal administration of business functions.[3][16] Activities of this kind cannot be core R&D activities, although some may qualify as supporting R&D activities if directly related to other core experiments and conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting them.[1][3][2] This matters in expenditure analysis because subscriptions primarily used to build internal administration systems may yield little or no claimable R&D expenditure unless documented as supporting more fundamental experimental work.

ATO’s TA 2017/5 underscores the risk that companies may claim the R&DTI on software development projects where significant parts of the expenditure are on activities that are not eligible R&D activities.[16] It notes that registration of activities does not by itself render those activities eligible, and that ATO and AusIndustry may review both eligibility of activities and related expenditure after registration.[16][17] The alert encourages companies to review their registrations to ensure only eligible R&D activities are registered, to ensure claims exclude expenditure on ordinary business activities, and to maintain records that demonstrate the experimental nature of activities and support claims.[16] This includes expenditure on software tools; if a subscription cost is claimed but records only show routine business use, it is at heightened risk of being disallowed.

Taken together, these positions establish that recurring software and SaaS expenditure can be eligible R&D expenditure when there is a clear, documented nexus to registered core or supporting R&D activities, and where the expenditure is incurred on those activities rather than on general operations.[1][3][7][13][14][18] The remainder of this report applies these principles to specific expense categories relevant to a small Australian software company.

## Category-by-Category Eligibility of Software and SaaS Operating Expenses

### Cloud Compute and Hosting (Azure, AWS, Vercel, Fly.io)

Cloud compute and hosting services such as Azure, AWS, Vercel, and Fly.io are typically used for a variety of purposes: development environments, test environments, staging, production hosting of customer-facing systems, and sometimes internal tools.[14][18] Under the R&DTI, their eligibility depends on the proportion of usage that can be demonstrably linked to registered R&D activities. If cloud resources are provisioned for experimental environments in which core R&D activities are conducted—for example, running performance experiments on a novel distributed architecture, or training and evaluating AI models as part of a technical hypothesis—then the associated hosting and compute costs for those environments are generally capable of being claimed as R&D expenditure.[1][3][12][14][18] These costs would usually be treated as directly incurred on core R&D or as supporting R&D expenditure, depending on whether the cloud environment itself is part of the experiment or merely hosting it.

In many small software companies, however, the same cloud account or subscription is used for both R&D and ordinary business functions, including production hosting of commercial SaaS offerings.[14][18] In these cases, apportionment is required. The ATO’s guidance on apportionment states that R&D expenses can only be claimed to the extent they are incurred on R&D activities, and that companies must be able to support their methodology for apportioning expenditure between eligible and non-eligible activities.[13] For cloud services, an acceptable apportionment methodology might involve segregating environments (for example, separate projects or resource groups for R&D versus production), tracking resource usage by environment, and attributing only the usage tied to experimental workloads to the R&DTI claim.[13][14][18] Where such segregation is absent, a more approximate method based on time, user counts, or project-level allocations may be used, but this increases evidentiary risk.

It is important to distinguish between cloud usage that is integral to experiments and usage that merely supports commercial operations. For instance, spinning up autoscaling clusters to run experimental load tests that aim to resolve technical uncertainty about system behaviour is likely a supporting R&D activity directly related to a core testing activity, and cloud costs for that environment can be claimed if properly documented.[1][11][14] Conversely, hosting a stable production API with known performance characteristics and no ongoing technical uncertainty is not itself an R&D activity, and costs for that environment would generally be excluded.[16][3][13] The same provider may be used in both contexts, but the R&DTI claim must be limited to the experimental or supporting usage.

From a risk perspective, AusIndustry and the ATO have signalled heightened scrutiny of software claims that attempt to treat broad platform costs (including all of AWS or Azure bills) as R&D, particularly where registrations are framed around entire product development and deployment life cycles rather than discrete experimental activities.[16][3] For a 79-line expense claim covering cloud subscriptions, the legal team should classify each line by environment and purpose—development/test for experiments versus production/ops—and mark as eligible only the portion of costs demonstrably incurred for core or supporting R&D activities. True/False classification for each line will therefore depend on whether that particular subscription period and resource usage correspond to experimental workloads or routine hosting, supported by invoices, architecture diagrams, and environment logs.

### Observability, Logging, and Error Monitoring (Sentry, Axiom)

Observability and error monitoring tools such as Sentry and Axiom serve dual roles in software businesses: they provide visibility into production systems for operational reliability, and they can be used to monitor experimental environments and collect data needed for evaluating hypotheses.[3][7][11] Under the R&DTI, logging and monitoring activities are generally not core R&D activities because they do not themselves involve experiments designed to resolve technical uncertainty.[11][16] However, they can often qualify as supporting R&D activities where they are directly related to core experiments and conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting those experiments.

For example, if an experiment aims to test whether a new algorithm reduces error rates under specific conditions, and Sentry is configured to capture granular error metrics from the experimental build, then the use of Sentry in that context is directly related to the core R&D activity and supports observation and evaluation components of the systematic progression of work.[3][11][7] The associated subscription cost for the period during which Sentry is used in this way could be claimed as supporting R&D expenditure, provided records (configuration snapshots, dashboards, tickets) show that the monitoring was designed and used specifically to evaluate experimental hypotheses.[3][7][13] In contrast, a Sentry subscription used mainly to monitor a mature production system for routine bug tracking and uptime, with no link to ongoing experiments, would be an operational cost not eligible for the R&DTI.

The dominant purpose test may apply where observability tools are used across both R&D and production environments, or where they fall within excluded categories such as activities associated with complying with statutory requirements or standards (for instance, monitoring driven entirely by service-level agreements or regulatory uptime obligations).[1][2][3] In those cases, the taxpayer must show that the prevailing purpose of the monitoring configuration or usage was to support core R&D activities, not merely to meet commercial or compliance objectives.[2] If a Sentry project is set up solely for an experimental microservice and turned off after experiments conclude, the evidentiary case for dominant R&D purpose is stronger than where a single project monitors all services in perpetuity.

For the expense schedule, each line for Sentry or Axiom subscriptions should be assessed against these criteria. If the subscription was used during a period where the tool’s primary usage was monitoring experimental builds as part of registered core activities, and there is documentation tying dashboards, alerts, or traces to those experiments, the line may be marked TRUE (eligible) in whole or part. If usage was predominantly operational, unrelated to R&D, the line should be marked FALSE. Mixed periods may need partial apportionment, but given subscription granularity, the practical approach is often to treat entire months as eligible or ineligible based on dominant use.

### Managed Databases and Data Services (Redis Cloud and Similar)

Managed databases such as Redis Cloud, as well as other data services, underpin both experimental and production workloads. Under the R&DTI, database configuration and usage may form part of core R&D activities where they are integral to experiments addressing technical uncertainty—for example, evaluating novel caching strategies, consistency mechanisms, or high-throughput data pipelines whose performance characteristics are unknown and must be tested.[3][11][12] In such cases, the experimental activity might involve constructing specific data models, configuring Redis in unusual ways, or implementing custom sharding strategies, and running experiments to observe behaviour under varied loads. The managed database subscription cost associated with these experimental deployments can be claimed as R&D expenditure, either directly on core activities or as supporting activities facilitating experiments.[1][3][14]

However, routine use of managed databases to support standard application state, sessions, or simple caching where known patterns exist and outcomes are predictable is generally not core R&D, as it involves applying established methods without resolving technical uncertainty.[3][16] If a Redis Cloud subscription is primarily used for conventional production caching in a typical SaaS stack, the associated cost would lack the necessary nexus to R&D and should be excluded. Even in mixed-use scenarios, the burden is to allocate database instances or clusters between experimental and non-experimental workloads and apportion subscription costs accordingly, with supporting evidence from configuration, billing, and logs.[13][14][18]

Another dimension is the dominant purpose of any supporting activity associated with database usage. If a particular Redis instance is set up exclusively to support experiments on an AI model’s latency under varied caching strategies, and the service is retired after experiments, the subscription cost arguably meets the dominant purpose requirement for supporting R&D.[1][2][12] If the same instance serves live customers and occasionally supports experiments, the case is weaker, and apportionment based on usage metrics or time windows is needed.[13][14] For classification in the claim schedule, each line item should be linked to specific instances or projects; if billing records or internal tagging can show that a subscription relates to trial environments or research clusters, those lines may be marked TRUE, while lines for pure production instances should be marked FALSE.

### AI and LLM APIs and AI Assistant Subscriptions (Anthropic Claude, ElevenLabs, xAI/Grok)

AI and LLM APIs, as well as AI assistant subscriptions, represent an emerging class of software tools with particular R&DTI relevance. AusIndustry’s AI-related activities guidance explains that AI activities may involve scoping and designing AI-enabled solutions, developing and training models, testing and evaluating performance, integrating AI into platforms, deploying models, and monitoring or maintaining them.[12] It emphasises that using an AI model or technique that is new to the taxpayer does not by itself make an activity eligible; AI-related activities are only eligible as core R&D where a technical hurdle exists and can only be resolved through experimentation, with hypotheses tested through planned experiments.[12] Conversely, using AI APIs for routine tasks with known behaviours and no technical uncertainty is generally outside the scope of core R&D.

For AI and LLM API usage, eligibility therefore hinges on whether the company is conducting experiments to resolve technical uncertainty about the capability, method, or appropriate design of AI components in its system.[12][3] For example, calling Anthropic Claude’s API in repeated experiments to assess different prompt-engineering strategies, training pipelines, or hybrid retrieval architectures where performance and reliability outcomes are not known in advance may form part of core R&D activities.[12] In such scenarios, the usage cost of the API can be claimed as R&D expenditure, analogous to consumable materials used in experiments, provided there is documentation linking API calls, experiment plans, and evaluation results to registered activities.[7][12] By contrast, using Claude as a general writing assistant for blog posts or internal documentation would be a non-R&D overhead and ineligible.

AI assistant subscriptions (including tools like ElevenLabs or xAI/Grok if used as generic assistants) are more often used for productivity rather than experimentation. Where they are used to generate content, transcribe meetings, or automate routine coding tasks based on known patterns, without addressing technical uncertainty, the associated subscription costs do not have a nexus to core or supporting R&D.[12][3][13] However, if a subscription is procured specifically to run structured experiments—for example, systematically evaluating model hallucination rates in a novel domain, or testing custom audio generation techniques in ElevenLabs where no known method assures desired output—then part of the cost may be claimable as R&D expenditure.[12] The difficulty here is evidentiary: many AI tools blur experimental and routine usage, and the taxpayer must show, through logged prompts, experiment designs, and evaluation metrics, that usage was actually undertaken as experiment rather than casual assistance.

For the expense schedule, the classification of AI-related subscription lines should be conservative. Where invoices relate to AI APIs or tools clearly used in registered AI R&D activities, evidenced by experiment logs and model performance analysis, lines may be marked TRUE in whole or proportionally. Where usage was unstructured or primarily for general productivity, lines should be marked FALSE. Mixed usage may justify partial apportionment, but only if robust usage analytics or internal logs support a defensible percentage.

### Developer Tooling and Issue Tracking (Linear, Ref.tools, Tailscale, Supermemory)

Developer tooling and issue tracking platforms such as Linear, Ref.tools, Tailscale, and Supermemory are commonly used across all engineering work, spanning R&D experiments, routine development, bug fixes, and maintenance. As with other overheads, their eligibility under the R&DTI depends on whether the subscription costs are incurred on R&D activities and can be apportioned accordingly.[13][14][18] These tools rarely constitute core R&D activities themselves; rather, they are supporting tools that may contribute to coordinating experiments, tracking hypotheses, documenting results, or enabling secure access to experimental environments.

For instance, Linear tickets may document experimental tasks, hypotheses, spike investigations, and test results for registered R&D activities.[3][6][7] In such cases, the use of Linear clearly forms part of the supporting infrastructure for core R&D, and a portion of the subscription cost may be treated as supporting R&D expenditure. ClaimKit’s guidance for SaaS startups explicitly notes that contemporaneous engineering records, including experiment tickets in tools such as Jira or Linear, are strong evidence of R&D activities and can be used to separate workstreams with genuine technical unknowns from routine delivery.[6] Nevertheless, the same Linear workspace often contains both R&D and non-R&D tickets, including standard feature development and bug fixes using known methods, so apportionment based on ticket tagging or staff time is required.[13][14][6]

Tools like Tailscale, which provide secure networking and remote access, may be used to connect developers to experimental environments or sensitive datasets. When configured and used primarily for R&D purposes, such as secure access to prototype infrastructure, their subscription costs may qualify as supporting R&D expenditure directly related to core activities.[1][2][14] If Tailscale is instead used for general VPN access across the company, with no specific linkage to experiments, costs should be treated as non-R&D overhead. Similarly, Supermemory or other specialised developer productivity tools may support experimentation where they are used to manage research notes, code snippets, or knowledge bases tied to technical hypotheses, but routine usage for general documentation sits outside the claim unless a dominant purpose link to R&D can be shown.

In classifying these subscriptions, accountants should examine the proportion of each tool’s usage attributable to staff engaged in registered R&D activities and to experiment-related projects, using artefacts such as ticket tags, project boards, or access logs.[7][13][14][6] If a subscription is predominantly used by the R&D team on experiment-related work, the line can be marked TRUE, perhaps fully. If usage is diffuse across the organisation with no clear R&D focus, the line is likely FALSE. Mixed scenarios call for partial apportionment, with the explanation column noting the basis, such as “50% of Linear seats allocated to R&D engineers; claim restricted to that portion.”

### Source Control and Continuous Integration (GitHub and Similar)

Source control and CI platforms like GitHub, GitLab, or Bitbucket are foundational development tools. Their use spans core experiments, supporting R&D activities, routine feature development, maintenance, documentation, and deployment. Under the R&DTI, these tools are generally treated as overheads or directly incurred expenditure that are claimable only to the extent they support registered R&D activities.[13][14][18] They are not core R&D activities in themselves, but repositories and CI pipelines can be critical artefacts for demonstrating experimentation, branching strategies, and evaluation cycles.

For example, GitHub repositories may contain experimental branches where hypotheses are tested, including prototype implementations, experimental feature flags, and test harnesses. Pull requests may document alternative approaches, code reviews may discuss technical uncertainty, and CI jobs may run specific test suites designed to evaluate experimental hypotheses.[3][7][6] Where GitHub is used in this way, and R&D engineers rely on the platform to conduct and record experiments, a portion of its subscription cost can be attributed to supporting R&D activities. However, the same organisation account typically hosts production code, routine bug fixes, and operational scripts, which must be excluded.

Apportionment for source control subscriptions can be based on metrics such as number of R&D engineers versus total engineers, proportion of repositories flagged as R&D projects, or time spent on R&D branches.[13][14] ClaimKit’s emphasis on mapping tickets, commits, and experiments suggests that linking GitHub projects to registered R&D activities in AusIndustry filings can help substantiate the nexus.[6][7] For the expense schedule, if a GitHub subscription is mainly used by a small team whose work is largely experimental and registered as R&D, the line may be marked TRUE with an explanation referencing the proportion of R&D staff. If most repositories and CI pipelines relate to matured production systems with little experimental work, the line should be marked FALSE.

### App Store Developer Registration (Google Play Developer Account)

App store developer registrations, such as Google Play developer accounts, are typically procured to enable distribution of applications to end users. Their primary purpose is commercial: to allow publishing, updating, and managing apps on a platform, rather than to conduct experiments resolving technical uncertainty.[16][3] Under the R&DTI, activities and expenditure incurred solely or predominantly for product distribution, market access, or commercialisation are generally not eligible, particularly where they fall within excluded categories such as market research or activities associated with complying with platform rules.[1][3][13][16]

There can be limited circumstances where app store registration is used to facilitate field experiments that form part of core or supporting R&D activities—for example, deploying experimental builds to a controlled cohort of test users to evaluate performance of a novel algorithm in real-world conditions, where such data cannot be obtained otherwise.[11][16] Even in such cases, however, the developer account itself remains a commercial gateway rather than an intrinsic experimental tool, and its subscription cost is more likely to be characterised as an ineligible business expense, with only incremental costs of experimental deployment treated as supporting R&D expenditure.[13][14] AusIndustry’s emphasis on direct linkage and dominant purpose suggests that app store registration will rarely satisfy the dominant purpose test for supporting R&D when its overarching function is commercial distribution.[1][2][3]

Accordingly, for a typical small software company, Google Play developer registration fees should be classified as non-R&D expenditure and marked FALSE in the claim schedule. Any experimental use of the platform (such as A/B testing of algorithm changes) should be documented in activity descriptions and supported by other costs (such as specific test harness subscriptions) rather than relying on the general registration fee.

### Domain Registration and DNS Services

Domain registration and DNS services are classic examples of marketing and operational overhead. The primary purpose of registering a domain is to establish a public-facing identity and route traffic to web properties, which is generally associated with commercial website hosting, branding, and customer access.[13][14] These activities fall outside the core R&D definition, as they do not involve experiments designed to resolve technical uncertainty, and they are not usually directly related to core R&D activities in the sense required for supporting R&D.[1][11][13]

In some cases, domains may be registered to host experimental systems accessible to test users or to facilitate real-world testing of prototypes. Even then, the registration itself remains a necessary business step rather than a supporting activity undertaken for the dominant purpose of supporting experiments, particularly where the same domain is later used for production.[2][14] The R&DTI regime generally treats marketing and brand-related expenses as non-claimable, reflecting the broader principle that expenditure incurred regardless of R&D is ineligible.[13][14] Domain registration costs fall squarely within this category.

Therefore, domain registration lines in the expense schedule should almost always be marked FALSE, with explanations noting that they are marketing/operational overheads not directly related to registered R&D activities. Any server or environment costs associated with experimental deployments behind those domains may still be claimable if the nexus to R&D can be shown, but the domain fee itself is unlikely to be eligible.

### Email, Collaboration, and Productivity Tools (Google Workspace, Slack)

Email and collaboration tools such as Google Workspace and Slack are ubiquitous business infrastructure. They facilitate communication, document sharing, and coordination across all functions, including management, sales, support, and engineering. Under the R&DTI, these categories are treated as general overheads that may only be claimable where a direct link to R&D activities can be demonstrated and where the expenditure is incurred to support those activities.[13][14][7] Because their dominant purpose is often general administration rather than R&D support, they usually do not satisfy the dominant purpose test for supporting activities subject to exclusions.[1][2][3]

For example, Google Workspace subscriptions cover email, calendaring, documents, and storage. While R&D teams use these tools to draft experiment plans, share results, and record decisions, so do all other departments for non-R&D tasks. The ATO guidance notes that administrative costs and overheads may be incurred on R&D activities if there is a direct link, but warns that expenses without sufficient linkage, particularly those related to general company operations, are ineligible.[13] Similarly, expenditure guides typically treat generic overheads such as office software subscriptions as potentially claimable only in narrow circumstances where they can be clearly and predominantly linked to R&D work.[14] For a small software company, allocating a portion of Google Workspace costs to R&D might be theoretically possible based on seat counts or time usage, but evidentiary support is weak and review risk is high.

Slack presents a similar challenge. R&D engineers may use Slack channels to coordinate experiments, discuss technical hypotheses, and share logs, which in principle could be seen as supporting R&D activities. Yet Slack also hosts management, general chat, customer support, and operations discussions. Absent strong segregation of workspaces or channels—such as a separate Slack instance used exclusively by the R&D team—the nexus between subscription cost and R&D is diffuse. From a compliance perspective, many advisors recommend treating such generic collaboration tools as non-R&D overhead for R&DTI purposes, focusing claims instead on more clearly attributable technical tools.[14][18] In the expense schedule, lines for Google Workspace and Slack should ordinarily be marked FALSE, unless the company has exceptional evidence showing that a distinct subscription (for example, a Slack workspace restricted to research staff) was procured solely to support R&D.

### Training and Education Subscriptions

Training and education subscriptions, including online courses, technical content platforms, or professional development tools, are frequently used by engineers to upskill. Under the R&DTI, however, training and general education are not typically treated as R&D activities, and may fall within excluded categories such as management studies, efficiency surveys, or research in social sciences, arts, or humanities.[1][3][14] While learning new techniques or tools may indirectly enable R&D, the training activity itself is not an experiment conducted to resolve technical uncertainty; it is preparation, which the regime does not generally incentivise.

Expenditure guides note that operational costs such as certain training expenses may qualify where they are clearly linked to R&D activities, but this is more often the case for training in specific experimental protocols or compliance for regulated research than for general technical courses.[14] For software, training engineers in a new programming language or AI framework is better characterised as upskilling rather than R&D. Moreover, training costs would have been incurred regardless of whether experiments are conducted, which weakens the nexus to R&D.[13][14]

Accordingly, training or education subscription lines should typically be marked FALSE in the R&DTI claim schedule, with explanations referencing their nature as professional development and lack of direct experimental linkage. If a particular subscription is demonstrably tied to an experimental program—for example, access to specialised simulation software training that is integral to conducting core experiments—there may be a narrow case for partial inclusion as supporting R&D expenditure, but this would require careful documentation and is uncommon.

## Apportionment Methodologies for Mixed-Use Software Tools

### Legal Requirement to Apportion Eligible and Ineligible Use

The ATO’s guidance makes clear that taxpayers can only claim a notional deduction under the R&DTI to the extent that expenditure has been incurred on R&D activities.[13] This requirement applies equally to direct R&D costs and to operational or overhead expenses such as software subscriptions. When a cost supports both eligible R&D activities and ineligible non-R&D activities, the taxpayer must apportion the expenditure and claim only the eligible portion.[13] The guidance emphasises that taxpayers must be able to support their methodology for apportioning expenditure between eligible and non-eligible activities, and that records must show how each expense relates to registered R&D activities.[13][7]

Expenditure guides further underline the importance of accurate apportionment, noting that the ATO has placed increasing emphasis on contemporaneous records, particularly time records for staff, to substantiate claims.[14] While time-based apportionment is more straightforward for wages, similar principles can be applied to software subscriptions and cloud services. The key is to develop a reasonable and evidence-backed method that reflects actual usage of each tool across R&D and non-R&D work, and to apply it consistently year-on-year.[13][14][18]

### Practical Apportionment Methods for Software Subscriptions

Several practical methodologies can be used to apportion software and SaaS expenditure in a mixed-use environment. One common method is user-based apportionment, where the proportion of subscriptions attributable to staff engaged in registered R&D activities is used as a proxy for R&D usage. For example, if a GitHub organisation has 10 paid seats and 6 are used by R&D engineers whose time is predominantly on core or supporting R&D activities, one might initially attribute 60% of the subscription cost to R&D.[13][14][6] However, this approach should be refined if those engineers spend significant time on routine non-R&D tasks, which should be reflected in staff time records.

Another method is project-based apportionment. Tools such as Linear, GitHub, or cloud platforms often allow tagging or grouping by project. If certain projects are registered as R&D activities and others are not, subscription costs can be allocated based on the relative usage attributable to R&D projects. This can be evidenced by counts of tickets, commits, builds, or resource consumption associated with R&D projects versus non-R&D projects.[7][13][6] For cloud compute, environment-based segregation is particularly effective: billing data can show resource usage for specific R&D resource groups or accounts, enabling precise attribution of those costs to R&D, while production accounts remain outside the claim.[13][14][18]

Time-based apportionment is also possible, particularly where tools are used more intensely during specific experimental periods. If, for example, Sentry is configured primarily for experiment monitoring during a three-month R&D campaign and then repurposed for production monitoring thereafter, the subscription costs for that three-month window could be attributed to R&D, while later periods are excluded.[7][13] This requires documentation of configuration changes, project timelines, and experiment schedules. For AI APIs billed per usage, apportionment can be based directly on usage logs: calls tagged as part of experimental scripts can be treated as R&D, while other calls are ineligible.

In all cases, the methodology must be reasonable, supported by contemporaneous records, and consistently applied. It should be documented in internal working papers explaining the basis of percentages used in the claim schedule, including any assumptions. The ATO guidance warns against arbitrary or retrospective estimates not grounded in actual data.[13] Where tools serve predominantly non-R&D functions, it may be prudent not to claim them at all rather than stretching apportionment.

### Evidence Required to Substantiate Apportionment

To substantiate apportionment, accountants should assemble a dossier of evidence for each tool and subscription. For user-based apportionment, this includes organisation charts, role descriptions, and timesheets showing the proportion of each R&D engineer’s time spent on registered activities versus other work.[13][14] For project-based apportionment, project plans, registration documents, issue trackers, commit histories, and billing breakdowns by project or repository are critical.[3][6][7][13] For environment-based apportionment in cloud services, architecture diagrams, environment names, tagging strategies, and provider billing reports showing usage by tag or account are particularly persuasive.[14][18]

AusIndustry’s record-keeping guidance provides examples of records that demonstrate core and supporting R&D activities, including experimental plans, trial run records, test reports, contracts, meeting minutes, emails, project plans, and expenditure approval requests outlining the purpose of activities.[7] These can be cross-referenced with software tool usage—for instance, experiment plans may mention specific tools used, tickets may link to dashboards, and logs may show tool involvement. The ATO’s guidance on keeping records emphasises that records must be in English or translatable, kept for five years after the claim, and specify and explain all transactions relevant to tax liabilities.[13] This underscores the importance of retaining invoices, receipts, bank statements, and subscription contracts alongside technical artefacts.

For the 79-line expense schedule, each line should be accompanied by notes summarising the apportionment basis where applicable. For example, a GitHub line might note “60% of subscriptions allocated to R&D based on 6 of 10 seats used by R&D engineers; engineers’ timesheets show approx. 80% of time on registered R&D activities; claim restricted accordingly.” This allows an accountant, and later an auditor or reviewer, to understand and test the logic. Lines where no robust apportionment basis exists should be marked FALSE, avoiding speculative inclusion.

## Excluded Activities and Non-claimable Expenditure

### Statutory Exclusions from Core R&D Activities

Division 355 and AusIndustry guidance list activities that are excluded from being core R&D activities and can only be included as supporting R&D activities, if at all, when they meet dominant purpose and direct relation tests.[1][3] Relevant exclusions for software and SaaS operations include activities associated with complying with statutory requirements or standards, market research, management studies or efficiency surveys, research in social sciences, arts or humanities, and developing, modifying, or customising computer software for internal administration of business functions.[1][3][16] Activities related to tobacco and gambling are also excluded unless conducted solely for harm minimisation.[1]

In the software context, the explicit exclusion for internal administration software is particularly important. AusIndustry notes that developing or customising software for the dominant purpose of internal administration by the developer or related entities is not an eligible core R&D activity, although it may still be a supporting activity in limited cases.[3][16] ATO’s TA 2017/5 reiterates that software projects undertaken for the sole or dominant purpose of internal use by the company for administration of business functions are not eligible for the R&DTI.[16] This means that subscriptions for tools used to build internal HR systems, finance automation, or other back-office tooling will generally be outside the R&D claim, even where some technical sophistication is involved.

Other excluded categories restrain qualifying activities related to business analysis and strategy rather than technical experimentation. Market research, customer surveys, business model testing, pricing experiments, and management efficiency studies, even when conducted using sophisticated software platforms, fall under these exclusions and should not be treated as core R&D.[1][3] If they are undertaken in support of core technical experiments—for example, a survey assessing user perceptions of an AI model’s responses—they may be considered supporting R&D, but only if the dominant purpose is to support the technical experiment, not to optimise marketing.[2][13]

### Expenditure Categories Specifically Excluded from Notional Deductions

Apart from activity exclusions, certain expenditure types are specifically excluded from notional deductions under the R&DTI. Expenditure guides and ATO commentary identify categories such as interest expenses, non-risk expenditure where the business does not bear financial exposure, capital expenditure on depreciating assets, and building costs including construction, acquisition, or structural modifications.[14][13] Expenditure on further R&D activities undertaken for the purpose of acquiring technology or intellectual property is also excluded.[14] These exclusions typically do not affect recurring software subscriptions, which are operating expenses, but they illustrate the regime’s focus on direct experimentation costs rather than asset acquisition or financing.

Operational costs such as accounting and legal fees, advertising, bank charges, employee benefits, facility maintenance, director fees, donations, and entertainment are generally treated as non-claimable overheads.[14][13] For software companies, this means that subscriptions for marketing automation platforms, legal research tools, or general financial systems will be ineligible even if the business is heavily engaged in R&D. Similarly, expenditure on building or acquiring office space, lab facilities, or server hardware must be handled through depreciation and may not be claimed as R&D expenditure, though associated running costs might be partly claimable if tied to R&D activities.[14]

In the context of the 79-line claim, accountants should therefore exclude lines relating to clearly non-technical or commercial subscriptions—such as pure marketing platforms, CRM systems, HR or payroll software, or entertainment-related tools—from the R&DTI schedule, regardless of any incidental benefit to R&D teams. This ensures that the claim focuses on costs with a plausible experimental nexus and reduces audit risk.

### Software-Specific Risk Scenarios in TA 2017/5

TA 2017/5 provides detailed examples of software development activities that are not eligible R&D unless undertaken as experiments to test technical hypotheses.[16] These include bug testing, beta testing, system testing, requirements testing, user acceptance testing, data mapping and migration testing, and testing efficiency of algorithms that are already known to work.[16] The alert states that such activities are only core R&D when they are conducted as part of experiments, and may otherwise be eligible only as supporting activities if directly related to core experiments.[16] It cautions that testing websites in operation by measuring the number of hits is not eligible R&D unless done as part of an experiment with a technical hypothesis.

These examples are relevant to subscription expenditure because they illustrate the line between experimental and routine use of tools. For instance, a subscription to a load-testing service used to evaluate known algorithms’ efficiency in typical conditions is routine testing; but when used to compare novel algorithmic approaches under diverse workloads as part of hypotheses, it becomes experimental. Similarly, Sentry used primarily for bug testing in existing systems is operational, whereas Sentry used to evaluate behaviour of a fresh experimental build may be supporting R&D. ATO’s alert encourages companies to review registrations, ensure that only eligible experimental activities are registered, and adjust claims to exclude expenditure on ordinary business activities.[16]

From a compliance perspective, including subscription costs where tools are predominantly used for routine testing or post-production maintenance, without clear experimental framing, is a known review trigger. The alert suggests that companies with such arrangements may be contacted by the ATO or AusIndustry, prompting audits or adjustments.[16] This should inform conservative classification of subscription lines in the claim schedule.

## Record-Keeping and Substantiation Requirements

### AusIndustry Activity Records and Software-Specific Guidance

AusIndustry mandates that companies keep records of R&D activities and related expenditure, and that these records must be created at the time activities are conducted to ensure accuracy and comprehensiveness.[7] Record keeping is defined as a systematic process of collecting and maintaining information relating to R&D activities and associated expenditure.[7] Under the R&DTI, records must show background research demonstrating that outcomes could not be known in advance, details of activities performed (steps, trials, development work), methods and processes used, timelines, personnel, resources used (facilities, equipment, tools, materials), and related expenditure, including what it was spent on, how much, who incurred it, and when.[7]

Software-specific guidance elaborates on records that demonstrate eligibility. For core R&D activities, records should show evidence that the outcome could not be determined in advance and that experiments were necessary, such as emails or reports from experts indicating gaps in current knowledge, and background research identifying such gaps.[3][7] Evidence of systematic progression of work includes experimental plans, trial run records, test reports showing results and evaluation, and contracts or research agreements.[7][11] Evidence that activities were conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge can be found in meeting records, project plans, and expenditure approval requests outlining the purpose.[7][3]

For supporting R&D activities, records should show how the activity is directly related to core R&D, via meeting records, emails, and experimental plans, and where the dominant purpose test applies, evidence that the activity was conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting core R&D, such as project plans showing that the supporting activity is needed for conducting core R&D and expenditure approval documents describing that purpose.[7][2] These requirements apply equally to software tools: usage of subscriptions must be documented in the context of activities with experimental plans, not just general project summaries. For instance, experiment tickets in Linear, architecture decision records in GitHub, or performance graphs in monitoring tools can all serve as evidence.[3][7][6]

### ATO Requirements for Expenditure Records and Nexus

The ATO’s guidance on keeping records and calculating notional deductions complements AusIndustry’s activity focus with expenditure-specific requirements.[13] Taxpayers’ records must specify and explain all transactions relevant to tax liabilities, be made as soon as transactions occur or shortly thereafter, relate to all taxes for which they are liable, and support any election, choice, determination, or calculation under tax law.[13] For the R&DTI, records must be in English (or easily translated), kept for five years after the claim, and need not be sent to the ATO unless requested.[13]

To substantiate R&D claims, ATO expects records that show the amounts claimed relate to registered R&D activities, that the taxpayer received the major benefit from the activities, that expenditure has been correctly apportioned between eligible and ineligible activities, when amounts have been paid to associates, and how adjustments have been calculated for government recoupments or feedstock (marketable product) adjustments.[13] Types of records include project plans and design specifications, contracts with people conducting work, contracts with third parties for product development, progress reports, sales and purchase invoices, receipts, bank statements, diaries, expenditure journals, cash books, and R&D working papers.[13] For software subscriptions, invoices, payment records, and contracts must be retained to show cost, while technical documents link usage to R&D.

The ATO notes that expenses can be claimed as R&D expenditure only to the extent they are incurred on R&D activities, which underscores the need for records demonstrating nexus.[13] For example, if an Azure invoice covers multiple services, internal records must show which services and environments are R&D-related. Similarly, if a GitHub invoice covers numerous seats, records must show which seats belong to R&D staff. Without such linkage, the taxpayer cannot demonstrate eligibility and is not entitled to claim the R&D tax offset for those expenses.[13][7]

### Implementing Record-Keeping Systems for Software R&D

AusIndustry recommends setting up record-keeping systems before starting R&D activities to ensure consistent capture of information that meets R&DTI requirements.[7] For a small software company, this can involve configuring ticketing systems (such as Linear) to tag experimental tasks, using repositories (GitHub) to label R&D branches, maintaining design notes and decision records in documentation tools, and capturing logs and performance dashboards for test runs.[3][6][7] Monetary records should be linked to technical records—for example, associating cloud invoices with environment tags that correspond to experimental projects, or noting subscription IDs in project budgets.

Practically, accountants should work closely with engineering teams to map R&D activities to tools and subscriptions. This may involve establishing monthly documentation cadences, as suggested by ClaimKit, to ensure experiment tickets, commits, benchmarks, and cost mapping are kept contemporaneously rather than reconstructed at year end.[6] Clear procedures should be defined for marking activities as core or supporting R&D in project systems and for recording which tools are used in each activity. When preparing the 79-line expense schedule, accountants can then trace each line item back to these records, confirming whether the tool supported registered R&D activities and to what extent.

Failure to maintain adequate records is itself a compliance risk. AusIndustry notes that if a company cannot demonstrate eligibility of R&D activities and expenditure, it is not entitled to claim the R&D tax offset.[7] ATO and AusIndustry can review and make findings on eligibility, which are legally binding; if they find activities ineligible, the company must amend its tax return and may face penalties and interest.[17][16] Robust record-keeping is therefore essential not only for accurate classification of expense lines but also for protecting against adverse findings.

## Offset Rates, Thresholds, Caps, and Minimum Expenditure

### Minimum Expenditure Threshold and Expenditure Cap

Under the R&DTI, the minimum amount of eligible R&D expenditure required in an income year to access a tax offset is A$20,000.[1] This threshold ensures that only entities undertaking a meaningful level of R&D benefit from the incentive. However, the threshold does not apply where the company uses a registered research service provider to conduct its R&D or contributes to the Cooperative Research Centres Program.[1] For a small software company whose R&D is undertaken internally, the A$20,000 threshold is therefore relevant; the aggregated eligible portion of the 79-line software subscription claim must be considered alongside other R&D expenditure (such as wages) to confirm that the threshold is met.

The R&DTI also imposes a cap on the amount of R&D expenditure on which the enhanced tax offset can be claimed. The maximum amount of eligible R&D expenditure that can be claimed in a single income year is A$150 million.[1] Expenditure exceeding this cap receives a tax offset equal to the company’s normal tax rate, meaning no additional R&D benefit. While this cap is unlikely to affect a small software company with A$6,600 of subscription costs and modest wages, it is part of the broader framework and ensures that claims above the cap do not distort the incentive’s design.

### Refundable and Non-refundable Offset Rates and Turnover Threshold

From 1 July 2021, reforms to the R&DTI introduced an intensity-based framework and adjusted offset rates. For companies with aggregated turnover less than A$20 million, the incentive generally provides a refundable tax offset equal to the company tax rate plus an 18.5 percentage point premium on eligible R&D expenditure.[4] For base company tax rates of around 25%, this corresponds to an approximate refundable offset rate of 43.5% of eligible R&D expenditure. The refundable nature means that if the company is in tax loss, the offset can be paid as a cash refund, subject to caps and anti-avoidance rules.[4] This is particularly valuable for early-stage software startups.

For companies with aggregated turnover of A$20 million or more, the R&DTI provides a non-refundable tax offset at a rate equal to the company tax rate plus incremental premiums determined by R&D intensity tiers. Broadly, R&D expenditure up to 2% of total expenses attracts a premium of 8.5 percentage points above the company tax rate, while R&D expenditure above 2% attracts a premium of 16.5 percentage points.[4] The offset is non-refundable but can be carried forward. These details matter for larger software companies but may be less relevant to a small entity with turnover below the threshold.

These offset rates apply to eligible R&D expenditure including software subscriptions that meet nexus and apportionment tests. However, accountants must also consider the interaction with other tax settings, such as feedstock adjustments when R&D produces marketable products, and at-risk rules that disallow expenditure where the company does not bear financial risk.[8][13][14] For the purposes of the 79-line claim, the primary focus is ensuring that only genuinely eligible software costs contribute to the notional deduction amount; the applied offset rate then follows from the company’s turnover and tax rate.

## Overseas Expenditure, Overseas Findings, and Cloud Services

### Overseas Finding Requirement for R&D Activities Conducted Outside Australia

AusIndustry’s overseas finding guidance explains that an overseas finding is a legally binding decision on whether R&D activities conducted outside Australia are eligible for the R&DTI.[10] Companies must have a positive overseas finding to claim expenditure on overseas R&D activities, and must still show that the expenditure on these activities is eligible when claiming the offset with the ATO.[10] The guidance sets out four conditions that overseas activities must meet to be eligible: the overseas activity must itself be an R&D activity (core or supporting), there must be at least one core R&D activity conducted solely in Australia that cannot be completed without the overseas activity, the overseas activity cannot be conducted solely in Australia for specific reasons (such as lack of facilities, expertise, or required populations), and the expenditure on overseas activities must be less than expenditure on related Australian activities.[10]

The requirement that the overseas activity cannot be conducted solely in Australia is tightly framed; allowable reasons include needing access to facilities, expertise, or equipment not available in Australia, needing access to populations or geographical features not available locally, or avoiding contravention of biosecurity or quarantine laws.[10] Financial reasons, such as lower cost overseas, are explicitly insufficient. The application must provide evidence that reasonable efforts were made to obtain required resources within Australia, such as recruitment campaigns or expert statements.[10] Applications for overseas findings must be submitted before the end of the income year in which overseas R&D activities are conducted or planned, and late applications or extensions are not permitted.[10] Overseas findings remain valid for the duration of activities they cover but become invalid if activities materially change.[10][17]

### Treatment of Overseas-Supplied Cloud Services

A critical distinction in the context of software subscriptions is between overseas R&D activities and overseas-supplied services. Cloud providers such as AWS, Azure, Vercel, or Redis Cloud may host servers and infrastructure in data centres outside Australia, and invoices may be issued by overseas entities. However, the R&D activities themselves—in terms of experiments, coding, and analysis—are often conducted by staff located in Australia, using overseas infrastructure as a tool.[14][18] In such cases, the overseas cloud service is not itself an “overseas R&D activity” under the overseas finding framework; rather, it is an overhead or consumable used in Australian-conducted R&D activities.

AusIndustry’s overseas finding guidance focuses on where the activity is conducted, not where services or equipment are located.[10] A core R&D activity is conducted solely in Australia when every part of the systematic progression of work is carried out in Australia.[10] Using offshore servers for compute does not, by itself, mean that the R&D activity is conducted outside Australia; the experiments, hypothesis formulation, observation, and evaluation by Australian staff still occur domestically.[10] Therefore, paying overseas suppliers for cloud services used in Australian R&D typically does not trigger the overseas finding requirement. The expense can be claimed as R&D expenditure in Australia, subject to usual nexus and apportionment rules, without an overseas finding.[14][18]

However, if R&D activities themselves are outsourced to overseas staff or facilities—for example, an overseas lab runs experiments, collects data, and evaluates hypotheses independently—then those activities are overseas R&D and require an overseas finding for expenditure to be eligible.[10][14] In such cases, cloud services used by overseas teams may form part of overseas activities and must be considered within the overseas expenditure cap relative to Australian R&D. For most small Australian software companies using overseas-hosted cloud environments but conducting R&D domestically, the overseas finding requirement will not apply to software subscriptions, and classification in the expense schedule can treat overseas-supplied cloud costs like domestic ones.

### Apportionment and Documentation for Overseas Suppliers

Even though overseas-supplied cloud services may not require overseas findings, they still must meet nexus and apportionment tests. Accountants should ensure that invoices from overseas providers clearly identify services and periods, and that internal records link usage to R&D activities.[13][7] Additionally, foreign currency considerations and GST treatment must be handled correctly, as discussed below. Where overseas suppliers are research service providers conducting R&D work, overseas finding requirements and related documentation become more complex, but this is outside the scope of a claim focusing solely on software subscriptions.

## GST Treatment of R&D Expenditure

For GST-registered entities, the general tax principle is that deductible expenses are recorded net of any input tax credits, as GST paid on taxable supplies is recoverable through the GST system rather than via income tax deductions. Applying this principle to the R&DTI, eligible R&D expenditure is typically considered on a GST-exclusive basis for entities entitled to full input tax credits on their purchases. Software subscriptions and cloud services supplied by Australian GST-registered entities will often include GST, but if the company claims input tax credits, the net cost (exclusive of GST) is the amount that can be notionally deducted under Division 355.[13]

The ATO’s guidance on keeping records emphasises that records must relate to all taxes, including GST, and that taxpayers must be able to show transactions and calculations under tax law.[13] While the guidance does not explicitly state R&DTI amounts must be net of GST, consistent tax practice and commentary from advisors treat GST-exclusive amounts as the basis for R&D expenditure where input tax credits are available.[14] For overseas suppliers not charging GST, the full invoice amount forms part of R&D expenditure, subject to nexus and apportionment. If an entity is not registered for GST or cannot claim input tax credits (for example, in certain exempt sectors), it may include GST-inclusive amounts in its expenditure calculations.

From a practical perspective, when preparing the 79-line expense schedule, accountants should extract GST-exclusive amounts for subscriptions from Australian suppliers and use those figures in the R&DTI claim, assuming the company claims GST credits. For overseas suppliers, the gross amounts are used. Care should be taken to ensure consistent treatment across all R&D expenditure categories, and any differences from standard practice should be documented in working papers.

## Common Review and Audit Triggers for Software R&D Claims

### Project-Level Claims and Incorrect Activity Focus

One major audit trigger identified in TA 2017/5 is the practice of claiming the R&DTI on entire software development projects rather than specific experimental activities.[16] Companies that register broad “development of new software” projects and then claim all associated expenditure, including routine development, bug fixing, maintenance, and commercial deployment, are at high risk of review. The alert notes that eligibility under the R&DTI is based on specific R&D activities, and that there must be experiments conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge, with outcomes not known in advance and determined through systematic progression of work.[16] Claims that treat production work as R&D, or that fail to distinguish routine from experimental activities, are likely to be challenged.

This has direct implications for subscription expenditure. If a company claims all GitHub, AWS, and Slack costs as R&D simply because they are part of software development projects, without distinguishing experimental from routine usage, the ATO and AusIndustry may question both activity eligibility and expenditure nexus.[16][3][13] Reviewers may request evidence that experiments were conducted, hypotheses formulated, and results evaluated, and that tools were used predominantly in that context. Absent such evidence, they may find activities ineligible, issue binding findings, and require tax return amendments.[17][16]

### Internal Use Software and Routine Activities

Another audit trigger is software development for internal administration or internal use by the company or related entities, which is explicitly excluded from core R&D eligibility unless harm-minimisation is involved.[3][16] Claims that treat internal system development, ERP customisation, or simple automation of manual processes using readily available technologies as core R&D are likely to be scrutinised. TA 2017/5 warns against arrangements where companies use existing software technologies exactly as intended to replace manual processes, and then claim such work as R&D.[16] Subscription costs for tools used primarily in these internal projects, such as workflow platforms or internal dashboards, are at significant risk of disallowance.

Routine activities such as bug testing, system testing, and user acceptance testing are also flagged as non-eligible unless part of experiments.[16] Claims that include expenditure on tools used mainly for these routine tasks—such as standard testing frameworks or monitoring systems for stable production—are exposed. Reviewers may seek evidence that tests were designed to resolve specific technical hypotheses rather than to confirm that systems meet standard requirements. Without such evidence, expenditure on these tools should not be claimed.

### Inadequate Record-Keeping and Apportionment

Inadequate record-keeping is a pervasive audit trigger. AusIndustry and the ATO emphasise that if a company cannot demonstrate the eligibility of its R&D activities and expenditure through records, it is not entitled to claim the R&D tax offset.[7][13] Claims based on retrospective reconstructions, undocumented apportionment percentages, or high-level descriptions without underlying experiment plans and technical artefacts are vulnerable. For subscription expenditure, this manifests when invoices are claimed but there is no documentation to show how tools were used in R&D activities or how usage was split between R&D and non-R&D.

The ATO’s focus on contemporaneous records, especially in salary apportionment, suggests similar sensitivity for overheads.[14][13] Where a claim includes significant software subscription costs allocated to R&D based on rough estimates, reviewers may challenge the methodology and require more granular data. They may also request copies of project plans, tickets, commits, logs, and dashboards to verify experimental activity. In extreme cases, failure to provide adequate records can result in denial of the entire claim and penalties.

### Examples of Disallowed Software Subscription Claims

While public case law specifically addressing software subscription claims is limited, TA 2017/5 indicates that ATO and AusIndustry have reviewed arrangements where software development projects include substantial expenditure on tools but only some activities are eligible.[16] In these reviews, expenditure on non-eligible activities, including routine development and production support, has been disallowed. Expenditure guides also note that certain overhead categories, such as marketing-related tools, accounting systems, and general productivity platforms, are commonly excluded, reflecting ATO practice.[14][13]

Advisory commentary suggests that claims including broad categories of software subscriptions without detailed nexus analysis have faced pushback, with auditors requiring companies to strip out generic overheads and focus on specific technical tools clearly tied to experiments.[14][18] For the 79-line schedule, this means that lines representing generic tools—like Slack, Google Workspace, domain registration, and training subscriptions—are the most likely to be challenged if included and should be excluded unless exceptional evidence supports their classification as supporting R&D.

## Practical Application: Classifying Each Expense Line

For the concrete classification task, the legal team and accountants should proceed as follows. First, map each of the 79 subscription lines to a functional category, such as cloud compute, observability, database, AI API, developer tooling, source control, app store registration, domain, email/collaboration, or training. Second, for each line, identify the primary purpose and actual usage during the subscription period, drawing on technical records—environment tags, tickets, commits, logs, dashboards—and personnel records.[7][13][6] Third, determine whether that usage is directly related to registered core R&D activities and, where required, whether the dominant purpose of the activity supported by the subscription is to support those core activities.[1][2][3][11]

For lines clearly linked to experimental environments or tools used by R&D staff in hypothesis-driven work—such as AWS accounts hosting experimental clusters, Sentry projects monitoring experimental builds, AI API usage logs for model evaluation, or Linear subscriptions predominantly used for experiment tickets—the line can be marked TRUE, possibly with a partial percentage noted in working papers if apportionment is required. The explanation column should summarise the R&D nexus, referring to specific activities and records, such as “Azure subscription for R&D resource group ‘exp-graph-engine’; used exclusively for performance experiments described in core activity CA-1; logs and metrics retained.”

For lines where usage is predominantly operational, commercial, or internal administration—and for categories like app store registration, domain registration, general email and collaboration tools, and training subscriptions—the line should be marked FALSE, with explanations referencing their nature as general overheads, marketing, or internal-use tools not directly related to core or supporting R&D activities. Where usage is mixed and some proportion supports R&D (for example, GitHub, Linear, or cloud accounts with both R&D and production workloads), apportionment should be performed based on robust methodology, and only the eligible portion should be included in the R&DTI calculation, even though the claim schedule may mark the line TRUE with a note “partial” in internal documentation.

By grounding each classification in statutory definitions, guidance on nexus and dominant purpose, and documented usage, the company can prepare a defensible claim schedule that an accountant can review line-by-line and that stands up to potential ATO or AusIndustry scrutiny.

## Conclusion

The Australian R&D Tax Incentive provides substantial support to small software companies undertaking genuine experimental work, but it is tightly focused on activities and expenditure that directly contribute to generating new knowledge through hypothesis-driven experiments with unknown outcomes.[1][9][11][16] For software and SaaS operating expenses, eligibility turns on whether each subscription cost is incurred on core R&D activities or directly related supporting activities conducted for the dominant purpose of supporting those experiments.[1][2][3][13] Cloud compute, observability tools, managed databases, AI APIs, developer tooling, and source control can all be eligible expenditure where their usage is clearly tied to experimental environments and documented in technical records; at the same time, generic overheads such as app store registrations, domain fees, email and collaboration platforms, and training subscriptions will usually sit outside the R&DTI claim.

Correct classification of each of the 79 expense lines requires granular evidence: invoices, environment configurations, tickets, commits, logs, and staff time records must all be assembled to demonstrate nexus and support reasonable apportionment where tools serve both R&D and non-R&D functions.[7][13][14][6] AusIndustry and ATO guidance emphasise that inadequate record-keeping and over-broad claims, particularly those treating whole projects or generic overheads as R&D, are key audit triggers and can result in binding findings of ineligibility and required amendments.[16][17] By applying the statutory tests for core and supporting activities, respecting exclusions, and rigorously documenting how each subscription supports specific experimental work, a small Australian software company can leverage the R&DTI while maintaining compliance and reducing review risk.

Ultimately, the R&DTI is not a blanket subsidy for software development or cloud spending; it is a targeted incentive for experimental activities. Mapping subscription costs to those activities with precision and evidence is the essential step that turns a list of operating expenses into a defensible R&D claim schedule.
