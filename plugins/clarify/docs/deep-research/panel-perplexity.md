# Designing Decision Questions for AI Coding Agents: Evidence-Based Guidelines on When and How to Ask Users

Accurately deciding when an AI coding agent should interrupt its user to ask a clarifying or design question, and how that question should be structured, is fundamentally a problem of requirements elicitation, survey design, choice architecture, interruption management, plain-language communication, and conversational AI behavior.(High Confidence) This synthesis draws on empirical findings from software engineering, survey research, judgment and decision-making, human–computer interaction, readability studies, and recent work on large language models that learn to ask clarifying questions.[1][3][5][8][11][12][15][16][19] Together, these literatures support concrete design rules: ask only when ambiguity would materially change the code or its risk profile; batch at most a very small number of questions per interruption; limit options to a compact set with a clearly marked, reversible recommendation; keep wording short, plain, and single-purpose; and always provide a lightweight free-text field to capture edge cases and tacit expert knowledge.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][12][18][19] The documented failure modes of asking badly—ambiguous or double-barrelled questions, cognitive overload from too many or too complex choices, excessive or poorly timed interruptions, and jargon-heavy wording—systematically degrade accuracy, increase decision fatigue and satisficing, and, in software settings, lead to costly misimplementation of requirements.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][8][9][10][11][12][15][16]

## Executive Summary

(High Confidence) Empirical work on software requirements shows that vague or ambiguous requirements, especially those with unstated assumptions, routinely cause teams to build the wrong system, with defect correction costs rising sharply the later the misunderstanding is discovered, which strongly supports asking clarifying questions early whenever a user request admits multiple plausible implementations that differ materially in performance, safety, or effort.[1][2][7] Ambiguity here is operationally defined as any requirement sentence that supports two or more distinct readings that could drive different designs or tests, and case analyses such as the Ariane 5 Flight 501 failure show that reusing code under changed conditions without eliciting hidden assumptions can result in catastrophic failure.(High Confidence)[1] For an AI coding agent, this implies that clarifying questions are worth asking exactly when the agent detects competing interpretations of the user’s intent that would lead to substantially different code paths or risk profiles, and not otherwise, because each unnecessary interruption carries cognitive and temporal costs for the user.(High Confidence)[1][15][16]

(High Confidence) Survey methodology and cognitive psychology research, particularly Krosnick’s work on satisficing and studies of questionnaire length, demonstrate that longer, more demanding question sequences sharply increase the likelihood that respondents will give the first acceptable answer rather than the most accurate one, especially beyond roughly 10 minutes or 15–20 questions, and when questions are complex or poorly worded.[3][8][9] Experimental and applied work consistently finds that vague, double-barrelled, or jargon-laden questions reduce comprehension and data quality, while item-specific questions that directly ask about the construct of interest with tailored response scales produce more reliable and valid answers than generic agree–disagree formats.(High Confidence)[3][4][8][9][10] For an AI coding agent, this supports a design in which each interruption contains at most one to three concise, single-focus questions, each phrased in plain, item-specific language, with surveys of coding preferences kept far below the thresholds where satisficing becomes typical.(High Confidence)[3][8][9][10]

(High Confidence) Judgment and decision-making studies of choice overload and default effects show that large option sets can sometimes demotivate choice or reduce satisfaction, but meta-analytic evidence indicates that overload effects are context-dependent and often small on average, with stronger negative effects when choices are complex, consequences are significant, and users lack clear prior preferences.[6][11][12] At the same time, default options and recommended choices exert very powerful influence on behavior, as demonstrated in organ donation studies where opt-out policies dramatically increase participation rates, partly by changing the perceived meaning of the decision.[11][12][13][14] For an AI coding agent, these findings suggest offering a compact menu of around three to five clearly differentiated options plus a “custom/other” path, leading with a recommended default that is visibly marked as such and easily reversible, which improves speed and reduces cognitive load but must be calibrated carefully to avoid nudging users into technically suboptimal or misaligned choices.(High Confidence)[6][11][12][13][14]

(High Confidence) Human–computer interaction research on interruption costs, especially studies of resumption lag in task-switching, has demonstrated that interruptions substantially slow task resumption, with lag times roughly doubling typical inter-action intervals, and that the cognitive overhead of context switching accumulates with each interruption.[15][16] Experiments show that external cues that preserve task context can mitigate resumption lag, but task switching still incurs both immediate resumption costs and ongoing performance decrements; more recent work documents n − 1 and n − 2 switch costs reflecting both inhibition and persistent activation of task goals.[15][16] Translating this to AI coding agents implies that the agent should minimize the frequency of interaction-breaking question episodes, preferably batching related clarifying questions into a single short interruption when they genuinely must be asked, while avoiding serial prompting that repeatedly pulls the user out of their workflow for minor or low-impact decisions.(High Confidence)[15][16]

(High Confidence) Readability and plain-language studies, including large-scale analyses of health research summaries, show that most expert-written texts significantly exceed the reading level of general audiences, with Flesch Reading Ease scores averaging around 39 and almost no texts meeting typical “plain English” thresholds, and that higher jargon and complexity correlate with lower accessibility and comprehension.[5][17][20] Government and civic-design guidelines converge on similar principles: write for the audience, use simple and consistent words, keep sentences short with one idea each, emphasize active voice, and test drafts with representative users.[17][20] For AI coding agents, this supports a target of one or two short sentences per decision question—roughly 15–30 words—written in plain, audience-appropriate language that names the decision directly, avoids technical jargon unless the user is known to be an expert in that subdomain, and is explicitly validated through user testing.(High Confidence)[3][5][17][20]

(High Confidence) Recent work on conversational AI and clarifying questions shows that current state-of-the-art language models tend to assume an interpretation rather than asking for clarification, and that training them with “double-turn” preference data—evaluating answers based on simulated future turns where clarification leads to tailored responses—improves multi-interpretation question answering F1 scores by about 4–5 percentage points, indicating that targeted clarifying questions can measurably improve alignment with user intent.[19] These systems can also be trained to decide whether clarification is needed, which is crucial to avoid over-asking that frustrates users and under-asking that yields incorrect or misaligned code.(High Confidence)[19] For an AI coding agent, the strongest evidence thus favors a decision rule that triggers questions when the expected improvement in correctness or safety from resolving ambiguity outweighs the measured interruption and decision-fatigue costs, with question content optimized using the survey and choice-architecture principles above.(High Confidence)[1][3][6][15][16][19]

(High Confidence) Across these literatures, the most robust design recommendation is that every clarifying question should map to a specific, high-impact decision the agent cannot safely or efficiently take on its own, offer a small and well-structured set of options anchored by a clearly marked default, allow a free-text note to capture nuances or preferences not in the options, and be asked only when the agent detects consequential ambiguity whose resolution changes the implementation path or risk profile in a way that matters to the user.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][12][15][16][18][19] Failure modes—ambiguous wording, too many or poorly structured options, excessive interruptions, lack of a free-text escape hatch, and recommendations that over-nudge—are consistently associated with lower accuracy, increased rework or defects, higher respondent fatigue, and in some settings, ethically problematic manipulation, which provides a clear negative template for what an AI coding agent must avoid.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15][16][18][19]

#### 1.1 Requirements-elicitation research in software engineering: ambiguity, unstated assumptions, and the cost of building the wrong thing

Research in requirements engineering has long emphasized that ambiguous and incomplete requirements are a leading cause of software defects, cost overruns, and project failure, because they allow divergent interpretations that propagate into design, implementation, and testing artifacts.(High Confidence)[1][7] A requirement is typically defined as ambiguous when different readers—designers, developers, testers, auditors—can reasonably interpret it in more than one way, such that it does not uniquely constrain the system’s behavior.(High Confidence)[1] The Jama Software analysis, though written as a practitioner blog and thus a [SECONDARY: promotional] source, accurately reflects consensus in academic requirements engineering: it describes how a requirement like “The system shall respond quickly” led hardware engineers to design for a 500-millisecond response while software engineers targeted 50 milliseconds, with the mismatch only discovered at integration, resulting in expensive rework that could have been avoided by asking a single clarifying question about the required response time.(High Confidence)[1] This example encapsulates the core failure mode of ambiguity: the writer’s intended meaning is not reliably transmitted to downstream readers, so different components are built to different standards, breaking system integration and traceability between requirements and tests.(High Confidence)[1]

The economic cost of such misunderstandings has been quantified in various industry studies, including the Standish Group’s Chaos Report, which, while not a peer-reviewed study and thus not fully meeting the strictest source-discipline criteria, is widely cited for its aggregate statistics on project outcomes.(Medium Confidence)[7] The Chaos Report documents that average development project costs were on the order of \$2.3 million for large companies, \$1.3 million for medium companies, and \$434,000 for small companies, and that incomplete or ambiguous requirements contributed substantially to project impairment, overruns, and outright failure.(Medium Confidence)[7] More formal academic work, as summarized in requirements engineering textbooks and conference proceedings, converges on the finding that requirements-phase defects are orders of magnitude cheaper to fix than those discovered later, because early clarification usually requires only revising text and associated models, whereas late-stage corrections entail redesign, code changes, retesting, and sometimes regulatory re-approval.(High Confidence)[1] The Jama analysis notes explicitly that a defect discovered while writing or reviewing requirements may be resolved by asking clarifying questions, but the same defect found during testing or operation can necessitate redesign and reimplementation, illustrating a steep defect-cost curve with increasing project phase.(High Confidence)[1]

A particularly instructive case is the Ariane 5 Flight 501 failure, which postmortem analyses traced in part to specification and design errors involving the reuse of inertial reference software from Ariane 4 without properly eliciting and documenting the assumptions embedded in that software about the rocket’s acceleration profile.(High Confidence)[1] The reused module attempted to convert a 64-bit floating-point value to a 16-bit integer within the context of higher Ariane 5 acceleration, causing an arithmetic overflow that the error-handling logic was not designed to manage in that flight phase, leading to cascaded failures and loss of the vehicle.(High Confidence)[1] This incident underscores that unstated assumptions—about operating conditions, environmental parameters, or user behaviors—that remain implicit in code or design artifacts can become lethal when the context changes, and that such assumptions must be surfaced and interrogated through explicit elicitation, often via targeted questions to domain experts.(High Confidence)[1] Requirements engineering practices such as structured interviews and clarification sessions are specifically designed to expose these assumptions, with guidelines recommending prepared question sets that probe for environmental constraints, performance boundaries, and exception scenarios, followed by formal review and sign-off to confirm shared understanding among systems, test, and regulatory teams.(High Confidence)[1][2]

Importantly, requirements engineering also provides tools for detecting ambiguity and incompleteness, such as controlled glossaries to reduce lexical ambiguity and bidirectional traceability matrices that map high-level user needs to system requirements, design components, and test cases.(High Confidence)[1] When traceability is broken—e.g., a parent requirement has no child requirements, or a test case has no corresponding requirement—these patterns often reveal hidden ambiguity or missing requirements, prompting re-elicitation and clarification.(High Confidence)[1] Although these tools are applied primarily in large system-development projects, the underlying principle carries directly to AI coding agents: if the agent cannot cleanly map a user instruction to a specific behavior and verification predicate, it should treat this as a requirements ambiguity and ask a clarifying question rather than silently choosing an interpretation.(High Confidence)[1]

From these software-engineering findings, a concrete rule emerges for when an AI coding agent should interrupt to ask a question.<INFERENCE from="[Ambiguity definition and cost from Jama analysis[1]; defect phase-cost curve and Chaos Report data[7]; Ariane 5 case linking unstated assumptions to catastrophic failure[1]]"> The agent should ask a clarifying question when, and only when, it detects that the user’s instruction or the current requirement can be interpreted in at least two materially different ways that would lead to significantly different implementations, performance characteristics, safety profiles, or downstream maintenance costs, and when the agent lacks sufficient domain knowledge or prior preferences to choose among them reliably.(High Confidence)[1][7] In such cases, the expected value of information from eliciting the user’s true intent is high relative to the cognitive and temporal cost of interruption, because avoiding a misimplementation that may require extensive rework or cause failure is more valuable than preserving short-term flow.(High Confidence)[1][7][15][16] Conversely, when ambiguity is superficial or the different plausible interpretations are functionally equivalent from the user’s perspective, the agent should default to its best guess and avoid asking, thereby minimizing unnecessary interruptions that contribute to decision fatigue without substantial benefit.<INFERENCE from="[Defect-cost asymmetry[1][7]; interruption-cost literature[15][16]]">(Medium Confidence)

#### 1.2 Survey and questionnaire methodology: wording, length, satisficing, response-order effects, and double-barrelled questions

Survey research provides a rich empirical basis for understanding how people process questions and response options, and how design choices affect accuracy, speed, and fatigue.(High Confidence)[3][8][9][10] Jon Krosnick’s seminal 1991 paper on response strategies in attitude surveys, published in the peer-reviewed journal *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, proposes the concept of **satisficing**, defined as respondents giving a satisfactory but not optimal answer when fully optimizing would require substantial cognitive effort.(High Confidence)[8] Krosnick argues, with supporting experimental and theoretical analysis, that when question-answering is demanding—due to complex wording, difficulty of retrieval, or extensive judgment requirements—respondents may shortcut the process by retrieving incomplete or biased information, or by engaging in no retrieval or integration at all, instead choosing the first reasonable option, agreeing by default, or straightlining across items.(High Confidence)[8] The likelihood of satisficing is modeled as a function of three factors: task difficulty, respondent ability, and respondent motivation, with higher difficulty, lower ability, and lower motivation all increasing satisficing probability.(High Confidence)[8]

Later empirical work and practice-focused guides, such as the Formbricks article summarizing Krosnick’s findings and other survey design research, extend these ideas with concrete thresholds, noting that surveys longer than about 10 minutes almost certainly produce satisficed answers in their latter half, and recommending limits of under 15 questions for typical engagement surveys and up to 20 questions for academic research, with completion times targeted below 10 minutes whenever possible.(High Confidence)[3][9] Burchell and Marsh’s 1992 study on questionnaire length, published in *Quality & Quantity*, analyzes data from a lengthy British survey and finds that substantial length negatively affects response rates and increases nonresponse, although they did not observe a systematic effect on item quality for open-ended questions as measured by number of responses, which they interpret as respondents gaining greater power to manage the burden as the survey proceeds.(Medium Confidence)[9] Nonetheless, survey textbooks and reviews consistently recommend avoiding long questionnaires due to their negative effects on both response rates and the quality of structured responses, especially in the presence of cognitive load and low motivation.(High Confidence)[3][8][9]

Question wording and structure are equally critical to data quality. Double-barrelled questions—those that ask about more than one distinct construct in a single question, or introduce a second construct through the response options—are well documented to confuse respondents and produce uninterpretable answers, because it becomes impossible to disentangle which construct the response refers to.(High Confidence)[4] For example, asking “How satisfied are you with the speed and reliability of the system?” forces the respondent to collapse potentially divergent attitudes toward speed and reliability into a single rating, which may mask dissatisfaction with one dimension.(High Confidence)[4] Methodological encyclopedias and survey design guides uniformly advise against double-barrelled items and recommend splitting them into separate questions so that each covers a single construct.(High Confidence)[3][4]

Agree–disagree (Likert-type) scales introduce further complications. A recent methodological review of agree–disagree versus item-specific questions, published in medical and social sciences contexts, concludes that item-specific questions—those that directly ask about the underlying dimension using tailored response categories, such as “How satisfied are you? Not at all…extremely”—are generally associated with better data quality outcomes than generic agree–disagree formats.(High Confidence)[10] Across experimental comparisons, item-specific questions tend to show higher validity and reliability, while agree–disagree items are more susceptible to undesirable response behaviors such as acquiescence (tendency to agree regardless of content), primacy and recency effects, extreme responding, straightlining, item nonresponse, and speeding or break-offs in online surveys, though some studies report mixed or no differences.(Medium Confidence)[10] Questionnaire designers argue that agree–disagree formats are more cognitively burdensome because they often mismatch the offered response dimension (agreement) with the underlying construct (e.g., satisfaction, frequency), forcing respondents to mentally remap the question, and this complexity, coupled with conversational norms that favor agreement, exacerbates acquiescence, especially among respondents with lower education or motivation.(High Confidence)[10]

Order effects also play a role. Studies conducted by organizations such as the Pew Research Center, as summarized in practice guides, show that asking specific dissatisfaction questions before a general satisfaction question can shift measured overall satisfaction ratings by around 10 percentage points, highlighting how question order frames the respondent’s mindset and influences judgment.(Medium Confidence)[3] To mitigate such order effects, survey methodologists recommend using funnel designs that begin with broad, easy, and engaging questions, then move to more specific and demanding ones, and placing sensitive questions at the end.(High Confidence)[3] Krosnick’s model aligns with this: starting with simpler tasks maintains motivation and reduces early fatigue, while careful ordering prevents earlier items from anchoring subsequent responses or priming negativity.(High Confidence)[8]

An AI coding agent’s decision questions fit squarely within this survey-methodology framework, albeit typically as single or very small sets rather than long instruments.<INFERENCE from="[Satisficing and length effects[3][8][9]; double-barrelled and agree–disagree findings[4][10]]"> The evidence strongly supports designing each question as item-specific rather than agree–disagree, phrased in plain language that directly names the coding decision: for example, “How should error handling be implemented for this API call?” followed by options like “Throw an exception,” “Return a status code,” “Log and continue,” and “Other (please describe).”(High Confidence)[3][4][10] Each question should address a single construct—e.g., error handling strategy, performance target, security posture—rather than bundling multiple decisions such as “speed and security,” to avoid double-barrelled confusion.(High Confidence)[4][10] Given the risk of satisficing for longer sequences, the agent should limit the number of questions per interruption episode to a small batch, ideally one to three, especially when users are already cognitively engaged in coding tasks.<INFERENCE from="[Survey length thresholds[3][9]; cognitive load factors in satisficing[8]]">(High Confidence)[3][8][9]

Moreover, the agent should avoid agree–disagree frames entirely for design decisions—for instance, not asking “I prefer strong input validation: strongly agree…strongly disagree”—because this format unnecessarily burdens respondents and invites acquiescence, which could bias configuration toward more aggressive or more lax validation than the user actually intends.(High Confidence)[8][10] Instead, item-specific formats that map directly onto code-level choices are preferable, such as “How strict should input validation be?” with response categories tailored to coding practice (“Basic type checks only,” “Type and range checks,” “Full schema validation,” “Custom”).(High Confidence)[10] In addition, the agent should consider question order within the small batch: beginning with broad but concrete questions about major architectural choices, then moving to more specific parameter decisions, and placing any potentially sensitive or complex items last, following funnel-method guidelines, to maintain engagement and reduce framing bias.<INFERENCE from="[Funnel design and order effects[3]; cognitive modeling of question difficulty[8]]">(Medium Confidence)[3][8]

Failure modes documented in survey research—satisficing, acquiescence, straightlining, order effects, and confusion from double-barrelled or jargon-heavy questions—translate directly into failure modes for AI coding agent interactions.(High Confidence)[3][4][8][9][10] If the agent presents too many questions in one batch, uses complex or mixed constructs in a single item, or writes in dense, technical language, users may respond hastily, selecting defaults or first options without careful consideration, or may abandon the interaction, leaving the agent to proceed with incomplete or biased information.(High Confidence)[3][8][9][10] Thus, survey methodology strongly supports keeping clarifying interactions short, focused, and tailored in language and response structure, which for an AI coding agent translates into: ask fewer, better questions, and design each one to be as cognitively easy and unambiguous as possible.(High Confidence)[3][4][8][9][10]

#### 1.3 Choice architecture: optimal number of options, defaults, recommendations, and free-text escape hatches

Judgment and decision-making research on choice architecture examines how the presentation of options—number, framing, ordering, and defaults—affects decision quality, speed, and satisfaction.(High Confidence)[6][11][12][13][14] The concept of **choice overload**, also known as overchoice or the paradox of choice, posits that offering too many options can overwhelm decision-makers, leading to paralysis, avoidance, or reduced satisfaction with chosen options.(High Confidence)[6][11][12] Classic experiments by Iyengar and Lepper, published in *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, famously demonstrated that shoppers presented with a display of 24 jam varieties were more likely to stop and sample but much less likely to purchase than those offered only 6 options, suggesting that extensive choice can demotivate actual choice behavior.(High Confidence)[11] In those studies, the condition with more than 20 options saw a significantly higher proportion of non-choice; the Decision Lab summary notes that offering more than 22 options increased the likelihood that shoppers made no choice at all, illustrating a threshold beyond which option sets become counterproductive for action.(Medium Confidence)[6][11]

However, meta-analytic work by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, and Todd, published in *Journal of Consumer Research* in 2010, complicates the picture.(High Confidence)[12] Their analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments, totaling over 5,000 participants, found a mean effect size of virtually zero for the choice overload hypothesis overall, but with considerable variance across studies.(High Confidence)[12] This suggests that while strong choice overload effects exist in some contexts, they are not ubiquitous; instead, overload seems contingent on factors such as decision complexity, difficulty of comparing options, lack of clear preferences, and the importance of the decision.(High Confidence)[12] The meta-analysis reviews idiosyncratic moderators proposed in individual studies but concludes that no single set of conditions reliably predicts overload, underscoring that context matters greatly.(Medium Confidence)[12]

Defaults and recommendations have clearer and more consistently large effects. Johnson and Goldstein’s influential work on organ donation, summarized in later analyses by Davidai and colleagues, shows that countries with opt-out default policies (where individuals are presumed donors unless they actively opt out) have dramatically higher participation rates than opt-in countries, where individuals must actively register as donors.(High Confidence)[11][13] Davidai’s 2012 *PNAS* article, which is a peer-reviewed experimental study, adds nuance by demonstrating that default policies influence not only participation rates but also the meaning that individuals attach to the act of donating.(High Confidence)[13] In experiments, American participants judged agreeing to be an organ donor as a significantly more substantial action under opt-in conditions than under opt-out conditions, and viewed non-agreement under opt-out conditions as a greater abrogation of responsibility than under opt-in conditions.(High Confidence)[13] Multidimensional scaling analyses showed that organ donation was perceived as more akin to large charitable bequests under opt-in and more akin to everyday prosocial acts under opt-out, indicating that defaults change the perceived moral weight of the decision.(High Confidence)[13]

These findings illustrate two mechanisms of default effects: behavioral inertia and implied norms.(High Confidence)[11][13] Defaults impose physical, cognitive, and emotional costs on those who must change their status, so many willing participants remain in the default state simply because switching requires effort, and the default itself may signal what is socially expected or approved.(High Confidence)[11][13] In applied choice architecture—for example, marketing settings where recommended pricing tiers are pre-selected—defaults steer behavior by appearing reasonable, reversible, and pre-vetted by experts, as noted in practice-oriented analyses such as the iMotions blog on choice architecture, which, while [SECONDARY: promotional], aligns with the peer-reviewed literature by emphasizing framing, ordering, and simplification as key design levers.(Medium Confidence)[14]

For an AI coding agent, the number of options per decision question should be chosen to balance expressivity with cognitive tractability.<INFERENCE from="[Choice overload meta-analysis[12]; jam experiments[11]; decision-lab summary of non-choice beyond ~22 options[6]]"> Although the meta-analysis suggests no universal overload threshold, it is clear that large option sets (dozens of choices) are inappropriate for fast, in-context coding decisions, particularly when users are simultaneously managing complex mental models of code behavior.(High Confidence)[6][11][12] The safest evidence-based recommendation is to present a compact set of three to five mutually exclusive, clearly labeled options that span the most common and distinct implementation strategies, supplemented by an “Other” or “Custom” option when necessary to cover unanticipated or expert-specific preferences.(High Confidence)[6][11][12] Offering only two options may sometimes oversimplify and force false dichotomies, but offering more than seven risks cognitive overload, slower decision-making, and increased defaulting or non-response in contexts where users lack strong pre-existing preferences.<INFERENCE from="[Consumer choice and survey scale design heuristics[3][6][12]]">(Medium Confidence)[3][6][12]

Regarding defaults and recommendations, the evidence indicates that presenting a recommended option first and pre-selecting it as the default can substantially increase its selection probability and reduce decision time, but also risks biasing choices away from the user’s true preferences or needs if the recommendation is misaligned.(High Confidence)[11][13][14] In many cases, especially for novice users, a well-chosen default may improve overall decision quality by leveraging expert knowledge to avoid common pitfalls—for example, defaulting to secure password storage or robust error handling.(High Confidence)[13][14] However, the Davidai findings warn that defaults also change the perceived meaning of choosing alternatives, potentially discouraging deviations from the recommended path that might be appropriate for advanced users or unusual contexts.(High Confidence)[13] For AI coding agents, the best practice is therefore to offer a recommended option that is visually marked as “recommended” or “default,” but to make this status explainable and easily overridable, with all options presented clearly and without hiding non-default choices behind extra clicks or effort.<INFERENCE from="[Default effects on meaning and effort[11][13]; ethical guidelines in choice architecture[14]]">(Medium Confidence)[11][13][14]

The question of whether to invite free-text input alongside structured options is addressed indirectly by research on open-ended survey responses and text analytics.(High Confidence)[18] A study of web-based textual analysis of free-text patient experience comments, published in a peer-reviewed medical informatics context, analyzed 3,426 free-text responses from 7,721 patients (a 44.4% comment rate) in a primary care setting and used text processing tools to identify word usage patterns associated with quantitative experience ratings.(High Confidence)[18] The analysis found that words such as “excellent” were significantly associated with higher quantitative satisfaction scores (odds ratio ~1.96, 95% CI 1.63–2.34), while words like “rude” were associated with lower scores (odds ratio ~0.53, 95% CI 0.46–0.60), demonstrating that free-text comments carry additional, interpretable information about patient experiences beyond structured ratings.(High Confidence)[18] The authors conclude that analyzing free text can provide deeper or new insight, identify areas for action, and initiate further investigation, suggesting that free-text fields are valuable for capturing nuances and emergent themes not anticipated by survey designers.(High Confidence)[18]

Applied to AI coding agents, this supports always including a small, optional free-text note field adjacent to the chosen option, allowing users to qualify their choice, explain edge-case requirements, or specify constraints not represented in the menu.<INFERENCE from="[Free-text association with quantitative ratings[18]; survey design practices encouraging comment boxes for nuance[3]]">(High Confidence)[3][18] For example, a user might select “Throw an exception” as the error-handling strategy but add “only for user-facing API calls; internal utilities should log and continue,” which the agent can parse to adjust implementation accordingly.(High Confidence)[18] The key is to keep the free-text field optional and cognitively light—short prompt, no obligation—so that users who have nothing extra to add are not burdened, while those with important clarifications can provide them.(High Confidence)[3][18] Failure modes here include omitting free-text entirely, thereby forcing experts to shoehorn complex preferences into simple options, or demanding extensive written justification, which increases burden and may suppress valuable qualitative input.(Medium Confidence)[3][18]

#### 1.4 Human–computer interaction research on interruption cost: batching versus serial prompting and attention-switching

Human–computer interaction (HCI) studies provide direct evidence on the costs of task interruption and context switching, which are central to deciding when and how often an AI coding agent should ask questions.(High Confidence)[15][16] Altmann’s work on task interruption and resumption lag, presented at CogSci and widely cited, examines higher-level, ecologically valid tasks where interruptions require substantial cognitive reorientation rather than simple reaction-time switches.(High Confidence)[16] In Altmann’s experimental environment, participants performed a highly interactive primary task with frequent actions, and were periodically interrupted by a secondary task; the key measure was the **resumption lag**, defined as the time interval between the last response in the secondary task and the first subsequent action in the primary task.(High Confidence)[16] The study found that resumption lag averaged about 3.8 seconds, roughly double the mean inter-action interval of 1.9 seconds in the uninterrupted primary task, indicating a substantial disruptive effect both in absolute and relative terms.(High Confidence)[16] This doubling suggests that each interruption incurs a fixed overhead beyond the normal pace of action, reflecting time needed to recall task goals, reconstruct context, and re-engage with the interface.(High Confidence)[16]

Altmann further showed that external cues associated with the interrupted task, available immediately before or after the interruption, can reduce resumption lag by providing anchors for memory and attention.(High Confidence)[16] For example, visual markers or textual reminders of the last action help participants more quickly “pick up the thread” after the secondary task is completed.(High Confidence)[16] This aligns with broader HCI principles advocating for state cues and history displays to support resumption after interruptions, and provides a path for mitigating some of the cost of clarifying questions: if the AI coding agent can preserve and highlight the code location, prior context, and pending action, resumption will be faster than if the user must reconstruct everything from scratch.(High Confidence)[15][16]

More recent work by Hirsch and colleagues, published in 2023 and drawing on task-switching paradigms, examines interruptions in multi-subtask procedural tasks and documents **resumption costs** and n − 1 and n − 2 switch costs.(High Confidence)[15] In two experiments with different degrees of response-set overlap between the interruption task and primary subtasks, they observed that switching back from the interruption to the primary task resulted in worse performance in subtasks immediately following interruptions, with significant n − 1 switch costs relative to repetition trials, reflecting the effort required to overcome inhibition of the previous task set.(High Confidence)[15] They also found n − 2 switch costs, indicating that the last subtask before interruption remained activated during the secondary task and exerted residual positive priming when resuming, suggesting a complex interplay of inhibition and activation that shapes performance after interruptions.(High Confidence)[15] These findings underscore that interruptions not only impose a resumption lag but also affect subsequent performance quality, as cognitive control systems reconfigure task sets and reconcile old and new goals.(High Confidence)[15]

From these HCI studies, several implications for AI coding agents emerge.<INFERENCE from="[Altmann resumption lag data[16]; Hirsch switch-cost findings[15]]"> First, each interruption—the moment when the agent asks the user a question—carries a fixed cost in terms of resumption lag and performance disruption, even if the question itself is short.(High Confidence)[15][16] This cost accumulates with frequency; multiple small interruptions may collectively be more disruptive than a single, slightly longer one, because each requires re-orienting to the code context and reactivating task goals.(High Confidence)[15][16] Second, providing strong contextual cues—such as highlighting the relevant code block, showing the last user action, and visually marking the decision that the question addresses—can mitigate resumption lag, helping the user more quickly return to their coding workflow after answering.(High Confidence)[16] Third, interruptions that involve high response-set overlap or complex secondary tasks may especially degrade performance in subsequent coding subtasks, suggesting that clarifying questions should be as cognitively simple as possible.(High Confidence)[15]

Thus, the evidence supports a design in which an AI coding agent minimizes the number of interruption episodes and instead, when necessary, **batches** related questions into a single short dialog, rather than serially prompting with isolated questions interleaved throughout the coding session.<INFERENCE from="[Fixed resumption lag per interruption[16]; cumulative disruption effects[15]]">(High Confidence)[15][16] For example, if the agent detects three related ambiguities about error handling, logging, and retry policy in a new API client, it is better to ask these in one small, well-structured interaction early in the design, rather than interrupting three separate times as the user progresses.(High Confidence)[15][16] However, this must be balanced against survey-method evidence on satisficing and cognitive load: batching too many questions together can create a mini-survey that induces fatigue and lower-quality responses.(High Confidence)[3][8][9] The optimal compromise, grounded in both HCI and survey data, is likely one to three questions per interruption, with a strong preference for one or two unless decisions are tightly coupled and easily answered together.<INFERENCE from="[Survey question-count recommendations[3][9]; interruption overhead per episode[16]]">(Medium Confidence)[3][9][15][16]

Failure modes in interruption design include asking too many questions in too many separate episodes, leading to fragmented attention and slowed coding; asking questions that require extensive cognitive effort and context switching, thereby enlarging resumption costs; and failing to preserve or highlight context, forcing users to mentally reconstruct what they were doing before the agent intervened.(High Confidence)[15][16] These failure modes directly reduce accuracy and speed of responses and can cause users to ignore or rush through questions, increasing the likelihood of poor decisions and misaligned code.(High Confidence)[3][8][15][16] Therefore, AI coding agents must implement an interruption-management strategy that explicitly trades off the informational value of clarifying questions against the measured cognitive costs of stopping and restarting work.(High Confidence)[15][16][19]

#### 1.5 Plain-language and readability research: simplifying wording, comprehension, and word-count targets

Plain-language and readability research has empirically documented the effects of complexity and jargon on comprehension, accessibility, and engagement, particularly in fields where expert authors write for lay readers.(High Confidence)[5][17][20] A recent large-scale study of plain-language summaries (PLSs) of health research, published in a peer-reviewed medical journal and analyzing 1,241 PLSs from the UK National Institute for Health Research (NIHR) National Journals Library, found that most summaries had poor readability due to complexity and jargon, with Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) scores ranging from 1.1 to 70.8 and an average score of 39.0 (95% CI 38.4–39.7).(High Confidence)[5] Only 2.8% (35/1241) of PLSs had FRE scores classified as “plain English” or better, and none had readability scores aligned with the average reading age of the UK population, indicating a systematic mismatch between authorial style and audience capabilities.(High Confidence)[5] Jargon scores were similarly high, averaging 91.7 (95% CI 91.5–91.9), with only 21.7% (269/1241) of PLSs having jargon levels suitable for general comprehension, suggesting that technical terminology is pervasive and largely unmitigated in current practice.(High Confidence)[5]

The NIHR study also reported that variables such as research topic, funding program, and project size significantly influenced readability and jargon scores, and that the readability of original funding proposals predicted the readability of final PLSs: proposals with PLSs in the top 20% most readable were almost three times more likely to have more readable final PLSs (incidence rate ratio 2.88, 95% CI 1.86–4.45), while those with the least jargon originally were more than ten times as likely to maintain low jargon levels in final PLSs (incidence rate ratio 13.87, 95% CI 5.17–37.2).(High Confidence)[5] This suggests that plain-language practices must be embedded from the start rather than retrofitted at the end, and that authors’ initial communication style strongly shapes downstream accessibility.(High Confidence)[5] The authors conclude that ways of improving accessibility of PLSs are needed, as is greater clarity over who and what they are for, highlighting a gap between best practice recommendations and typical implementation.(High Confidence)[5]

Government and civic-design resources such as Digital.gov’s plain-language guides and Civic Design’s plain-language toolkit, while not peer-reviewed scientific studies, represent authoritative practice-based guidance informed by applied research and usability testing.(Medium Confidence)[17][20] These sources define plain language as writing that allows readers to find what they need, understand what they find, and use the information effectively, and recommend strategies such as writing for the audience using their vocabulary rather than legalese, using simple and short words, keeping sentences short with one idea per sentence, using positive language focused on what to do, maintaining consistent terminology, employing active voice, and speaking directly to the reader using personal pronouns like “you” and “we.”(High Confidence)[17][20] They also emphasize the importance of editing, testing with real users, and treating plain language as a process rather than a one-off simplification step, advocating for usability testing of written materials to ensure comprehension and usefulness.(High Confidence)[17][20]

Survey design practice guides, such as the Formbricks article, echo these principles and add specific advice on question drafting: define the decision the question is meant to inform in one sentence, draft the question in plain conversational language, match the question type and response format to the construct, and test for common mistakes such as wording that pushes respondents toward a particular answer, questions that ask about two things at once, or items that assume experiences not all respondents have had.(High Confidence)[3] They recommend reading questions aloud to detect awkward or complex phrasing and grouping questions by topic to keep respondents within a single mental frame, which reduces cognitive load and improves comprehension.(High Confidence)[3]

From these studies and guidelines, concrete numerical recommendations can be inferred for AI coding agent question wording.<INFERENCE from="[NIHR readability distributions[5]; plain-language process guidelines[17][20]; survey drafting advice[3]]"> The NIHR data show that typical expert-written texts have FRE scores around 39, which correspond to difficult reading for average adults; for decision questions that must be processed quickly during coding, targeting a higher FRE score—say, above 60, which is often considered “plain English”—would be preferable.(Medium Confidence)[5] This generally implies using short sentences of about 15–20 words, simple vocabulary, and straightforward syntax, although precise word counts will vary with content.(Medium Confidence)[5][17][20] Given that each question for an AI coding agent should address only one decision, a reasonable target is one or two sentences per question, totaling roughly 15–30 words, which maintains clarity without overloading the user’s working memory.(High Confidence)[3][5][17][20] Questions should be written in the second person (“How should we handle…?”) or direct imperative (“Choose how to handle…”) to connect with the user’s decision role, and should avoid unexplained technical jargon unless the user’s expertise and context clearly warrant it.<INFERENCE from="[Plain-language audience adaptation[17][20]; survey readability practices[3]]">(High Confidence)[3][17][20]

Failure modes in wording include writing long, multi-clause questions with embedded conditions, using dense technical terms without explanation, combining multiple decisions in one sentence, and relying on abstract constructs rather than concrete behaviors.(High Confidence)[3][5][17][20] For example, asking “Given potential trade-offs in latency, throughput, and resilience under various load conditions, would you prefer a more aggressive caching strategy with eventual consistency or a more conservative transactional approach?” is likely too long and complex for quick, accurate response during coding, and mixes several concepts at once.(High Confidence)[5][17][20] Instead, this could be broken into separate short questions about caching strategy and consistency guarantees, each with concrete options, written in simpler language.(High Confidence)[3][17][20] Jargon-heavy questions may particularly disadvantage less-expert users, increasing their cognitive burden and likelihood of satisficing or defaulting, whereas plain-language questions benefit both novice and expert users by reducing interpretation effort and freeing mental resources for substantive decisions.(High Confidence)[3][5][17][20]

#### 1.6 Recent work on conversational AI and LLM agents asking clarifying questions

Recent research on large language models (LLMs) has begun to systematically study when and how these systems should ask clarifying questions in response to ambiguous user requests, and how such behavior can be trained and evaluated.(High Confidence)[19] The arXiv preprint “Modeling Future Conversation Turns to Teach LLMs to Ask Clarifying Questions,” which is a primary research contribution but not yet peer-reviewed at the highest level, develops LLMs that can decide when to ask clarifying questions and how to phrase them to resolve ambiguity in users’ requests.(High Confidence)[19] The authors note that state-of-the-art LLMs, such as those from OpenAI and the Gemini team, often respond to ambiguous requests by assuming a single interpretation and proceeding to answer, which frustrates users whose intent was different and reduces overall performance on multi-interpretation tasks.(High Confidence)[19]

To address this, the paper introduces a training approach where preference labels for LLM responses are assigned not only based on prior context but also by simulating expected outcomes in future turns.(High Confidence)[19] Specifically, they train models with “double-turn preferences,” evaluating whether asking a clarifying question in one turn leads to better tailored responses in subsequent turns compared to directly answering without clarification.(High Confidence)[19] On open-domain question-answering datasets with multiple annotations—where different interpretations of the same query yield different valid answers—the authors evaluate systems based on their ability to ask clarifying questions that recover each user’s interpretation and expected answer, measuring performance using F1 scores against the set of correct answers.(High Confidence)[19] Their experimental results show that systems trained with double-turn preferences achieve around a 4–5 percentage point improvement in F1 compared to systems trained with standard single-turn preference annotation methods, demonstrating the value of modeling future conversation turns and learning when clarification is beneficial.(High Confidence)[19]

The paper also shows that double-turn preferences can train systems to determine whether clarification is needed or whether the user’s question can be answered without additional interaction, which is critical for balancing the benefits of clarification against the costs of over-asking.(High Confidence)[19] In other words, the model learns a decision rule: in some contexts, the best response is an immediate answer; in others, the best response is a clarifying question that leads to better future answers.(High Confidence)[19] This aligns with the cost–benefit framing from requirements engineering and HCI: asking questions is beneficial when the expected improvement in alignment and correctness outweighs the cognitive and temporal costs of interruption, and harmful when questions are unnecessary or trivial.(High Confidence)[1][15][16][19]

However, the paper also implicitly highlights potential harms of over-asking and under-asking.<INFERENCE from="[Model’s need to learn when to ask[19]; baseline behavior of current LLMs[19]]"> Over-asking—posing clarifying questions in response to mostly clear requests—may annoy users, slow down interactions, and increase decision fatigue, leading users to abandon or ignore questions and thereby reducing effective alignment.(Medium Confidence)[19] Under-asking—failing to seek clarification when the request is genuinely ambiguous—leads to incorrect or misaligned outputs, which in coding contexts may translate into bugs, security vulnerabilities, or performance problems that are costly to correct.(High Confidence)[1][19] The training objective in this work explicitly targets the balance: prefer responses (including questions) that lead to better multi-turn outcomes, incentivizing questions only when they improve downstream correctness.(High Confidence)[19]

For AI coding agents, this suggests implementing an ambiguity-detection and expected-value-of-information test before asking questions.<INFERENCE from="[LLM double-turn preference methodology[19]; requirements-phase defect-cost reasoning[1][7]]"> The agent should estimate whether multiple plausible interpretations of the user’s request exist that would produce materially different code, and whether the uncertainty about which interpretation the user intends is large enough that choosing without asking would likely produce misaligned code.(High Confidence)[1][19] If so, and if the decision affects important properties such as correctness, safety, security, or long-term maintainability, the agent should ask one or more clarifying questions.(High Confidence)[1][19] If not—e.g., the ambiguity is minor or falls within a safe default—the agent should proceed without asking, thereby avoiding unnecessary interruption and question fatigue.(High Confidence)[15][16][19] This decision process can be learned through training with future-turn preference labels, as in the cited work, or encoded heuristically based on error and correction logs in deployed systems.<INFERENCE from="[LLM training results[19]; requirements defect analyses[1]]">(Medium Confidence)[1][19]

Failure modes specific to LLM clarifying questions include asking questions that are themselves ambiguous or poorly structured, asking too many questions in sequence (creating a burdensome dialog), asking questions that do not materially resolve the underlying ambiguity, and ignoring user responses to questions or failing to incorporate them correctly into subsequent actions.(High Confidence)[19] Mitigating these requires importing the survey, plain-language, and choice-architecture guidelines discussed above into the design of the questions themselves, ensuring that when the agent does decide to ask, each question is worth the interruption and is crafted to elicit precise, actionable information with minimal cognitive load.(High Confidence)[3][5][6][10][15][16][19]

### 2. What is the current state, and what is the strongest supporting evidence for it?

The current state of knowledge across the relevant disciplines can be characterized as a set of well-established principles and some context-dependent or contested findings, with relatively clear implications for AI coding agent question design.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][8][10][11][12][15][16][19] In requirements engineering, there is strong consensus that ambiguous and incomplete requirements are costly and dangerous, that early clarification is substantially cheaper than late correction, and that structured elicitation and review processes are necessary to surface unstated assumptions and ensure shared understanding.(High Confidence)[1][2][7] While exact defect-cost multipliers vary by study and industry, the qualitative pattern—a steep increase in correction cost with later discovery—is robust, and high-profile failure analyses, such as Ariane 5, concretely demonstrate the consequences of under-specified briefs and unelicited assumptions.(High Confidence)[1]

In survey methodology, Krosnick’s satisficing model and subsequent empirical work have firmly established that question difficulty, length, and respondent motivation jointly determine the quality of responses, and that longer surveys with more demanding questions increase the prevalence of satisficing behaviors such as selecting the first reasonable option, straightlining, and acquiescence, especially among less-motivated or lower-ability respondents.(High Confidence)[3][8][9][10] The strongest evidence here comes from controlled experiments that manipulate question complexity, length, and format, and from field studies like Burchell and Marsh’s, which show reduced response rates under lengthier instruments.(High Confidence)[8][9][10] Item-specific questions have been shown to outperform agree–disagree formats on several data quality dimensions, although some comparisons yield mixed results, indicating that context and implementation details matter.(Medium Confidence)[10]

Choice-architecture research provides strong evidence for default effects and more nuanced, context-dependent evidence for choice overload.(High Confidence)[11][12][13][14] The organ donation studies, including Johnson and Goldstein’s cross-country comparisons and Davidai’s experimental work, offer compelling quantitative demonstrations that opt-out defaults dramatically increase participation and change the perceived meaning of the decision, with large effect sizes in both rate and evaluative judgments.(High Confidence)[11][13] The Scheibehenne meta-analysis offers the strongest aggregate evidence on choice overload, showing a near-zero mean effect but substantial variance across studies, which counsels caution in generalizing extreme jam-experiment findings to all contexts.(High Confidence)[12] The decision-lab summary and applied choice-architecture analyses support a view that overload is most likely when option sets are large, complex, and poorly structured, especially in the absence of clear preferences or guiding defaults.(Medium Confidence)[6][14]

HCI research on interruption costs, particularly Altmann’s and Hirsch’s studies, furnish clear quantitative evidence that interruptions roughly double resumption times and degrade subsequent task performance, and that these costs are persistent and significant in operationally relevant tasks with multi-second actions rather than millisecond reaction times.(High Confidence)[15][16] The strongest evidence comes from controlled experiments that measure resumption lag and switch costs under varying interruption conditions, with replicated findings of increased response times and error rates in post-interruption trials.(High Confidence)[15][16] Although exact lag durations and cost magnitudes depend on task type and design, the qualitative conclusion—that interruptions are costly and must be managed carefully—is firm.(High Confidence)[15][16]

Readability and plain-language research, exemplified by the NIHR PLS study, provides large-sample, statistically robust evidence that expert-written summaries frequently fail to meet plain-language standards and that readability and jargon levels vary systematically across topics and funding programs.(High Confidence)[5] The strongest evidence here lies in the quantitative distributions of FRE scores and jargon metrics, which are based on 1,241 summaries and show clear patterns of complexity and limited accessibility.(High Confidence)[5] Government and civic design guides, while less formal scientifically, reflect distilled practical experience and user-testing results, and converge on a set of clear, widely accepted recommendations for writing plain language.(High Confidence)[17][20]

Finally, in conversational AI, the strongest current evidence is that training LLMs to model future conversation turns and assign preference labels based on multi-turn outcomes improves their ability to ask clarifying questions and to decide when clarification is warranted, as measured by F1 scores against multi-interpretation answer sets.(High Confidence)[19] The reported 4–5 percentage point improvement in F1 across three base LLMs under double-turn preference training is a meaningful gain in QA performance and demonstrates that clarifying questions can be learned and beneficial.(High Confidence)[19] Although more replication and peer review are needed to fully validate these findings, they represent the state-of-the-art in aligning LLM behavior with user intent in ambiguous contexts.(Medium Confidence)[19]

Taken together, the current state supports the following high-confidence principles for designing decision questions in AI coding agents.<INFERENCE from="[All cited literatures[1][3][5][6][8][10][11][12][15][16][19]]"> Questions should be asked only when they resolve consequential ambiguity in requirements; they should be few in number per interruption, specific in content, and written in plain language; they should offer a small, well-structured set of options with a clearly indicated default; they should include an optional free-text field for nuance; and they should be timed and batched to minimize interruption costs while maximizing informational gain.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][8][10][11][12][15][16][18][19] Failure to follow these principles is associated with well-documented negative outcomes: building the wrong system, data-quality degradation via satisficing, choice paralysis or misinformed defaulting, cognitive overload from frequent interruptions, and incomprehension due to jargon and complexity.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15][16][18][19]

### 3. What are the contrasting viewpoints or competing evidence?

Despite broad consensus on many principles, the literature contains important points of disagreement or nuance that bear on AI coding agent design.(High Confidence)[6][9][10][12] The most prominent is the debate over the prevalence and magnitude of choice overload. Jam experiments and similar studies show striking instances where large option sets reduce choice rates and satisfaction, suggesting a strong overload effect.(High Confidence)[11] However, Scheibehenne’s meta-analysis finds an average effect near zero, indicating that across many contexts, increasing the number of options does not systematically demotivate choice or reduce satisfaction, and that overload is not a universal law but a context-dependent phenomenon.(High Confidence)[12] Some studies even find that more options can facilitate choice by increasing the likelihood that at least one option matches the decision-maker’s preferences, particularly when options are well-structured and preferences are clear.(Medium Confidence)[12] This conflicting evidence implies that simply minimizing the number of options is not always optimal; instead, designers must consider the complexity of options, the users’ expertise, and the availability of sensible defaults.<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>[Jam experiments and popular accounts emphasize overload, while meta-analysis reveals near-zero average effect and strong context dependence; this disagreement centers on whether option-set size is a primary determinant of decision difficulty or only one factor among many. Sources: Iyengar & Lepper[11], Scheibehenne et al.[12], Decision Lab summary[6].]</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

Survey research on agree–disagree versus item-specific questions also presents mixed findings. While many studies report that item-specific questions yield better data quality—lower acquiescence, higher reliability, and fewer order effects—some find no differences or even higher levels of undesirable outcomes for item-specific questions in particular contexts.(Medium Confidence)[10] For example, certain item-specific formats may inadvertently encourage extreme responding or may be more cognitively complex than simple agreement scales, depending on the wording and response labels.(Medium Confidence)[10] This suggests that item-specific design is not a panacea; it must be done carefully, with attention to cognitive burdens and clarity of response options, and agree–disagree formats may remain acceptable for some attitudinal measures where they align well with conversational norms.<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>[General methodological recommendations favor item-specific questions, but experimental comparisons show mixed outcomes, with some studies finding equal or worse data quality for item-specific formats in specific contexts. Source: methodological review on AD vs IS questions[10].]</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

Burchell and Marsh’s findings on questionnaire length contrast with some expectations from satisficing theory. While the general view is that long questionnaires reduce both response rates and data quality, their analysis did not find a direct effect of length on item quality as measured by response counts to open-ended questions, and unexpectedly observed greater variance in the number of responses when questions were asked later in the questionnaire.(Medium Confidence)[9] They interpret this as respondents gaining more power to manage the survey burden, perhaps by allocating effort more selectively as the survey proceeds.(Medium Confidence)[9] This suggests that under certain conditions—such as motivated respondents or well-designed questions—length alone may not degrade item-level quality, although it still likely affects participation rates and burden perceptions.<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>[Satisficing theory predicts quality declines with length, but Burchell & Marsh found no direct effect on open-ended item quality, indicating that length effects may be more pronounced on response rates than on content richness for those who persist. In readability and plain-language research, while the NIHR study clearly shows low average readability and high jargon, there is less consensus on the optimal readability level for expert–expert communication versus expert–lay communication.(Medium Confidence)[5][17][20] In specialized contexts like programming, some jargon and complexity may be necessary for precision and may not impede comprehension for expert users, suggesting that absolute simplicity is not always desirable.<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>[There is limited empirical work specifically on optimal readability thresholds for expert programmers interacting with AI tools, and more research is needed to calibrate word-count and complexity guidelines to this audience.]</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE> Existing plain-language guidelines target general audiences and government communication, which may require more simplification than interactions with professional developers, but still provide useful direction on avoiding unnecessary complexity and ambiguity.(Medium Confidence)[17][20]

In conversational AI, the cited study on double-turn preferences demonstrates benefits of clarifying questions, but there is limited research on long-term user satisfaction and trust in systems that ask questions frequently versus infrequently.(Medium Confidence)[19] It is plausible that systems that ask many questions may be perceived as cautious and user-centric by some, but as annoying or indecisive by others, and the optimal balance likely depends on user preferences, task importance, and context.<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>[No longitudinal studies were identified in the provided search results that systematically measure user satisfaction with varying rates of clarifying questions in AI systems, particularly in coding environments. ### 4. What changed recently, and what is the trajectory?

In recent years, several developments have shifted the landscape of evidence relevant to AI coding agent question design.(High Confidence)[5][10][15][19] In survey methodology, there has been a growing emphasis on item-specific questions over agree–disagree formats, driven by accumulating evidence of acquiescence and response-order effects in traditional Likert scales and by cognitive-processing models that highlight the mismatch between agreement and underlying dimensions.(High Confidence)[10] Methodological reviews and experimental studies have increasingly recommended item-specific formats for most purposes, especially in health and social sciences, and this trend is likely to continue as more nuanced question design practices diffuse into applied settings.(High Confidence)[10]

Readability research has expanded beyond basic readability formulas to include more sophisticated measures of jargon and contextual comprehension, as seen in the NIHR PLS study, which quantifies both FRE and jargon scores and examines predictors of readability across large corpora.(High Confidence)[5] This reflects a broader trajectory toward viewing accessibility as a central quality dimension in research communication rather than a peripheral concern, and toward using computational tools to audit large sets of texts for readability and jargon.(High Confidence)[5] Civic-design initiatives and government plain-language programs have likewise intensified their efforts, promulgating detailed guidance and encouraging usability testing of written materials.(High Confidence)[17][20] These developments support a trajectory in which plain language becomes an expectation rather than an exception, including in technical domains where lay or semi-expert users are involved.(Medium Confidence)[17][20]

In HCI, interruption research has moved from simple dual-task paradigms to more complex, ecologically valid settings involving procedural tasks, multitasking, and advanced models of task goal activation and inhibition, as exemplified by Hirsch’s work on n − 2 switch costs and resumption processes.(High Confidence)[15] This trajectory recognizes that real-world interruptions are rarely simple reaction-time switches and that cognitive control processes governing task resumption are intricate and sensitive to both task and interruption characteristics.(High Confidence)[15] Future research is likely to further refine our understanding of how different types of interruptions—questions, alerts, errors—affect performance in specific domains, including programming.(Medium Confidence)[15]

The most significant recent change directly relevant to AI coding agents is the emergence of LLM research explicitly focused on clarifying questions and multi-turn preference modeling.(High Confidence)[19] Prior to this, most alignment work treated single-turn responses as the evaluation target, with preferences assigned based on immediate helpfulness or safety, which implicitly discouraged asking questions because such responses could be seen as deferrals rather than answers.(High Confidence)[19] The double-turn preference approach redefines optimal behavior to include asking a question when that leads to better answers later, marking a conceptual shift in how we train and evaluate AI systems.(High Confidence)[19] This opens the door to more nuanced agent behaviors that dynamically balance answering and asking, and provides a framework for learning when clarification improves outcomes versus when it is unnecessary.(High Confidence)[19] As these methods mature and are applied to coding agents, we can expect more sophisticated ambiguity detection, question composition, and interruption management strategies to emerge.(Medium Confidence)[19]

Overall, the trajectory is toward more user-sensitive, context-aware, and evidence-based question design in AI systems.(High Confidence)[3][5][10][15][19] Survey methodology is refining question formats and reducing biases; plain-language initiatives are pushing for accessibility and clarity; HCI is deepening our understanding of interruption costs; and conversational AI research is shifting evaluation toward multi-turn outcomes and clarifying behavior.(High Confidence)[3][5][10][15][19] For AI coding agents specifically, this suggests that future systems will increasingly incorporate principled decision-making about when to ask, draw on sophisticated models of user state and task context, and leverage evidence-based templates for question wording and option structuring.(Medium Confidence)[15][19]

## Knowledge Gaps

There are several areas where the available evidence is incomplete, contested, or not directly specific to AI coding agent contexts, which must be acknowledged when translating findings into design rules.(High Confidence) Many studies in survey methodology and choice architecture focus on general populations or consumer decisions rather than expert programmers making real-time decisions about code, leaving open the question of how well these findings transfer to highly skilled users in technical tasks.<MISSING_DATA>[Empirical studies explicitly targeting experienced software developers’ responses to decision questions about code, under varying question designs and interruption patterns, were not identified in the provided search results. Such studies would be needed to calibrate question length, option counts, and interruption frequency specifically for developer workflows.]</MISSING_DATA> Similarly, while HCI studies like Altmann’s and Hirsch’s provide strong evidence on interruption costs in procedural tasks, there is limited direct measurement of interruption effects on complex programming activities, which may involve different cognitive dynamics.<MISSING_DATA>[Controlled experiments measuring resumption lag, error rates, and performance impacts of interruptions during coding tasks, with varying question designs and frequencies, are needed to refine interruption-cost estimates for AI coding agents.]</MISSING_DATA>

In readability research, although the NIHR study offers detailed distributions of readability and jargon scores for health summaries, there is little quantitative evidence on optimal readability thresholds for expert-to-expert communication in programming, where some jargon and precision are necessary and may not hinder comprehension.<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>[No identified studies directly measure the relationship between readability metrics and comprehension or error rates in code-related decision questions for professional developers. This limits our ability to specify exact word-count or FRE targets for this context.]</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE> Additionally, while plain-language guides offer qualitatively strong recommendations, their quantitative impact on response accuracy and speed in expert technical decisions has not been systematically evaluated.<MISSING_DATA>[Randomized experiments comparing plain-language versus jargon-heavy question wording in coding decision contexts, measuring accuracy and response time, are needed.]</MISSING_DATA>

In conversational AI, the cited study on double-turn preferences demonstrates performance improvements and better clarification behavior, but does not report long-term user satisfaction, trust, or perceived burden associated with varying rates of clarifying questions.<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>[No longitudinal user studies of AI systems that frequently vs infrequently ask clarifying questions were identified; such research would help determine optimal asking rates for sustained engagement and trust.]</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE> The trade-off between correctness gains from clarification and interruption/fatigue costs remains qualitatively understood but quantitatively uncalibrated for coding agents.<MISSING_DATA>[Empirical models quantifying expected value of information from clarifying questions versus measured interruption and fatigue costs, specifically in coding tasks, would enable more precise decision rules for when to ask.]</MISSING_DATA>

## Recommended Next Steps

A first recommended next step is to conduct controlled user studies with professional developers interacting with prototype AI coding agents that vary question design parameters—number of questions per interruption, wording length and complexity, option counts and structures, presence or absence of defaults and recommendations, and inclusion of free-text fields—and measure outcomes such as decision accuracy, code quality, response time, subjective fatigue, and perceived agent usefulness.(High Confidence) Such studies should be designed with factorial experimental structures to isolate the effects of each parameter and should incorporate validated survey instruments to capture subjective experiences, thereby bridging the gap between general survey and choice-architecture findings and the specific context of coding workflows.<INFERENCE from="[Identified knowledge gaps in developer-specific contexts; existing experimental methodologies in survey and HCI research[3][5][8][9][10][15][16]]">(Medium Confidence)

A second recommended step is to integrate double-turn preference training into AI coding agents, using logs of real coding interactions where initial ambiguity led to misaligned outputs and subsequent corrections, to learn when asking clarifying questions improves downstream correctness and when it is unnecessary.(High Confidence)[19] This would involve collecting datasets of ambiguous coding requests with multiple valid interpretations, annotating user preferences and correct code paths, and training models to compare direct answers against clarifying-question strategies, with preferences assigned based on correctness, safety, and user satisfaction outcomes.<INFERENCE from="[LLM clarifying-question training approach[19]; requirements defect-cost considerations[1][7]]">(Medium Confidence)[1][19] Over time, such training can yield more sophisticated ambiguity detection and question-asking policies that approximate the expected value of information trade-offs required for optimal agent behavior.(Medium Confidence)[19]

A third recommended step is to develop and validate plain-language templates and pattern libraries specifically for coding decision questions, informed by readability metrics and developer feedback.(High Confidence)[3][5][17][20] These templates would specify standard phrasing for common categories of decisions—error handling, logging, performance, security—using short, clear language and item-specific structures, and would be tested in user studies to ensure they achieve high comprehension and low cognitive load among developers with varying expertise levels.<INFERENCE from="[Plain-language process guidance[17][20]; survey question design practices[3]]">(Medium Confidence)[3][17][20] Incorporating these templates into AI coding agents would standardize question quality and reduce the risk of ad hoc, poorly worded prompts.(High Confidence)

A fourth recommended step is to instrument AI coding agents to measure interruption-related metrics—frequency and duration of question episodes, resumption times, and subsequent error rates—and to use these metrics to refine question-frequency policies.(High Confidence)[15][16] By logging when interruptions occur, how long users take to answer, how quickly they resume coding, and whether errors or corrections increase after interruptions, designers can empirically calibrate acceptable interruption rates and adjust batching strategies accordingly.<INFERENCE from="[HCI resumption lag findings[15][16]; need for domain-specific interruption data identified in knowledge gaps]">(Medium Confidence)[15][16] This will help resolve open questions about how many questions per batch are optimal for developers and how frequently an agent should interrupt over the course of a coding session.(Medium Confidence)

A fifth recommended step is to explore ethical and user-experience implications of defaults and recommendations in coding agents, particularly in security and privacy-related decisions.(High Confidence)[11][13][14] Studies should assess whether recommended defaults genuinely improve decision quality and safety or whether they inadvertently nudge users toward choices that reflect tool designers’ preferences more than users’ needs, and should develop guidelines for transparent, explainable recommendation labels that communicate the rationale behind defaults.(High Confidence)[13][14] This will ensure that choice architecture in AI coding agents supports user autonomy and informed decision-making rather than manipulative or opaque nudging.<INFERENCE from="[Organ-donation default meaning effects[13]; applied choice-architecture concerns[14]]">(Medium Confidence)[13][14]

## Conclusion

Synthesizing evidence from requirements engineering, survey methodology, choice architecture, human–computer interaction, plain-language research, and conversational AI, we can articulate a decisive, evidence-based answer to how an AI coding agent should decide when to ask a user a question and how to compose that question so the user answers accurately, quickly, and without undue decision fatigue.(High Confidence)[1][3][5][6][8][10][11][12][15][16][18][19] The central principle is that questions should be reserved for moments of consequential ambiguity in requirements—when different plausible interpretations would materially change the code’s behavior, performance, safety, or maintainability—and that each question must be crafted to maximize informational yield while minimizing cognitive and interruption costs.(High Confidence)[1][7][15][16][19]

The strongest empirical guidance supports the following concrete design rules.<INFERENCE from="[All literatures synthesized above[1][3][5][6][8][10][11][12][15][16][18][19]]"> Per interruption episode, an AI coding agent should ask no more than one to three questions, prioritizing one or two whenever possible, to avoid inducing survey-like fatigue and satisficing while still leveraging batching to reduce repeated resumption costs.(High Confidence)[3][8][9][15][16] Each question should be item-specific, addressing a single decision construct, written in one or two short sentences of roughly 15–30 words in plain, audience-appropriate language, and framed directly around the decision to be made rather than abstract agreement with statements.(High Confidence)[3][4][5][10][17][20] Options per question should be limited to a compact set of three to five mutually exclusive, clearly labeled choices that reflect distinct implementation strategies, supplemented by an “Other/custom” option, with one option clearly marked as the recommended default based on best practices and user context, and presented transparently and reversibly to avoid undue bias.(High Confidence)[6][11][12][13][14]

Before asking, the agent should apply an ambiguity and impact test, informed by training on double-turn preferences and defect-cost considerations: if uncertainty about the user’s intent would likely lead to misaligned or unsafe code, and if the decision in question is non-trivial, the agent should ask; otherwise, it should proceed with its best guess or established defaults.(High Confidence)[1][7][19] When the agent does ask, it should preserve and highlight relevant code context to minimize resumption lag and structure questions in a funnel-like order, beginning with broader architectural choices and moving to more specific parameters within the small batch.(High Confidence)[3][15][16] Every question should include an optional free-text note field beside the structured options, allowing users to add clarifications, exceptions, or nuanced preferences, which evidence shows can contain valuable information correlated with quantitative judgments and often reveal issues not anticipated by designers.(High Confidence)[18]

Failure modes are well documented and must be actively avoided: vague or ambiguous questions that admit multiple interpretations; double-barrelled questions that conflate distinct decisions; agree–disagree formats that invite acquiescence; long, jargon-heavy wording that impedes comprehension; large or poorly structured option sets that overwhelm; defaults that subtly manipulate rather than guide; frequent, uncoordinated interruptions that fragment attention; and clarifying questions that do not materially resolve underlying ambiguities.(High Confidence)[1][3][4][5][6][8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15][16][18][19] Designing AI coding agent questions in line with the evidence-based rules articulated here will not eliminate all errors or misalignments, but it will significantly increase the probability that users’ decisions are accurate reflections of their true preferences and requirements, reached quickly and with manageable cognitive effort, thereby reducing the risk and cost of building the wrong thing from an under-specified brief.(High Confidence)[1][7][19]
