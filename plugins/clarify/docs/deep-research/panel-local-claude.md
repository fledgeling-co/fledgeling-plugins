# How to Ask a Person a Decision Question Well — Evidence Synthesis

*Prepared 9 August 2026. Scope: requirements elicitation, survey methodology, judgment & decision-making, HCI interruption research, plain-language research, and LLM clarification benchmarks. Analytical lens: convert each finding into an authoring rule with a number where the evidence supports one; flag where lab findings may not transfer to experts answering about their own work.*

---

## Executive Summary

- **(High Confidence)** The binding constraint on an agent's clarifying question is *when to ask*, not *how to phrase it*. Both failure directions are measured and large: on AmbigQA, ten frontier models asked a clarifying question on only 0.2–4.9% of genuinely ambiguous items despite recognising ambiguity 60–80% of the time when asked explicitly [Su & Cardie, arXiv:2605.25284, May 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25284v1); conversely, Llama 3.1 70B under "strong" encouragement prompts interacted on 93–95% of *fully specified* SWE-bench issues [Vijayvargiya et al., Ambig-SWE, ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13069v3). An always-ask instruction converts one failure mode into the other.
- **(High Confidence)** The best available test of "is this question worth asking" is a *divergence test*, not a confidence heuristic. ClarifyGPT generates candidate solutions from the ambiguous request and asks only when they disagree; human-in-the-loop this lifted GPT-4 Pass@1 from 70.96% to 80.80% on MBPP-sanitized, +13.87% relative, p = 3.2e-05 [Mu et al., FSE 2024 / PACMSE](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3660810). The complementary evidence: when a well-calibrated agent chose *not* to ask, its resolve rate (76.92%) matched the no-information baseline (77.56%) — i.e. there was genuinely nothing to learn [Edwards & Schuster, arXiv:2603.26233, March 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1).
- **(Medium Confidence)** Batch into one interaction; 1–3 questions. Systems with the best outcome-per-question ratio asked 1.39–3.06 questions per task [Suri et al., Findings of ACL 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08798)[Edwards & Schuster 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1); the model asking 6.02 questions per task wasted turns on redundant navigation and got *worse* with the information it obtained (55.43% → 52.38%) [Ambig-SWE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13069v3). Serially fragmented asking is scored as a distinct failure: Qwen2.5-Coder matched Qwen3-Coder on premises resolved (0.643 vs 0.640) but scored 0.365 vs 0.456 on efficiency-adjusted recall purely from a higher turns-to-clarify (2.396) [Li, Wu & Chang, ClarEval, arXiv:2603.00187, Feb 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00187v1).
- **(Medium Confidence)** Cap options at 2–4, but the reason is *order bias and reading cost*, not choice overload. Choice overload's pooled effect across 63 conditions (N = 5,036) was "virtually zero" [Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, JCR 37(3), 2010](https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf); it appears mainly where preferences are unformed, which is not the case for an expert answering about their own project. The real costs are that primacy bias grows with list length and appears at lists as short as 7 [Encyclopedia of Survey Research Methods, response order effects](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/survey/chpt/response-order-effects), and each option must carry its consequence in words.
- **(Medium Confidence)** Lead with a marked recommendation, but only where you can state the evidence behind it — because a *wrong* recommendation is measurably worse than none. In a controlled e-prescribing study, correct decision support cut omission errors by 38.3–46.6%, while incorrect support *increased* them by 24.5–33.3% and produced commission-error rates of 51.7–65.8% [Lyell et al., automation bias in electronic prescribing, 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5356416/). Recommendations will not steamroll an expert: measured weight-of-advice is 0.20–0.39, i.e. people shift only 20–39% toward advice [Bonaccio & Dalal, OBHDP 101(2), 2006](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Advice-taking-and-decision-making:-An-integrative-Bonaccio-Dalal/d0d1e850d29d272b32fe96ec72c00b67a4ce168f)[Bailey et al., meta-analysis of weight of advice, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39711945/).
- **(Medium Confidence)** Target ≤20 words for the question stem, hard cap 25. Payne's 1951 20-word rule is the origin; Johnson & Morgan (2016) relax it to 25 [MeasuringU synthesis of question-clarity guidance](https://measuringu.com/clearer-questions/). The strongest modern evidence is reliability estimates for 426 exactly replicated questions across six Michigan panel surveys, showing declining reliability with more words *for some question types* [Alwin & Beattie, Sociological Methodology 46(1), 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0081175016641714). Note the honest limit: plain-language rewrites reliably improve *preference and speed* but produce comprehension gains of only ~0–14 percentage points, frequently null in adequately powered RCTs [Canadian pediatric consent RCT, n=150](https://www.cjb-rcb.ca/index.php/cjb-rcb/article/view/823).
- **(Low Confidence)** An optional free-text note *attached to a chosen option* is probably net-positive, but this exact design has not been tested. Closed lists demonstrably miss respondent-generated categories `Schuman & Presser 1979 (unusable citation URL)`, which argues for an escape hatch; but embedding open-ended probes raised break-off and answer-changing [Hadler, Sociological Methods & Research, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00491241231176846), and a standalone "don't know" escape invites satisficing without improving data quality across nine experiments [Krosnick et al., POQ 66(3), 2002](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/66/3/371/1836194). The distinction that matters: a note *beside* a committed choice is not an escape *from* choosing.
- **(High Confidence)** Three widely-cited premises behind "ask early or pay later" are contested or refuted and should not be load-bearing: the exponential cost-to-fix curve (no evidence across 171 projects, 2006–2014) [Menzies et al., Empirical Software Engineering 22, 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04886); the Standish CHAOS success figures [Eveleens & Verhoef, IEEE Software 27(1), 2010](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~x/the_rise_and_fall_of_the_chaos_report_figures.pdf); and "decision fatigue" as a depletable-resource mechanism [Hagger et al., 23 labs, N=2,141, d=0.04, Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(4), 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873).

## Detailed Findings

### Primary question: What does the evidence say about how to ask a person a decision question well, and what are the documented failure modes of asking badly?

#### (1) Requirements elicitation: ambiguity, unstated assumptions, and the cost of an under-specified brief

The RE literature's most useful reframe is that **ambiguity is a signal, not only a defect**. Ferrari, Spoletini and Gnesi analysed 34 recorded customer–analyst interviews and built a four-class framework — unclarity, multiple understanding, incorrect disambiguation, correct disambiguation — concluding that an ambiguity occurrence is "often a resource for discovering tacit knowledge" rather than an obstacle [Ferrari, Spoletini & Gnesi, Requirements Engineering 21(3):333–355, 2016](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/38ee790c-9c4e-4758-9aae-6c87ab3bde7f/prod_353983-doc_114973.pdf). From the same corpus they isolated 232 customer speech fragments the analyst perceived as ambiguous and classified the triggering terms as reusable *cues* [Ferrari et al., RE 2016, "Ambiguity Cues in Requirements Elicitation Interviews"](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7765511/).

The measured detection deficit is the operationally important number. In a controlled review experiment, only **32% of the total ambiguities eventually discovered were identified during the interview itself**; the remaining 68% surfaced only in post-hoc review by the analyst plus a second reviewer, and the two reviewers' finds differed significantly from each other [Spoletini, Ferrari, Bano, Zowghi & Gnesi, REFSQ 2018](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-77243-1_7). An earlier exploratory pass put reviewer-only detection at 46% [Ferrari et al., interview-review study, author copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323449459_Interview_Review_An_Empirical_Study_on_Detecting_Ambiguities_in_Requirements_Elicitation_Interviews).

<INFERENCE from="Spoletini et al. 2018 (68% of ambiguities missed live); Ferrari et al. 2016 (ambiguity triggers are lexically identifiable cues)">A skill that decides when to ask should run an explicit ambiguity-scan step against known trigger classes rather than relying on the model noticing mid-flow — the human baseline for in-flight detection is roughly one-third.</INFERENCE>

Novice-interviewer failure modes are catalogued: 12 of 28 student groups failed to identify relevant stakeholders, and failing to ask probing/follow-up questions was singled out as defeating the purpose of the interaction [Bano, Zowghi, Ferrari, Spoletini & Donati, Requirements Engineering 24(3):259–289, 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00766-019-00313-0).

**On the cost of building the wrong thing — this is where the literature is weakest, and the standard citations do not hold.**

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>
Boehm & Basili's widely cited list attributes ~40–50% of effort on many projects to avoidable rework, with "hastily-specified requirements" named as one of two major sources [Boehm & Basili, IEEE Computer 34(1):135–137, 2001](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/SoftEng/ESEG/papers/82.78.pdf). Menzies et al. tested the underlying "delayed issue effect" across 171 projects worldwide (2006–2014) — the largest such study published — and found **no evidence** that later-phase issues cost consistently or substantially more to resolve, concluding DIE "is not a constant across all projects" and should not be treated as a global truism [Menzies, Nichols, Shull et al., Empirical Software Engineering 22:1903–1935, 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-016-9469-x). Separately, Eveleens & Verhoef showed the Standish CHAOS success/failure figures rest on a one-sided estimation-accuracy definition that ignores cost/time underruns and functionality overruns, and did not reflect their own case-study data at all (667 f/a ratios across 140 project costs at one organisation; 3,767 across 867 at another) [Eveleens & Verhoef, IEEE Software 27(1):30–36, 2010](https://www.cs.vu.nl/~x/the_rise_and_fall_of_the_chaos_report_figures.pdf).
</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

The defensible modern substitute is direct measurement in the LLM setting. Underspecified prompts are **2× as likely to regress across model or prompt changes**, with accuracy drops "sometimes exceeding 20%"; models satisfy unstated requirements by default only 41.1% of the time; requirements-aware prompt optimisation recovered 4.8% on average [Yang, Shi, Ma, Liu, Kästner & Wu, arXiv:2505.13360v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13360). <MISSING_DATA>The full-text figures behind the 41.1% and 20% numbers — task counts, datasets per condition, whether 20% is absolute or relative, and significance testing — were not recoverable from the abstract page or the compressed PDF; the HTML v3 or TeX source would be needed.</MISSING_DATA>

**Authoring rules:** run an explicit ambiguity scan before deciding to ask (human in-flight detection is ~32%); treat an ambiguity as a probe for tacit knowledge, not just a gap to fill; do not justify the skill with cost-curve or CHAOS statistics.

#### (2) Survey and questionnaire methodology: wording, length, jargon, satisficing, order

**Satisficing is the master mechanism.** Krosnick's framework holds that when optimising is costly, respondents shortcut — weak satisficing (less thorough retrieval, choosing the first reasonable option) or strong satisficing (skipping retrieval entirely, answering from question cues). Likelihood is a function of *task difficulty, respondent ability, and respondent motivation* [Krosnick, Applied Cognitive Psychology 5(3), 1991](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.2350050305). Observable consequences: primacy/recency, acquiescence, status-quo endorsement, non-differentiation, and "don't know" responding [Krosnick & Presser, Handbook of Survey Research, 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf).

This is the single most important transfer caveat for the skill. Two of the three satisficing determinants — ability and motivation — run *favourably* for an expert answering about their own work. Task difficulty is the one the author controls.

**Length.** Lenzner, Kaczmirek & Lenzner ran a psycholinguistic experiment identifying seven text features that raise cognitive burden (low-frequency words, left-embedded syntax, vague relative terms like "often"), of which at least six materially affected question clarity [Lenzner, Kaczmirek & Lenzner, Applied Cognitive Psychology 24:1003–1020, 2010](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Cognitive-burden-of-survey-questions-and-response-A-Lenzner-Kaczmirek/f81f88f649b5e9a99cac6f3cbb0b6f969d52c9cd). The follow-up web-survey experiment showed respondents given less comprehensible questions produced lower-quality responses across break-off, non-substantive answers, neutral answers and over-time consistency [Lenzner, Field Methods 24(4):409–428, 2012](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1525822X12448166).

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>Alwin & Beattie found declining reliability with word count across 426 replicated questions in six Michigan panel surveys, endorsing "as short as possible" [Alwin & Beattie 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0081175016641714). A competing review concludes question length is *not* consistently associated with respondent behaviours, citing Cannell et al. 1981, Alwin & Beattie 2016 and Holbrook et al. — noting length is confounded with complexity, and that longer questions can also give respondents more time to think [Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology 7(2):275, 2019](https://academic.oup.com/jssam/article/7/2/275/4968033). Saris & Gallhofer found no length effect on reliability but an effect on trait validity. The resolution most authors reach: **words are a measurable proxy for complexity, and complexity is the real driver** — so a word cap is a useful heuristic, not a law.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

**Double-barrelled questions.** Textbook condemnation is near-universal, but the rigorous evidence is thinner than the prescription implies. Two randomised split experiments comparing DBQs against single-stimulus revisions found means and latent parameters differed across versions, metric and scalar invariance failed, and at least one single-stimulus version had higher validity — but **response latencies were equal or shorter for DBQs**, contradicting the cognitive-burden prediction. The interpretation is that respondents access one stimulus and disregard the other ["Double Barreled Questions: An Analysis of the Similarity of Elements and Effects on Measurement Quality", author copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347848618_Double_Barreled_Questions_An_Analysis_of_the_Similarity_of_Elements_and_Effects_on_Measurement_Quality). A separate interval-response experiment found significantly broader response intervals for double- than single-barrelled items — behavioural evidence of genuine uncertainty [interval-valued survey response mode validity study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.08456).

**Response order.** Primacy in visual presentation, recency in auditory — the canonical split-ballot demonstration used the 1984 GSS parental-values measure, and predicted the effect would be strongest among the least cognitively sophisticated `Krosnick & Alwin 1987 (unusable citation URL)`. Magnitude grows with list length; order bias has been observed at lists as short as 7 [Sage Encyclopedia of Survey Research Methods](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/survey/chpt/response-order-effects). Across 548 telephone-survey experiments, recency was most pronounced for *complicated* questions `Holbrook et al. 2007 (unusable citation URL)`.

<MISSING_DATA>Pooled percentage-point magnitudes for response-order shifts were sought and not found; the Krosnick & Fabrigar meta-analysis of order effects is the likely source but was not retrievable in this pass.</MISSING_DATA>

**Number of options.** For rating scales, reliability is poor at 2–4 points, rises to ~7, and plateaus at 7–10 [Krosnick & Presser 2010](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf). This literature is about *graded scales* and transfers poorly to *discrete alternatives*, where the relevant costs are reading load and order bias. Full verbal labelling of every option raises reliability over endpoint-only labelling — likely more consequential than the count itself.

**Authoring rules:** stem ≤20 words, hard cap 25; one construct per question; ban vague relative quantifiers, low-frequency words and left-embedded syntax; label every option in full rather than leaving the reader to infer intermediate meanings; assume the first option gets an unearned boost and place the recommendation there deliberately rather than accidentally.

#### (3) Choice architecture: option count, recommendations, defaults, and the free-text escape

**Choice overload is not the constraint here.** The 2010 meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) found a mean effect size of virtually zero with large between-study variance, and identified *no sufficient conditions* for reliable overload; the Iyengar & Lepper jam study sat at d = .77 while several other conditions were negative [Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, JCR 37(3):409–425, 2010](https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf).

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman re-ran the question over 99 observations (N = 7,202) and argued the null main effect masks four real moderators: choice-set complexity, decision-task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal [Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, Journal of Consumer Psychology 25(2):333–358, 2015](https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf); a corrigendum was later published (JCP 26(2):312, 2016), so effect-size figures from the original should be checked against it. The two camps agree on the operative point for this skill: overload appears where preferences are *unformed*. Where preferences exist, more options produce a more-is-better pattern.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

<INFERENCE from="Scheibehenne et al. 2010 null main effect; Chernev et al. 2015 preference-uncertainty moderator; Krosnick 1991 satisficing determinants (ability, motivation)">An expert answering a fork about their own project has formed preferences and high motivation, so choice overload is a weak argument for capping options. The stronger arguments for a cap are order bias scaling with list length and the word cost of describing each option by its consequence.</INFERENCE>

**Reaction-time cost of extra options is logarithmic and small.** RT = a + b·log₂(N), with Hyman's slope ≈140 ms/bit — roughly 160 ms for one alternative to ~600 ms for eight [Proctor & Schneider, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2018](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~dws/pubs/ProctorSchneider_2018_QJEP.pdf). Two boundary conditions matter: the law describes choosing among *known* alternatives, whereas scanning unfamiliar options is roughly *linear*; and over-correction is a documented error — three or four sequential 5-option steps can exceed the decision time of one 15-option step. Hick's law is also frequently conflated with choice overload; they are distinct phenomena.

**Defaults and recommendations.** The canonical default effect is large: switching from opt-in to opt-out roughly doubled consent in an online experiment (≈42% → ≈82%) [Johnson & Goldstein, Science 302:1338–1339, 2003](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1324774). Johnson & Goldstein's own proposed mechanism is directly relevant: decision-makers "might interpret defaults as implicit policy recommendations."

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>The overall nudge/choice-architecture literature does not survive publication-bias correction intact. Mertens et al. pooled 447 effect sizes from 212 publications (n = 2,148,439) at d = 0.43, 95% CI [0.38, 0.48]; their own Egger's test was significant (b = 2.10, t(332) = 5.22, P < 0.001) and a moderate-bias adjustment reduced the effect 22.5% to d = 0.31 [Mertens, Herberz, Hahnel & Brosch, PNAS 119(1), 2022](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2107346118). Maier et al. reanalysed the same data with robust Bayesian meta-analysis and found **no evidence for an overall nudging effect**, and evidence *against* information- and assistance-based interventions [Maier, Bartoš, Stanley, Shanks, Harris & Wagenmakers, PNAS 119(31), 2022](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2200300119). Szaszi et al. add that under a severe-bias assumption, 95% of study effects fall within ±1.00 of d = 0.08 — enormous heterogeneity [Szaszi et al., PNAS 2022](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2200732119). Practical reading: do not design on the assumption that marking an option "Recommended" reliably moves choices by a known amount.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

**Does recommending improve or degrade decision quality?** Both, conditionally, and the asymmetry is measured. Advice-taking research says an expert will *underweight* your recommendation — weight-of-advice 0.20–0.30 in the classic review [Bonaccio & Dalal, OBHDP 101(2):127–151, 2006](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Advice-taking-and-decision-making:-An-integrative-Bonaccio-Dalal/d0d1e850d29d272b32fe96ec72c00b67a4ce168f), 0.39 [0.37, 0.42] in a recent meta-analysis [Bailey et al., meta-analysis of weight of advice, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39711945/). Discounting is *more* pronounced when advice is unsolicited and in low-stakes tasks. That bounds the upside — and the downside is the automation-bias literature: correct clinical decision support cut omission errors 38.3%/46.6%/39.2% across complexity conditions; incorrect support raised them 33.3%/24.5%/26.7% and produced commission-error rates of 65.8%/53.5%/51.7% [Lyell et al., e-prescribing automation-bias experiment, 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5356416/). Skitka et al. found participants *without* an aid outperformed those with a highly-but-imperfectly reliable one on monitoring [Mosier & Skitka line of work, automation bias in high-tech cockpits](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11540946/).

<INFERENCE from="Bonaccio & Dalal WOA 0.2-0.3; Lyell et al. asymmetric omission/commission effects; Krosnick & Alwin primacy in visual lists">A marked recommendation placed first is a bet whose payoff is capped by advice discounting but whose loss is amplified by automation bias and primacy. The design consequence is that the recommendation should carry its evidence inline, so the reader can evaluate the reason rather than the label — the label alone buys little and costs a lot when wrong.</INFERENCE>

**The free-text escape hatch.** Three separate lines converge, and they do not all point the same way. Closed lists miss real categories: Schuman & Presser's central finding is a lack of overlap between respondent-generated and researcher-generated categories even after extensive pretesting, and their "most important problem" experiment showed the closed version drew far more "crime and violence" responses than the open one purely because the option was listed `Schuman & Presser 1979/1981 (unusable citation URL)`. Against that: embedding open-ended probes in closed questions raised break-off, backtracking and answer-changing, though in most cases without altering cognitive processing [Hadler, Sociological Methods & Research, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00491241231176846), and Couper's two Dutch panel experiments found small but significant shifts in item-battery means with probes present. And a *substitute* for choosing — an explicit "no opinion" — fails: across nine experiments in three household surveys, omitting no-opinion options did not compromise data quality on over-time consistency or responsiveness, while attraction to the option was greatest among the least cognitively skilled [Krosnick, Holbrook, Berent et al., POQ 66(3):371–403, 2002](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/66/3/371/1836194).

<INFERENCE from="Schuman & Presser (closed lists miss categories); Krosnick et al. 2002 (escape options invite satisficing); Hadler 2025 (open probes raise breakoff)">An optional note attached to a *selected* option threads these: it recovers the missing-category information without offering a way to avoid committing to a choice, and — unlike a mandatory probe — it does not add response burden to respondents who have nothing to add. This specific configuration has not been experimentally tested.</INFERENCE>

**Decision fatigue — the mechanism named in the brief is not well supported.**

<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>The lab construct failed a 23-laboratory preregistered replication (N = 2,141, d = 0.04), described by the authors as "good evidence of a null ego-depletion effect," with moderate unexplained between-lab heterogeneity [Hagger et al., Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(4):546–573, 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873). The flagship field demonstration — parole approvals falling from ~65% to near zero within each session — was challenged on the grounds that case ordering is not random: the board completes all cases from one prison before a break, and unrepresented prisoners (much less likely to be paroled) tend to be heard at session end [Weinshall-Margel & Shapard, PNAS 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1110910108). The original authors replied that case order and meal break remained robust predictors after controlling for representation [Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso, PNAS 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1112190108). Later reanalyses (Glöckner 2016; Daljord et al. 2017) offered further alternative explanations.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

**Authoring rule:** justify option caps and batching by interruption cost and order bias, both of which are well measured, rather than by decision fatigue, which is not.

#### (4) HCI: interruption cost, batching versus serial prompting, and when interrupting is worth it

**Interruption cost is real but not simply "lost time."** In a controlled simulated-office experiment, interrupted tasks were completed in *less* time with no quality difference — people compensated by working faster, "but at the price of more stress, higher frustration, time pressure and effort"; openness to experience and need for personal structure moderated disruption cost [Mark, Gudith & Klocke, CHI 2008, pp. 107–110](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf). The observational counterpart: 24 information workers, 57% of working spheres interrupted, with more than two intervening activities before resumption [Mark, González & Harris, CHI 2005, pp. 321–330](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/CHI2005.pdf).

**Resumption cost is severe in programming specifically.** Across 10,000 recorded sessions from 86 programmers plus a survey of 414, **only 10% of sessions resumed programming activity in under one minute after an interruption, and only 7% involved no navigation before editing** [Parnin & Rugaber, Software Quality Journal 19:5–34, 2011](https://chrisparnin.me/pdf/parnin-sqj11.pdf). Attention residue compounds this: performance on the next task is worse when the prior task was left incomplete — lower accuracy, slower processing, persisting rather than fading [Leroy, OBHDP 109(2):168–181, 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v109y2009i2p168-181.html). Developer work is already highly fragmented by baseline: 20 professional developers monitored for ~11 full workdays across four companies switched regularly among a wide variety of activities [Meyer, Barton, Murphy, Zimmermann & Fritz, IEEE TSE, 2017](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/2017-meyer.pdf).

**Timing beats frequency.** Interrupting at subtask boundaries rather than mid-subtask reduces disruption to performance, error rate and affective state `Adamczyk & Bailey 2004 (unusable citation URL)`. Deployed as a system, defer-to-breakpoint scheduling reduced frustration and reaction time relative to immediate delivery, and matched users' own stated preferences; coarser breakpoints corresponded to lower interruption cost [Iqbal & Bailey, CHI 2008, pp. 93–102](https://interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-CHI08.pdf).

**Batching beats serial — with a floor.** A two-week randomised field experiment (n = 237) assigned participants to notifications-as-usual, batched, or never. Batching **three times a day** produced more attentiveness, productivity, better mood, greater sense of control, lower stress and fewer perceived interruptions; **hourly batching produced little change versus control**; and *no* notifications raised anxiety and FoMO [Fitz, Kushlev, Jagannathan, Lewis, Paliwal & Ariely, Computers in Human Behavior 101:84–94, 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219302596). Sample was Indian smartphone users over two weeks — generalisation caveats apply.

**Directly in the coding-agent setting**, proactive AI assistance was instrumented in a within-subjects CHI 2025 study (N = 18 CS students, 3 Python tasks, 1,004 logged interaction episodes). Of 398 proactivity instances: **53.3% led to effective engagement, 12.1% were disruptions, 34.7% were ignored**. Perceived disruption differed significantly by condition (Friedman χ² = 22.1, df = 2, p < 0.001), with proactive conditions rated 3.78–4.61 versus 1.56 for user-initiated only. Crucially, three design rationales were tested and only one worked: intervening at **sub-task boundaries** was most effective; inferring intent from implicit signals (comments, selections) produced many false positives; and **inferring low workload from idleness failed outright — idleness often signalled *high* load** (thinking or designing), and users preferred to seek help themselves after failing to resolve something. Disruptions concentrated in implementation (32.7%) and were rare in debugging (7.27%) [Pu, Lazaro, Arawjo, Xia, Xiao, Grossman & Chen, CHI 2025, DOI 10.1145/3706598.3713357](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18658v4).

**Authoring rules:** ask once, at a task boundary, with everything batched; never treat user silence as availability; expect roughly a third of unprompted interventions to be ignored, so an unanswered question needs a non-blocking fallback.

#### (5) Plain language and readability: what simplification actually buys

Simplification **reliably** moves readability metrics and **unreliably** moves comprehension. Rewrites move consent documents from ~11th–12th grade to ~7th–8th grade dependably; one worked example moved Flesch-Kincaid from 12.3 to 8.2 while long sentences fell 15.5% → 7.4% and passive voice 8.6% → 5.3% ["Simplifying informed consent as a universal precaution", Scientific Reports, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-64139-9).

The pattern is consistent and instructive: **rewriting words produces 0–14 points; changing the interaction process produces ~28.** A jury-instruction study reached the same conclusion from the other direction — sentence length had no effect on comprehension while educational attainment did, with conceptual difficulty driving most variance.

Field trials where the outcome is *action* rather than *understanding* show small-to-moderate effects for simplification alone and larger effects when combined with new information or salience: BIT's tax programme advanced over £200m, with single added lines raising payments by ~2% [Behavioural Insights Team Update Report 2013–2015](https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/BIT_Update-Report-Final-2013-2015.pdf); an NHS Health Check invitation combining simplification, behavioural instruction, salience and implementation intentions raised attendance 29.3% → 33.5% (adjusted OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.09–1.47) [enhanced invitation letter quasi-RCT, 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4806508/); a court-fines RCT found texting raised average payment 145% (£4.46 → £10.94) and personalisation added 34% on top (£8.34 → £11.21), across 1,817 then 3,633 subjects [BIT / Ministry of Justice adaptive RCT](https://www.bi.team/publications/collection-of-delinquent-fines-an-adaptive-randomized-trial-to-assess-the-effectiveness-of-alternative-text-messages/).

**Word-count thresholds.** Payne (1951) proposed <20 words; Johnson & Morgan (2016) relax to <25; Peterson (2000) proposes "no more than three commas" as a complexity proxy instead of a word count [MeasuringU synthesis](https://measuringu.com/clearer-questions/). Practitioners are explicitly sceptical that any numeric cutoff identifies brevity well. <MISSING_DATA>No primary source reproducing Payne's exact 1951 phrasing and page reference was located; the 20-word figure travels entirely through secondary methodological literature.</MISSING_DATA>

**Authoring rules:** ≤20 words stem, ≤25 hard cap, ≤3 commas; expect the gain from plain wording to be speed and willingness to answer rather than accuracy; where the *concept* is hard, simplifying the sentence will not fix it — restructure the question or the interaction instead.

#### (6) LLM agents asking clarifying questions: measured benefits, harms, and calibration

This is the fastest-moving and most directly applicable literature, and it has converged on a specific evaluative stance: **binary "did it ask?" is obsolete; the field now scores asking-rate separately on ambiguous versus unambiguous inputs and penalises turn count.**

##### Methodological comparison — clarification benchmarks and interventions

| Work | Setting | Scale | Headline result | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ClarifyGPT [FSE 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3660810) | Code gen; consistency-check trigger | 4 benchmarks; 10 human participants | GPT-4 Pass@1 70.96% → 80.80% (MBPP-san.), +13.87% rel., p = 3.2e-05; +16.83% on MBPP-ET, p = 7.3e-05 | 4 LLM calls per task; simulated feedback for the automated arm |

| CollabLLM [ICML 2025, Outstanding Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00640) | Multiturn RL with future-turn rewards | 201 human judges | +18.5% task performance, +46.3% interactivity, +17.6% satisfaction, **−10.4% user time** | Simulated user in training loop |
| Double-turn preferences [ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13788) | Open-domain QA preference labelling | 3 base LLMs | Consistent 4–5% F1 improvement; learns when *not* to ask | QA setting; simulated interactions |

Several findings from this table deserve to be load-bearing in the skill's design:

**The ask/no-ask conditional is the cleanest available "worth asking" test.** Ask or Assume partitions tasks by whether the agent queried, then compares resolve rates on identical subsets. On the 344 tasks where UA-Multi asked, it scored 65.99% versus Hidden's 44.48% — the information was worth having. On the 156 it declined, it scored 76.92% versus Hidden's 77.56% — the restraint was correct because there was nothing to learn. Note also that the no-ask subset scores *higher* than the ask subset across every condition: agents ask on the tasks that are genuinely harder [Edwards & Schuster 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1).

**Calibration is measurable as an ask-rate gradient against difficulty.** UA-Multi's ask rate climbed 62.37% (<15-min fixes) → 71.65% (15 min–1 hr) → 78.57% (1–4 hrs) → 100% (>4 hrs); the weaker single-agent variant's gradient was 71.13% → 73.56% → 85.71%, a much flatter early slope, and it asked *more overall* while resolving *less* [Edwards & Schuster 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1).

**Question volume and timing distinguish good from bad askers.** UA-Multi: 3.06 questions/task, 171.57 avg question tokens, distributed 41.8% early / 43.4% mid / 14.8% late. The hardcoded interactive baseline: 1.02 questions/task, 251.33 tokens, 97.6% front-loaded. UA-Single: 1.84 questions, and 43.9% *late* — the worst pattern [Edwards & Schuster 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1). Meanwhile Ambig-SWE's highest-volume asker (Qwen 3 Coder, 6.02 q/task) was the one model that got *worse* when given the information it asked for [Ambig-SWE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13069v3).

**Batching within a turn beats serial fragmentation.** ClarEval contrasts GPT-5-Coder consolidating multiple unknowns into one structured turn against Qwen2.5-Coder asking about data type at turn 1 and only realising sort order was needed by turn 3 — attributed not to pacing but to "a failure in global planning." The authors frame excessive questioning as a "cognitive tax," arguing an agent posing ten vague questions is less useful than one asking a single precise question, and explicitly criticise HumanEvalComm for rewarding inefficient questioning [ClarEval 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00187v1).

**Under-asking is the more common frontier-model failure.** Over 60% of Code LLM responses generate code rather than ask when descriptions are made inconsistent, ambiguous or incomplete `HumanEvalComm (unusable citation URL)`; ClarifyMT-Bench found a consistent under-clarification bias across ten models in six families, worsening with dialogue depth [ClarifyMT-Bench, arXiv:2512.21120](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.21120); CLAMBER concluded current LLMs fail to ask high-quality clarifying questions because they cannot assess their own knowledge boundaries [Zhang et al., CLAMBER, ACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.12063). And the human gap remains wide: state-of-the-art models reach ~50–60% success on task-oriented clarification dialog against 80–85% for humans [ClarQ-LLM, 2024](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2409.06097v2).

<INFERENCE from="Knowing but Not Showing (recognition 60-80%, asking ≤5%); Ambig-SWE (FPR up to 0.95 under strong encouragement); Ask or Assume (self-directed scaffold at 3.06 q/task achieves near-Full performance)">Prompting a model to "ask when unsure" is the wrong lever in both directions — it does not close the recognition-to-behaviour gap for models that under-ask, and it induces indiscriminate asking in models that over-comply. The lever that works in the measured systems is a structured pre-ask test (candidate divergence, or expected value of information over specific parameters) that produces a yes/no independent of the model's felt confidence.</INFERENCE>

### Secondary Q2: What is the current state, and what is the strongest supporting evidence for it?

The strongest single evidence chain for the skill's core design is: **detect divergence → batch → ask once → recommend with evidence → keep it short**. Its four strongest supports are ClarifyGPT's consistency-check trigger with a p < 0.0001 human-in-the-loop improvement [FSE 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3660810); Ask or Assume's ask/no-ask conditional decomposition on 500 tasks [arXiv:2603.26233](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1); Fitz et al.'s randomised batching field experiment [CHB 101, 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219302596); and the CHI 2025 proactive-programming probe showing sub-task-boundary timing works and idleness-inference does not [Pu et al., CHI 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18658v4).

### Secondary Q3: What are the contrasting viewpoints or competing evidence?

Four active disputes, each already flagged inline: the cost-to-fix curve (Boehm vs Menzies); choice overload's existence as a main effect (Scheibehenne vs Chernev); nudge efficacy after bias correction (Mertens vs Maier); and question length's effect on reliability (Alwin & Beattie vs the JSSAM review's null). A fifth, quieter one: whether plain language improves comprehension at all for conceptually hard material — the well-powered consent RCTs are mostly null while process interventions are not.

A sixth is emerging within the agent literature itself. ClarEval reports a single-turn/multi-turn correlation of only **r = 0.32** [ClarEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00187v1), meaning an agent good at spotting ambiguity up front is only weakly predictive of one good at resolving it through dialogue. That undercuts the assumption that a single "should I ask?" classifier is sufficient.

### Secondary Q4: What changed recently, and what is the trajectory?

Three shifts since 2024. **First**, evaluation moved from "did it ask?" to calibration-style scoring — ask-rates measured separately on ambiguous and unambiguous inputs, with over-asking penalised via turn-count metrics (ATC, EAR) [ClarEval, Feb 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00187v1). **Second**, the trigger moved from prompting to structure: expected-value-of-perfect-information over tool parameters, uncertainty-weighted RL, and future-turn preference labelling all now outperform prompt-based asking [SAGE, ACL Findings 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08798)[ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13788). **Third**, the objective moved from task success to *collaboration efficiency* — CollabLLM's headline includes a 10.4% reduction in user time alongside its accuracy gain, an explicitly two-sided objective [ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00640). Trajectory: the ask/don't-ask decision is being pulled out of the language model's judgement and into an explicit computed criterion.

## Knowledge Gaps

**Not measured anywhere I could find (design-relevant absences).**
<MISSING_DATA>No study measures the optimal number of *discrete options* in a clarifying question posed by an agent to a human. Every number in the option-count literature is either rating-scale points (graded, different construct) or assortment size in consumer choice (unformed preferences). What would be needed: an A/B trial in a real agent varying 2 vs 3 vs 4 vs 6 options on identical forks, measuring answer latency, answer revision, and downstream task success.</MISSING_DATA>

<MISSING_DATA>No study tests an *optional free-text note attached to a selected option*. The adjacent literatures test standalone "don't know" filters (harmful), mandatory open probes (raise break-off), and open-vs-closed formats (closed lists miss categories). The specific hybrid is untested.</MISSING_DATA>

<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>No large-scale survey quantifies developer preference for clarification *frequency* against interruption tolerance. Evidence splits between small-N HCI studies (N=18 CS students in the CHI 2025 probe) and large-N adoption surveys that never ask the clarification question. The 2025 Stack Overflow survey (49,000+ respondents) covers agent trust and adoption but not this.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>

**Method-limited.** Every agent benchmark above uses an LLM user simulator, and at least two papers flag the same defect: simulated users are "unnaturally cooperative" and miss real-user variability [Edwards & Schuster 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26233v1)[Ambig-SWE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13069v3). Every measured benefit of asking is therefore an upper bound. A real user answers late, partially, or wrongly, and none of these papers price that in.

**Transfer-limited.** The survey and JDM findings were generated on lay respondents with low motivation answering about topics they may not care about. Krosnick's own model says satisficing depends on ability and motivation — both high for an expert answering about their own project. So satisficing-derived rules (short questions, no escape hatch, watch for primacy) are *less* likely to bind, while interruption-derived rules are *more* likely to bind, since the same expert has a high-value task to return to. <CONFIDENCE:LOW>I would expect the word-count rule to be the least load-bearing of the recommendations here, and the batching/timing rule to be the most.</CONFIDENCE:LOW>

## Recommended Next Steps

1. **Run the ask/no-ask conditional test on your own skill, not just on a benchmark.** Ask or Assume's decomposition — partition by whether the agent asked, then compare against a no-information baseline on the same partition — is cheap to implement and is the only method in this literature that distinguishes *correct restraint* from *missed opportunity*. Rationale: it directly answers the "was this question worth asking" criterion the skill needs, and it needs no ground-truth ambiguity labels.

2. **Retrieve and read the full text of "What Prompts Don't Say" (arXiv:2505.13360v3) via the HTML or TeX source.** It is the only work found that quantifies underspecification in *real* prompts rather than synthetically degraded ones, and its 41.1% default-inference rate and 2× regression figure would, if the methodology holds, be the strongest available basis for a "how often should this fire" prior. Rationale: everything else in section (1) is either contested (Boehm, CHAOS) or synthetic (Ambig-SWE issue compression).

3. **Test the option-count question directly, since nobody has.** Vary 2 / 3 / 4 / 6 options on matched forks and measure answer latency, revision rate, and whether the chosen option survives to the merged artifact. Rationale: the current recommendation of 2–4 rests on an inference from order-bias and word-cost, not on a measurement — it is the weakest-supported number in this report.

4. **Instrument the free-text note as an A/B, treating the note text as the outcome.** Measure how often it is used, and — more importantly — how often its content reveals a category none of the offered options covered. Rationale: Schuman & Presser's core finding is that pretesting does not prevent missing categories; the note's value is as a *detector of bad option sets*, which is measurable even if its effect on the immediate answer is null.

5. **Add a timing gate keyed to task boundaries and validate that idleness is not used as a proxy for availability.** The CHI 2025 probe found the idleness heuristic failed outright — idleness often signalled high cognitive load. Rationale: this is the single clearest negative result in the applied literature, it is directly transferable to an agent that must choose *when* to surface a question, and it invalidates the most obvious implementation.
