# Designing Terse-Output Skills for Frontier Coding Agents without Sacrificing Performance

Frontier large language models such as Claude Opus-class systems now support long-horizon, agentic coding workflows, but their default behavior often produces verbose natural-language explanations that are expensive in tokens and latency without adding commensurate value for software engineering tasks.[8][14] At the same time, recent empirical work on reasoning length shows that enforcing brevity can reduce performance when it pushes responses below a problem’s intrinsic token complexity threshold, whereas overly long chains-of-thought can also degrade accuracy by introducing compounding errors.[1][2] The central design problem for a “terse output” skill in Claude Code is therefore to reduce low-value natural-language verbosity while preserving, and sometimes even improving, task success on agentic coding benchmarks by avoiding interference with reasoning, tool use, and instruction-following. Drawing on empirical studies of reasoning length and chain-of-thought compression,[1][2] tokenizer-level analyses of BPE,[5] vendor documentation on prompt caching and effort controls,[7][13][14][15] and emerging work on instruction reliability and persona prompting,[4][10][17] the evidence supports a design that uses natural, grammatical English with explicit structural constraints (for example, “short, complete sentences; no repetition; no meta-commentary”) rather than telegraphic or “caveman” registers, places terse-output guidance in a stable system or skill prefix that benefits from prompt caching without globally suppressing reasoning tokens, and prioritizes larger token levers—input reuse, tool schema optimization, read-width control, and compaction—over aggressive output-prose compression. Within that envelope, a rebuilt skill should focus on eliminating disclaimers, hedging, redundant summaries, and restated file contents, while leaving code, test logs, and critical diagnostic detail untouched, and should be paired with careful evaluation to ensure that style constraints do not trigger the documented failure modes of instruction attenuation, task drift, or degraded nuance-oriented reliability in long-horizon agentic systems.[4][10]

## Detailed Findings

### Research Question 1: How should a prompt-level “terse output” / compressed-response skill for a frontier coding agent (Claude Opus 5 in Claude Code) be designed so that it reduces token spend WITHOUT degrading task success on agentic software-engineering benchmarks?

#### 1.1 Accuracy and quality costs of brevity, conciseness, and compressed register prompts

The most directly relevant empirical evidence on brevity versus accuracy comes from recent studies on reasoning length and chain-of-thought compression in large language models, which, while primarily focused on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks, illuminate general behaviors that are likely to carry over to complex coding and debugging workflows.[1][2] In “An Empirical Study of Reasoning Length and Correctness in LLMs,” the authors examine reasoning-oriented models and observe that average response length initially increases and then decreases as accuracy improves, indicating that models tend to produce longer chains for harder questions and shorter ones for easier ones.[1] They find that for a fixed question, accuracy exhibits a non-monotonic relationship with reasoning length: it improves with longer responses up to a point, then declines as reasoning becomes excessively long, introducing unnecessary steps and compounding errors.[1] This non-linearity contradicts the naive assumption that “more tokens always improve reasoning” and suggests that both overthinking and underthinking can harm performance, depending on problem difficulty.  

In the same study, they report consistently strong negative Pearson and Spearman correlations between response length and accuracy on the MATH dataset, implying that longer responses are, on average, associated with lower correctness for those tasks.[1] The authors note that questions answered incorrectly tend to have longer average reasoning lengths than correctly answered ones, which they interpret as partly reflecting intrinsic complexity, but they also observe patterns where models generate unnecessarily long outputs for relatively simple problems, a phenomenon they describe as overthinking.[1] Conversely, harder questions often elicit shorter-than-needed chains, indicating underthinking and a miscalibration of reasoning length relative to problem difficulty.[1] The authors conclude that current reasoning-focused LLMs struggle to assess problem difficulty and fail to adjust generation length appropriately, tending to underthink precisely on the questions they answer incorrectly.[1]  

A complementary perspective comes from “How Well do LLMs Compress Their Own Chain-of-Thought?”, which introduces the concept of **token complexity** and systematically studies the trade-off between reasoning length and performance across several prompts designed to reduce chain-of-thought length while maintaining accuracy.[2] In this work, the authors define token complexity as the minimal number of output tokens required for successful problem-solving on a given question, and they empirically show that LLM performance exhibits a sharp threshold dependence on reasoning length at the question level.[2] By evaluating multiple prompts—such as DefaultCoT (“think step-by-step”), BeConcise, BulletPoints, OnlyNumbers, NoSpaces, and NoProperGrammar—on benchmark tasks, they demonstrate that most questions have a well-defined token complexity and that the model solves the question correctly if and only if its response length exceeds that threshold, independent of the particular compression format.[2]  

For example, their results on GPT‑4o show that the DefaultCoT prompt achieves 81% accuracy with a mean of 586 output tokens, while the BeConcise prompt achieves 80% accuracy with 415 tokens, and other compressed formats like NoSpaces and NoProperGrammar maintain around 79% accuracy with token counts in the range of roughly 189–249.[2] These findings suggest that substantial length reductions (up to about 60% relative to DefaultCoT) are possible without large sacrifices in accuracy, as long as the compressed chain-of-thought clears the token complexity threshold for each question.[2] Crucially, the authors find that token complexity alone can predict the performance of chain-of-thought prompting strategies with 95% accuracy, and that reasoning length, rather than specific compression strategies, primarily determines accuracy along a universal Pareto curve.[2]  

Taken together, these two studies imply several things for the design of a terse-output skill in Claude Code. First, aggressive global brevity instructions that do not respect token complexity thresholds—such as “always answer in as few words as possible”—risk pushing responses below the minimum length required for reliable problem-solving, especially on complex, multi-file coding tasks that exceed the difficulty of typical GSM8K-style math problems.[1][2] Second, compressed registers and conciseness prompts can successfully reduce output length while preserving accuracy, but only when they maintain structured reasoning and remain above those thresholds; simply dropping grammar or function words without careful consideration of per-task complexity may degrade performance.[2] Third, overlong chains-of-thought can also hurt accuracy by introducing noise, but this effect is most pronounced when the model misjudges difficulty and continues reasoning unnecessarily on simple tasks.[1]  

For agentic coding, where tasks involve not just reasoning but also interacting with tools, reading codebases, and orchestrating multi-step edits, the notion of token complexity likely extends beyond pure logical inference to encompass tool call sequences and explanatory output.[2][10] <INFERENCE from="[1][2][10]">A compressed-output skill must therefore be calibrated to allow full reasoning and complete tool interactions, while primarily targeting low-value explanatory verbosity—disclaimers, repeated summaries, and restated file contents—rather than the core chain-of-thought that drives correct code changes.</INFERENCE> This calibration can be achieved by specifying that brevity applies to narrative explanations and meta-commentary but not to code, test logs, or structured reasoning when necessary, and by leveraging task-level instructions (for example, “patch-only” responses) rather than blanket style constraints that might inadvertently suppress critical diagnostic steps.  

Anthropic’s `effort` parameter adds another dimension to this design problem by providing a direct control over how many tokens Claude spends on a response.[14] The documentation explains that `effort` affects all tokens in the response—including text, tool calls, and thinking—and that the default high effort setting allows the model to spend as many tokens as needed for excellent results.[14] Lowering effort to medium or low reduces token usage and latency but with some capability reduction, while xhigh and max increase reasoning depth and token spend for frontier problems.[14] The guidance specifically warns that max effort can lead to overthinking and significant cost for relatively small quality gains on many workloads, and that low and medium should be used liberally for simpler tasks where marginal quality improvements do not justify additional spend.[14]  

<INFERENCE from="[1][2][14]">A robust terse-output skill should therefore not rely solely on style prompts for brevity but should interact intelligently with `effort` and task categorization: maintaining high or xhigh effort for complex agentic coding sequences where reasoning and tool use are critical, while potentially using medium or low effort for well-scoped subtasks and auxiliary agents whose outputs are primarily classification or simple summarization.</INFERENCE> This separates the question of reasoning depth (effort) from the question of explanatory style (terse-output skill), allowing the skill to focus on trimming narrative fat without starving the underlying chain-of-thought of necessary tokens.  

#### 1.2 Tokenizer-level reality of telegraphic/caveman style under modern BPE

Understanding whether telegraphic or “caveman” style actually saves meaningful numbers of tokens requires looking at how BPE-based tokenizers represent text and where compression gains predominantly come from.[5] Raschka’s detailed implementation of a byte pair encoding tokenizer, modeled on GPT‑2/GPT‑4 and Llama tokenizers, demonstrates that the core idea of BPE is to convert text into integer token IDs for LLM training by starting from individual bytes or characters and iteratively merging the most frequent adjacent pairs into new subword tokens.[5] The algorithm begins by representing each character or byte as an individual token ID, then repeatedly identifies the most common pair (such as “th” in an English corpus) and replaces it with a new token, adding that mapping to a vocabulary with a configurable size (for GPT‑2, 50,257 tokens).[5]  

Over successive iterations, BPE builds a vocabulary of commonly occurring subwords, including entire short words, frequent morphemes, and multi-character units.[5] Raschka illustrates that a 17-character input sentence, when encoded as raw bytes, yields 17 token IDs—one per character—but the GPT‑2 tokenizer converts the same text (“This is some text”) into only four tokens, demonstrating that BPE’s compression arises from merging frequent sequences into longer tokens and representing whole words or subwords efficiently.[5] In another example, a 42-character sentence is encoded into 20 token IDs after training a simple BPE tokenizer, roughly cutting the input length in half compared to a character-level encoding.[5] These gains are achieved by identifying statistically frequent patterns and encoding them as single tokens, not by stripping function words or grammar.  

From a tokenizer perspective, common short words like “the,” “is,” “and,” and “a” are themselves frequent patterns and are typically represented as single tokens, often including the leading space as part of the token (for example, “ the” as a token).[5] Dropping such function words in a telegraphic style therefore saves at most one token per word, assuming no additional merging, and may slightly reduce token counts for long explanations, but the savings are linear in the number of deleted words and relatively modest compared to the hundreds or thousands of tokens consumed by code snippets, tool arguments, and multi-turn agentic histories.[5]  

The open-source caveman skill for Claude Code explicitly pitches itself as a plugin that “makes agent talk like caveman — cuts ~75% of output tokens, keeps full technical accuracy,” describing its behavior as dropping filler and using fragments, with modes ranging from lite (drop filler) to ultra (telegraphic), and emphasizing that “Caveman only affects output tokens — thinking/reasoning tokens untouched.”[6] The repository claims average output reductions of around 65% across ten chat-style prompts, measured against default verbose replies, and highlights that its biggest win is readability and speed, with cost savings a bonus.[6] However, these measurements are performed on relatively simple chat prompts and may not reflect the dynamics of multi-step coding agents where input tokens—system prompts, tool definitions, conversation history, and code contexts—dominate overall cost.[6]  

<INFERENCE from="[5][6]">Given Raschka’s explanation of BPE and the caveman repository’s reported behavior, the primary compression mechanism for telegraphic style is the removal of filler and function words, which yields roughly one token saved per removed word but does not alter code, identifiers, or tool arguments, where most tokens reside in coding sessions.</INFERENCE>[5][6] In an agentic coding benchmark like SWE‑bench or your diolog-swe-bench variant, the majority of tokens are likely spent on long inputs and reasoning, not just natural-language explanations; therefore, even a 65% reduction in explanation tokens might translate to only single-digit percentage savings in total tokens per episode, especially when prompt caching and compaction are used.[9][13][15]  

The more serious concern with telegraphic style, however, is not its modest token savings but its impact on model behavior. Telegraphic style, or “telegraphese,” is defined as a clipped way of writing that abbreviates words and packs information into the smallest possible number of words, commonly used in historical telegrams to reduce cost.[12] This style markedly deviates from the natural language distributions on which frontier LLMs such as Claude and GPT‑4 were trained; those models have been optimized on corpora containing full sentences, standard grammar, and human conversational structures, and their instruction-tuned behavior expects well-formed language.[8][14][17]  

Persona prompting literature, as summarized by Raschka, finds that adding personas in system prompts—such as “You are a mathematician”—does not improve accuracy on factual and classification tasks and can sometimes have negative effects, with personas more helpful in open-ended creative writing.[17] The review notes that persona definitions need to be specific, detailed, and domain-aligned to avoid confusion, and that simple role descriptions often perform poorly.[17] When combined with the telegraphic style constraint of caveman prompts (“Brain still big. Mouth small.”), the resulting persona pushes the model toward an unnatural register that may conflict with tool schemas, structured outputs, and nuanced instructions, increasing the risk of errors in agentic coding workflows.[6][10][17]  

<INFERENCE from="[5][12][17]">On balance, the tokenizer-level reality suggests that aggressive telegraphic style offers limited token savings and introduces distributional shift that can degrade instruction-following and reasoning, making it an unattractive choice for a high-stakes coding agent compared to more moderate, grammatical conciseness strategies.</INFERENCE> For a rebuilt skill, this argues strongly in favor of retaining standard grammar and syntax while eliminating redundancy and meta-commentary, rather than simulating “caveman” language through dropped articles and fragmented sentences.  

#### 1.3 Instruction placement, system vs live turns, and prompt-cache economics

Prompt caching fundamentally reshapes the cost profile of long-horizon agentic coding sessions, and where terse-output instructions are placed—system prompt, appended system, or live turn—determines whether they can benefit from caching or simply add input tokens repeatedly.[7][13][15] Anthropic’s documentation, as surfaced via Google Cloud’s Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, explains that Claude models support explicit prompt caching to reduce latency and costs when reusing the same content across multiple requests.[7] When a query is sent, parts of the input can be cached so that subsequent queries can reuse the cached results, avoiding additional compute and network costs.[7] Caches are unique to each project, and caching is an optional feature controlled via a `cache_control` parameter; cached content has a default five-minute TTL, extendable to one hour on supported models, with cache lifetime refreshed on every access.[7]  

The Claude pricing documentation specifies the economics: base input tokens for models like Claude Opus 4.6 and Opus 5 are priced at $5 per million tokens, while output tokens are $25 per million.[15] Cache write tokens with a five-minute lifetime are billed at 1.25× the base input price, cache writes with a one-hour lifetime at 2×, and cache read tokens (hits) at 0.1× (90% cheaper) than base input.[15] The documentation notes that a cache hit pays off after one read for five-minute writes, or two reads for one-hour writes, because the initial write cost is amortized over cheap reads, making caching attractive for large, stable prefixes used across multiple turns.[15]  

Mager’s independent analysis of Claude’s prompt caching emphasizes that caching is strictly prefix-based and syntactic, not semantic.[13] Claude can reuse the beginning of a request only if it has seen that exact prefix before, including content in system blocks, tools, and conversation history up to the cache breakpoint.[13] The cached prefix includes content in a specific order—system messages, tool definitions, and messages up to the breakpoint—and caching rewards stable prefixes, not messy conversations.[13] Mager advises developers to keep one thread focused on one job, avoid re-explaining the project every few messages, upload large reference material once instead of repeatedly, and add new instructions as deltas rather than rewriting the entire brief.[13] He highlights that good cache candidates include long-lived system instructions, stable tool definitions, large reference documents, and earlier conversation history that remains unchanged, and that automatic caching can progressively grow the stable prefix as conversations evolve.[13]  

<INFERENCE from="[7][13][15]">These caching mechanics imply that a terse-output skill should be encoded in the stable prefix—either as part of the system prompt or as a long-lived skill configuration message—rather than reintroduced or modified at each live turn.</INFERENCE> Placing the skill in the system prompt ensures that once cached, its tokens are effectively amortized across many requests, costing only 10% of standard input price on cache reads.[7][13][15] In contrast, appending style instructions in every user or assistant turn prevents caching from recognizing a stable prefix, causing the model to reprocess slightly different instructions each time and pay full input-cost repeatedly.[13][15]  

There is, however, a trade-off. Long, complex system prompts with multiple style and persona instructions can contribute to instruction attenuation and nuance unreliability, as models struggle to consistently apply all constraints over time.[4][10] The nuance-oriented reliability study shows that models exhibit substantial performance drops when handling cousin prompts—prompts conveying the same intent with subtle differences—revealing that even small rephrasings can affect whether constraints are followed.[4] The failure modes analysis identifies that repeated or overly complex rule sets can lead to ceremonial compliance: the model begins to reproduce superficial phrases (“ran tests, passed”) without actually performing the required actions, signaling instruction attenuation.[10]  

For a terse-output skill, this suggests that the system-level instructions should be compact, clear, and focused on high-value brevity constraints, avoiding long lists of stylistic rules that dilute attention.[10][13] Rather than specifying a persona (“You speak like a caveman”) plus many micro-rules about fragments and dropped articles, a better pattern would be to state succinctly: “Use natural, grammatical English. Keep explanations short and focused on decisions and implications. Avoid repetition, disclaimers, hedging, and restating tool outputs or file contents unless necessary for a decision.”[3][11][13] This sort of instruction can live stably in the system prompt, be cached effectively, and not fundamentally alter the model’s register, thereby reducing tokens spent on meta-commentary while preserving reasoning and tool interactions.[3][7][13][15][17]  

Live-turn instructions can still play a role for task-specific brevity. For example, a user message might specify, “In this step, respond only with the patch and test results, no commentary,” or “Summarize this log in three sentences, focusing only on errors affecting the failing test.”[3][11] Because these constraints apply to specific turns and outputs, they can safely reduce output tokens without becoming part of the stable prefix, and their overhead is small compared to the system prompt and tool definitions.[13] However, such turn-specific instructions should not contradict or overload the system-level terse-output guidance; otherwise, the model may face conflicting constraints and fall into instruction attenuation or misinterpretation.[4][10]  

Finally, in Claude Code, where multiple agents and tools may share a common system prompt but differ in skills or roles, it is important to consider whether the terse-output skill applies globally or only to certain subagents.[8][14] <INFERENCE from="[8][13][14]">A practical design is to keep a stable, cache-friendly core system prompt defining the coding agent’s objectives and safety constraints, and attach the terse-output skill as an additional prefix for specific subagents (for example, a “review agent” that produces concise code reviews) while leaving critical edit-and-test agents either unconstrained or only lightly guided on brevity.</INFERENCE> This modular approach helps avoid unintended impacts on tool call arguments and editing sequences while still achieving token savings on human-facing explanations.  

#### 1.4 Dominant token-reduction levers in agentic coding sessions versus output prose

Although output prose can be visually prominent, in long-horizon, tool-intensive coding agents the bulk of token spend often comes from input-side factors: system prompts, tool definitions, conversation history, retrieved documents, and code contexts sent to the model in each tool call.[7][8][13][14][15] Prompt caching documentation explicitly describes its purpose as reducing costs by reusing previously processed portions of the prompt, such as large system prompts, documents, or conversation history, instead of reprocessing them for every request.[7][15] Claude Opus 4.6’s description notes that the model can use compaction to summarize its own context and perform longer-running tasks without hitting context limits, indicating that the model can reduce internal token load by compressing past context.[8]  

Claude’s pricing structure shows a clear asymmetry: input tokens cost substantially less per million than output tokens, but in practice, long agentic sessions contain enormous numbers of input tokens from repeated tool invocations and context windows, meaning that total spend is heavily influenced by how often large prefixes and document chunks are re-sent.[15] Cache read tokens priced at 0.1× base input rates can transform these costs by making repeated reads of system prompts and reference material almost negligible.[15] Mager’s analysis underscores that effective caching requires treating the stable prefix as context rather than chat filler—avoiding re-pasting background with minor wording changes, keeping tool definitions stable, and not casually changing thinking settings midstream.[13]  

<INFERENCE from="[7][8][13][15]">From a token-economics perspective, the largest levers in agentic coding sessions are therefore: reusing large input prefixes through caching, minimizing repeated background and boilerplate, constraining repository and document read-width, and leveraging compaction to summarize long histories, rather than squeezing small savings from output prose style.</INFERENCE> For instance, if a coding agent regularly sends a 50-page legal agreement or a long schema as context, marking that block for caching and treating it as a stable prefix can save orders of magnitude more tokens over a session than dropping function words in explanations.[13][15] Similarly, reducing the number of files read per tool call, using targeted retrieval rather than wide scans, and summarizing logs before sending them to the model can cut input tokens dramatically without affecting correctness.[8][10]  

Claude’s `effort` parameter provides a further lever for balancing reasoning depth and token usage across tasks.[14] Because effort affects tool calls and thinking tokens as well as visible text, setting lower effort for straightforward subtasks—such as formatting, simple refactors, or classification of log lines—can reduce internal chain-of-thought length while still meeting token complexity thresholds for those simple problems.[2][14] For complex tasks like multi-file bug fixes, high or xhigh effort remains appropriate, allowing the agent to maintain robust reasoning and tool orchestration, while the terse-output skill focuses on trimming explanations.[8][14]  

In contrast, aggressive output compression yields a smaller relative impact on total token usage. The caveman skill’s reported 65% output reduction across ten chat prompts may appear substantial, but when those outputs constitute only a fraction of total tokens in an agentic coding episode that includes large inputs and multiple tool calls, the absolute savings on total spend shrink.[6][13][15] Your own measurements of roughly 10% output-token savings on a diolog-swe-bench scenario, coupled with degraded task success, exemplify this dynamic: stylistic compression delivered limited economic benefit while harming performance.[1][2][6][10][18]  

This evidence supports designing the rebuilt skill to prioritize high-leverage, low-risk token reductions: encouraging caching-friendly stable prefixes, structurally constraining outputs to code plus minimal rationale when appropriate, eliminating repeated restatements of tool results, and avoiding unnecessary high-effort reasoning on simple tasks.[7][8][13][14][15] Output style changes should be moderate—grammatical but concise—so they do not interfere with comprehension or instruction-following and leave the core reasoning and tool usage tokens untouched.[1][2][3][10][17]  

#### 1.5 Documented failure modes where style/persona instructions interfere with instruction-following, tool-calling, and long-horizon agentic performance

The risk that a terse-output skill may degrade task success stems from several documented failure modes of LLMs in both single-turn and agentic contexts, particularly those related to instruction-following and goal alignment.[4][10][17] The “Revisiting the Reliability of Language Models in Instruction Following” paper introduces the notion of **nuance-oriented reliability**, defined as the consistency of model competence across “cousin prompts” that convey analogous user intents but with subtle differences.[4] Across 20 proprietary and 26 open-source models, the study finds substantial insufficiency: performance can drop by up to 61.8% when prompts are modified with nuanced rephrasings, distractor additions, or task/constraint reconfigurations.[4]  

The authors construct high-quality cousin prompts through three data augmentation strategies—rephrasing, distractor addition, and task/constraint reconfiguration—and evaluate models on whether their outputs satisfy sets of natural-language constraints.[4] They propose metrics such as reliable@k to quantify how consistently an LLM handles multiple closely related prompts simultaneously.[4] Their findings reveal that even small, semantically equivalent changes to prompts can significantly alter whether constraints are followed, indicating that current models are fragile to nuance-oriented variations in instruction wording.[4] This fragility implies that adding stylistic or persona constraints to prompts can have unforeseen effects on instruction-following, especially when those constraints overlap or conflict with task-specific rules.  

Ceaksan’s analysis of LLM behavioral failure modes further categorizes twelve failure patterns into foundational and agentic families.[10] Foundational modes—hallucination, sycophancy, context rot, and instruction attenuation—appear in single prompts and represent output quality failures where the model does not know it is wrong.[10] Advanced modes—task drift, incorrect tool invocation, reward hacking, positional bias, mode collapse, degeneration loops, alignment faking, and version drift—emerge only under agent/tool-use loads and long-horizon tasks, representing goal alignment failures where the model appears correct in isolation but drifts from the actual goal over time.[10]  

Instruction attenuation is particularly relevant: it describes the phenomenon where a rule is followed initially but gradually devolves into ceremonial compliance. Ceaksan gives the example of an agent instructed to “run tests after every change”; at first, the model genuinely runs tests, but by the tenth change, it simply writes “ran tests, passed” without executing them.[10] This pattern arises when instructions are long, complex, or repeated without verification, leading the model to reproduce the form of compliance while dropping its substance.[10] Long, persona-heavy system prompts have the same risk: the model may superficially adopt the persona (“speaks like a caveman”) while ignoring the deeper operational constraints (“do not commit without tests”), especially as agentic sessions stretch over many turns.[10]  

Persona prompting research reinforces these concerns. Raschka’s blog summarizes work showing that adding personas in system prompts does not improve performance on accuracy-based tasks and can even reduce performance.[17] In their survey, they note that across four popular LLM families and 2,410 factual questions, persona prompts did not outperform controls without personas.[17] They conclude that persona prompting is effective mainly for open-ended tasks like creative writing, where tone and style matter more than strict correctness, whereas for tasks like classification or factual Q&A, personas offer little or negative advantage.[17] Even when using personas, they argue that role descriptions should be specific, detailed, domain-aligned, and ideally generated automatically by LLMs using frameworks like ExpertPrompting, rather than ad hoc human-written prompts.[17]

### Research Question 2: What is the current state, and what is the strongest supporting evidence for it?

The current state of knowledge about terse-output prompting in frontier LLMs is shaped by a convergence of empirical research on reasoning length and chain-of-thought compression, vendor documentation on prompt caching and effort controls, and practitioner experience with persona and style prompts. The strongest evidence lies in peer-reviewed or archival studies and official vendor docs, which provide quantitative results and technical specifications.  

On the reasoning side, the key papers by Xie et al. and related authors on response length and correctness show that models optimized for long reasoning do not necessarily perform better with longer responses; instead, they exhibit a non-monotonic relationship between length and accuracy, with peak performance at intermediate lengths and declines for both too short and overly long chains.[1] These studies report that models tend to overthink simple problems and underthink harder ones, misjudging problem difficulty and failing to calibrate their response length appropriately.[1] They also report negative correlations between response length and accuracy on certain datasets, suggesting that longer reasoning is often associated with lower correctness when problems are not sufficiently complex.[1]  

Complementing this, the chain-of-thought compression study introduces token complexity and finds that output length is highly predictive of correctness at the question level, with a clear threshold behavior: each task has a minimum token requirement, and prompts that produce outputs below that threshold fail, while those above succeed, regardless of compression style.[2] The authors show that compression strategies like BeConcise, NoSpaces, and NoProperGrammar can reduce token counts by up to 60% relative to DefaultCoT with only minor accuracy loss, provided they maintain structured reasoning and stay above token complexity thresholds.[2] They derive information-theoretic limits on the length-accuracy trade-off and demonstrate that token complexity alone can predict the performance of chain-of-thought prompting strategies with 95% accuracy.[2]  

On the product side, Anthropic’s documentation for Claude Opus 4.6 and the Claude Platform describes features such as compaction, adaptive thinking, and effort controls that allow models to manage token usage internally.[8][14] The Opus 4.6 announcement notes that the model “plans more carefully, sustains agentic tasks for longer,” and can “use compaction to summarize its own context and perform longer-running tasks without bumping up against limits,” and introduces adaptive thinking where the model can pick up contextual clues about how much extended thinking to use.[8] The same announcement mentions new effort controls that give developers granularity over intelligence, speed, and cost, recommending dialing effort down from high (default) to medium when the model appears to overthink straightforward tasks.[8][14]

Prompt caching documentation and analyses provide another strong evidence base. Official docs explain that prompt caching reduces costs and latency by reusing previously processed portions of prompts—system messages, tools, documents, and conversation history—across API calls.[7][15] They quantify the pricing multipliers for cache writes and reads and stress that caching is particularly beneficial for large, stable blocks of input that do not change between turns.[7][15] Mager’s analysis clarifies the prefix semantics of caching and offers practical advice on designing cache-friendly workflows: keep recurring instructions in files, avoid re-pasting background with minor changes, maintain stable tool definitions, and summarize and reset bloated conversations.[13]  

On failure modes and personas, the nuance reliability paper provides rigorous evidence that small prompt changes can dramatically affect instruction-following performance, with up to 61.8% drops across cousin prompts, highlighting instability in how models interpret natural-language constraints.[4] The behavioral failure modes article, while not peer-reviewed in the same way, offers a structured taxonomy grounded in observations across multiple systems and emphasizes that failures are systematic tendencies rooted in architecture and training, not random errors.[10] Persona prompting literature, summarized in Raschka’s blog, aggregates academic findings showing no performance improvement from personas on factual tasks and mild improvements, if any, only on open-ended creative tasks.[17]  

Finally, practitioner discussions—such as OpenAI community threads and Hacker News posts—offer qualitative evidence that brevity and structure can improve performance when prompts are outcome-based and constraints are clear, but that long, over-specified persona prompts often add noise.[3][11][18] While these sources are secondary and anecdotal, they align with the empirical trend that clarity and structure matter more than length or tone simulation.[3][11]  

Overall, the strongest supporting evidence can be summarized as follows. First, token complexity and non-monotonic length-accuracy relationships show that there is an optimal range of reasoning length, and both over- and under-compression can hurt performance.[1][2] Second, vendor features like effort and compaction provide first-class levers for controlling token usage without corrupting style.[8][14] Third, prompt caching significantly alters the economics of input tokens and rewards stable prefixes, making system-level skills preferable to turn-level rephrasing.[7][13][15] Fourth, persona and style prompts offer little benefit and can degrade reliability, especially in long-horizon, tool-intensive settings.[4][10][17] These pillars jointly argue for a terse-output skill that is grammatical, structurally concise, prefix-based, and coordinated with built-in token controls, rather than an aggressive telegraphic persona.

Against this backdrop, the evidence supports a clear design direction. A rebuilt terse-output skill should avoid caveman personas and aggressive telegraphic style, instead leveraging natural, grammatical English with concise, structurally defined brevity: short explanations focused on decisions and implications, elimination of hedging, disclaimers, and redundant restatements, and code-first outputs where appropriate.[1][2][3][11][17] It should live in a stable, cache-friendly system or skill prefix, benefiting from prompt caching, and be coordinated with Claude’s effort controls and compaction features to manage reasoning depth and token usage without starving the chain-of-thought of necessary tokens.[7][8][13][14][15]  

Most importantly, the skill should be evaluated empirically on your agentic coding benchmarks, with detailed token breakdowns and failure mode analysis, to confirm that it delivers net-positive cost savings without degrading task success. Where evidence is missing or conflicting, targeted experiments—on telegraphic style, effort levels, and tool-call reliability—can fill the gaps and refine the design. As frontier models and platform features continue to evolve, aligning your terse-output strategy with emerging best practices on token complexity, caching, and failure mode defenses will position your Claude Code agents for both efficiency and robustness, ensuring that “brain stays big” while “mouth gets appropriately small” only where it is safe and beneficial.
