# Constructing Robust Remaining-Work Determinations from Partial Verification Evidence

A partially completed verification campaign, with many checks blocked, inconclusive, or carried forward and requirements largely self-reported rather than independently observed, creates a systematic risk that unmeasured work silently disappears from status views and is misread as “done.”(High Confidence)[10][12][14] Drawing on safety-critical standards such as DO-178C/DO-330, ISO 26262, IEC 62304, FDA software validation guidance, ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 and ECSS/NASA software safety practice, the central finding is that a robust “remaining work” determination must be requirements-centric, explicitly multi-valued in its verification status vocabulary, traceability-complete from each requirement to all associated tests and defects, and gated by formal exit conditions that only permit three terminal states for any obligation: objectively verified, formally waived/deviated with documented risk acceptance, or explicitly descope/deletion of the requirement itself.(High Confidence)[1][3][8][10][12][16] All other states—including blocked, inconclusive, carried-forward, untested, unverifiable-with-current-means, or solely self-reported—must be treated as forms of “unknown” and thus as remaining work, with mandatory denominator disclosure that makes visible the gap between the planned verification space and what has actually been executed.(High Confidence)[10][12][15][19] A remaining-work ledger driven by these principles will prevent unmeasured items from vanishing, will surface testability gaps as new work items rather than silent omissions, and will support an AI agent skill that reconciles feature briefs, PRDs, and test registries into an honest, ranked view of what is truly left to do.(Medium Confidence)<INFERENCE from="[1][3][10][12][15][17][19]">This synthesis generalizes safety-critical practice to a broader verification-planning context and proposes gating rules that an automated agent can implement.</INFERENCE>  

## Executive Overview and Context

The core problem addressed in this report is how to construct and gate a “remaining work” determination when the evidence base consists of a partially executed verification campaign, including numerous blocked or inconclusive tests, carried-forward checks, and requirements whose satisfaction is primarily asserted by developers rather than observed through independent tests or reviews.(High Confidence)[10][12][14][15] In such circumstances, traditional project status reporting—often dominated by aggregate percentages and coarse traffic-light indicators—systematically understates residual risk and obscures the true proportion of requirements that remain unverified in any objective sense.(Medium Confidence)[13][19] Safety-critical software standards have spent decades confronting analogous problems, and they offer detailed taxonomies of requirement and test status, mandated artefacts such as requirements traceability matrices and deviation registers, and explicit criteria for when an unverified requirement may be closed, deferred, or waived; those structures can be adapted to modern AI-enabled verification planning even in non-regulated domains.(High Confidence)[1][3][8][9][10][12][16]  

In regulatory contexts such as aviation, automotive, medical devices, and space missions, a requirement is not treated as “done” merely because code has been written, a developer asserts compliance, or some tests have been executed; rather, it is considered fulfilled only when there is clear traceability from system and safety requirements through software requirements to design, implementation, and verification evidence, with all applicable tests executed and evaluated, residual anomalies understood and accepted, and any deviations handled through a formal waiver process.(High Confidence)[1][3][8][9][10][12][16] These standards also insist on documenting when verification is impossible or incomplete, whether due to testability limitations, missing instrumentation, or environmental constraints, and on turning those limitations into explicit work items and risk decisions rather than allowing them to remain invisible.(High Confidence)[8][10][12][17] The empirical software engineering literature further shows that metric-driven reporting—especially code coverage and coarse “percent complete” indicators—can be gamed, exhibit Goodhart effects, and correlate poorly with actual defect rates, which implies that an AI agent should treat such metrics as diagnostic signals rather than primary gating criteria for remaining work.(Medium Confidence)[17][18][19]  

This executive context motivates a specific design for a remaining-work ledger and gating logic: a requirements-centric taxonomy of verification status based on multi-valued logic; explicit handling of “unknown” outcomes such as blocked or inconclusive tests; honest coverage denominators that distinguish between the planned verification space, executed checks, and the underlying behavioural space; and a discipline of deriving new testability-focused work items from any verification gap that cannot be closed with existing means.(High Confidence)[1][3][10][12][15][17] The remainder of the report develops this design in detail, anchored in specific standards and empirical findings, and then maps it to the needs of an AI agent skill that reconciles feature briefs, PRDs, and a machine-readable test campaign registry into a ranked, non-silent ledger of what is actually remaining.(Medium Confidence)<INFERENCE from="[1][3][5][8][10][12][14][15][17][19]">The recommended design is not itself a regulation but is constructed as a conservative envelope of safety-critical practice that can be scaled down for non-regulated contexts.</INFERENCE>

#### DO-178C and Requirements-Based Verification

#### ISO 26262: Confirmation Measures, Verification, and Independence

ISO 26262:2018, the functional safety standard for road vehicles, provides a rich taxonomy of verification and confirmation measures that distinguish between conformance to the standard and satisfaction of project-specific requirements.(High Confidence)[3][9] Quidditytech’s analysis explains that the standard divides development into twelve parts, with Parts 2–9 focused on safety lifecycle development and work products; to show completion of specified requirements, ISO 26262 introduces confirmation measures—confirmation reviews, functional safety audits, and functional safety assessments—performed to judge functional safety achieved by the item or element and the contribution of work products to that safety.(High Confidence)[9] This source is suitable under the discipline criteria because it is a technical explainer of the ISO standard, referencing specific clauses and tables, rather than a promotional piece.(Medium Confidence)[9]  

ISO 26262 distinguishes confirmation review and verification review in ways that matter for remaining-work gating.(High Confidence)[9] A confirmation review is defined as confirming that a work product provides sufficient and convincing evidence of its contribution to achieving functional safety, considering the corresponding ISO 26262 objectives and requirements, and it is required for nine key work products, with independence levels governed by the Automotive Safety Integrity Level (ASIL) and documented in Table 1 of Part 2 clause 6.4.9.(High Confidence)[9] A verification review, by contrast, is a “verification activity to ensure that the result of a development activity fulfills the project requirements, or technical requirements, or both,” and its reviewer must be different from the author but does not have ASIL-governed independence levels.(High Confidence)[9] ISO 26262 allows combining confirmation and verification reviews provided that independence is sufficient for confirmation per the ASIL; this combination is often used to avoid duplicated review activities.(Medium Confidence)[9]  

For remaining-work determination, ISO 26262 implies that a requirement or safety concept cannot be closed purely on the basis of internal project requirements satisfaction; its closure must also withstand confirmation that the work product adheres to ISO 26262 objectives, particularly for high ASIL levels.(High Confidence)[9] In terms of verification status, this effectively creates multiple layers: a requirement may be “verified against project requirements” yet still “open” from a functional safety confirmation perspective if evidence is not convincing or independence criteria are not met.(Medium Confidence)<INFERENCE from="[3][9]">Thus, a robust ledger should track both technical verification status and confirmation status.</INFERENCE> ISO 26262’s explicit independence requirements also respond to the issue of self-reported verification; they prevent a developer from both writing a feature and single-handedly declaring its safety verified, especially for higher ASIL items.(High Confidence)[9]

### Mandated Artefacts and Criteria for Closing or Deferring Unverified Requirements

Across DO-178C, ISO 26262, IEC 62304, FDA guidance, ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119, and ECSS/NASA, there is a consistent reliance on specific artefacts that provide structured traceability and status visibility; these artefacts in turn define what must be present before an unverified requirement may be closed, deferred, or formally waived.(High Confidence)[1][3][4][8][9][10][11][12][15][16] The requirements traceability matrix (RTM) is the central artefact in many of these standards, mapping system and safety requirements to software requirements, design elements, code modules, and test cases.(High Confidence)[1][3][4][10][12][16] Parasoft’s descriptions for both DO-178C and ISO 26262 stress that requirements traceability is “the ability to describe and follow the life of a requirement in both forward and backward directions,” and that an RTM illustrates satisfaction of requirements by corresponding work items such as unit tests, source code, and architecture elements.(High Confidence)[1][3]  

FDA guidance makes traceability analyses an explicit validation task, linking software requirements to system requirements and to risk analysis results, and then linking design specifications to software requirements and source code to design specifications, with test cases linked to source code and design.(High Confidence)[10] IEC 62304’s RTM structure, as described in Ketryx’s guide, adds practical fields such as status, owner, change history, comments, verification and validation results, and traceability links across artefacts, making it a comprehensive ledger of requirement lifecycle and verification state.(High Confidence)[4] ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119’s test documentation suite provides artefacts such as test cases, procedures, actual results, test logs, incident reports, and status reports, which together allow for detailed reconstruction of which tests were executed, with what outcomes, and which anomalies remained unresolved.(High Confidence)[11][15]  

From these artefacts, closure criteria for requirements can be inferred.(High Confidence)[1][3][10][12][16] A requirement can be considered “verified present” only when the RTM shows a link to implemented design and code, test cases derived from the requirement, executed test results with pass status (or otherwise acceptable deviations), structural or behavioural coverage analysis demonstrating adequate exercise of the implementation, and risk analysis showing that residual risk is acceptable; for safety-critical requirements, independence of verification and possibly confirmation review must also be satisfied.(High Confidence)[9][10][12][16] A requirement can be considered “verified absent” only when change control records show it has been removed or descope-approved, the RTM has been updated to reflect its deletion or non-applicability, and relevant risk analyses demonstrate that its absence does not create unacceptable hazards.(Medium Confidence)<INFERENCE from="[8][10][12][16]">This condition is often captured through change request and configuration indices rather than a distinct “verified absent” label.</INFERENCE>  

Unverified requirements may be closed or deferred only under tightly controlled conditions.(High Confidence)[8][9][10][12] NASA and ECSS practice require formal waivers or deviations when requirements cannot be met or verified, with packages documenting alternative measures, risk impact, and justification for safety equivalence; closure or deferral without waiver is not permitted for safety-critical items.(High Confidence)[8] FDA guidance expects unresolved software anomalies to be listed and evaluated for impact on safety and effectiveness, with submissions demonstrating that residual risk is acceptable; requirements associated with unresolved anomalies can be considered “verified with known issues,” but only within the context of explicit risk acceptance and monitoring plans.(High Confidence)[10][12] ISO 26262’s confirmation measures imply that work products—including requirements specifications, safety concepts, and safety analyses—must undergo confirmation review, and only when they pass such reviews can they be treated as complete from a functional safety perspective.(High Confidence)[9]

## Research Question 2: Current State and Strongest Supporting Evidence

### Safety-Critical Domains: Mature Practices for Remaining Work

In safety-critical domains such as aviation, automotive functional safety, medical devices, and space missions, the current state of practice for requirements traceability and remaining-work determination is relatively mature and conservative.(High Confidence)[1][3][8][9][10][12][16] DO-178C-driven projects commonly maintain detailed RTMs, structural coverage reports, defect tracking systems, and Software Accomplishment Summaries, and they treat unverified requirements or untested code segments as explicit gaps that must be addressed before certification objectives at a given software level can be considered satisfied.(High Confidence)[1][10][16] Functional safety in automotive, under ISO 26262, uses confirmation measures and independence, particularly for high-ASIL items, to ensure that safety-related work products—including requirements, safety concepts, and safety analyses—are not merely self-certified by development teams but undergo independent review or assessment before being closed.(High Confidence)[9]  

In medical devices, the FDA’s validation and premarket submission guidance institutionalize the need for SRS documents, traceability across requirements, design, code, and tests, risk management files, unresolved anomaly lists, and comprehensive test documentation at unit, integration, and system levels.(High Confidence)[10][12] Manufacturers are expected to demonstrate that software validation has been performed according to established protocols and that residual risk from unresolved anomalies is acceptable; devices are not cleared based solely on developers’ assertion that “testing was done,” but on documented evidence that planned tests were executed, outcomes recorded, and anomalies understood.(High Confidence)[10][12] IEC 62304-compliant RTMs further strengthen this practice by providing a structured map from requirements to tests and by making traceability gaps visible as missing links or empty verification fields.(High Confidence)[4][10][12]

## Research Question 3: Contrasting Viewpoints and Competing Evidence

### Coverage Metrics, Quality, and Goodhart Effects

There is a longstanding tension between the intuitive appeal of coverage metrics and their empirical limitations as predictors of software quality.
