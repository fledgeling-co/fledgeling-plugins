# Each section in depth

Read the part for the section you are writing or reviewing. **R** marks a default from the
research, **C** one from a recorded correction; `evidence.md` has the source for each. The
worked example at the end is an invented person, and none of its figures may be copied.

## The level each section speaks at

A résumé makes claims near the top and proves them further down. Most misplaced sentences are
at the wrong level for the section they sit in.

| Section | It speaks at the level of | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Summary | The person, whatever the job | Could each sentence begin "Usually"? |
| Key skills | An ability that carries to another role | Would it be true in their next job? |
| Scope line | This role | Does it say what is theirs here, and nothing about one occasion? |
| Bullet | One occasion | Does it have a detail that only this piece of work has, and end on what it changed? |

Keep the claim and its proof apart: state a skill generally and let a bullet show it once.
Writing the proof into the skill turns an ability into a description of one job. Naming a
skill so broadly that nothing could prove it is the opposite fault.

The examples below come from one engagement, a product owner in a bank, and from an invented
person. Read each for its shape and write the person's own field.

## 1. Name and contact line

The name is the first line of the body, with no "Résumé" or "Curriculum Vitae" above it. One
line follows, or two tidy lines when one would wrap: city and state, mobile, email, one web address, and the right to work where it is
true and the market expects it.

```
Sydney, NSW | 0400 000 000 | name@example.com | linkedin.com/in/name | Australian citizen
```

- Plain text in the document body. A parser can drop anything placed in a Word header, a text
  box or an icon (R).
- No photo, date of birth, age, marital status, gender or street address (R).
- One web address: a LinkedIn profile or a personal site. Print it as plain text and make it a
  live link in the PDF and the Word file. Check that the address resolves before it goes on the
  page (C).
- Internal applications can use the work email. An external copy needs a personal one. A copy
  that will sit on a public web address carries no phone number and no email (C).

## 2. Professional summary

One paragraph, 60 to 90 words (evals); the research's range is 60 to 100, which is the limit
when the résumé will be judged against it (R). It is the part most likely to be read by a person and
the part an AI summary is most likely to be built from, so it has to make the case alone, and it
has to be short enough that the reader reaches the roles.

Write it in this order:

1. **What they are responsible for now**, in the target role's terms, with one figure that sizes
   that responsibility (R, C). Name it at the level a reader outside their team understands:
   the platform or the field, not the niche product inside it, which belongs to the role below.
   "Product Owner working on the claims platform" carries further than the name of one
   backlog (C). Open with the responsibility and let the title ride along: "As
   Product Owner for claims lodgement, I am responsible for how about 9,000 customers a month
   lodge a claim." A summary that opens "I am the Product Owner for X, which has 9,000 users"
   gives a label and a fact about X; the title is already on the role line, and the figure has
   been spent on the product (C).
2. **What the role requires that they hold.** Name two or three of the ad's required items in
   the ad's own words, as things they do: "I write the user stories and acceptance criteria for
   2-week sprints". Cover the kinds of requirement the ad has: the role itself, the method, and
   the domain (an ad for card disputes wants to see chargeback operations named). This is where
   a reader and a ranking tool look first for the match, and a summary that spends its words
   elsewhere reads well and suits the ad badly (R, evals). Each item named here is also shown
   in the current or previous role, as a scope line or a bullet; a claim that lives only in
   the summary and the skills has no evidence under it (R, evals).
3. **What makes them different** from another person with the same title. For someone who moved
   between functions, this is usually where they came from and what that lets them see (C).
   For someone with a long career, it is usually the span: say the range of work behind them
   in one clause, most recent first, in words and without headcounts, and name the industry and
   the practice it was spent in. "I came to product from branch operations, after a long career
   in banking across lending, collections and frontline service." A list of functions alone
   leaves the reader to guess the industry (C).
   One past job stands for one past job; it does not stand for a career (C).
4. **The judgement the work turns on**, in one sentence that is true every week: the trade-off
   they keep having to make, or the question they ask of each piece of work. "Most of my work
   is weighing simpler access for staff against designing controls that keep our people and
   customers safe." Say what the control protects, not how it works (C). The
   strongest single example of it is then the first bullet of the current role, where it has
   its context and its figures. When the paragraph would pass 90 words, cut this sentence
   before anything in parts 1 to 3.

**Every sentence in the summary is standing, and none reports one occasion** (C). Apply this to
each sentence: could it begin "Usually" or "Each week"? "I put closing the access conflicts
ahead of another division's request for a new role" could not. It is one event, it needs the
role's context to mean anything, it repeats the bullet a few lines below, and out of context
it reads as a boast about turning a colleague down. The person it was written for called it
too specific. An event is evidence, and evidence belongs in a bullet.

Three ways to get this wrong, each of which has happened:

- *The named incident.* An earlier version of this file asked for "one proof, and it names the
  decision", because blind reviewers had marked down proofs that pointed without naming ("one
  such call", "a prioritisation decision of mine"). The pointing was the fault. Naming the
  incident fixed the evasion and put a bullet in the summary.
- *The pointer.* "One such call shows how I work" tells the reader an example exists and keeps
  it from them. Do not gesture at a bullet from the summary.
- *The habit made from one event.* "I put access risks ahead of new features when the evidence
  calls for it" turns one deferred request into a practice. A standing sentence describes the
  recurring work, which the person's sources show is recurring; it does not generalise a
  single decision.

When the summary stands alone, as a profile field or a form answer with no roles under it, it
may end on one result with its figure, because there is no bullet to carry it.

What stays out, and why:

- **A count of years.** "20+ years" is an age signal and says nothing about what was done; the
  dates below already show the tenure (R). This rule removes the number. It does not remove
  the career: breadth and length of experience go in, in words, as the difference in part 3.
  A person who wants the number in can have it; their rule outranks this default (C).
- **A list of the titles they would accept**, or a career objective. The summary states what
  they are (R).
- **Adjectives about themselves.** "Strategic, results-driven leader" gives a reader nothing to
  check (R).
- **A claim the experience does not carry.** Each statement in the summary points at a role
  below that shows it (C).

Two more failures to look for. The first is a summary built from whichever fact had a confirmed
number: one engagement's draft described eighteen years as "coordinated 5 to 10 change
workstreams" while the person's old résumé described running workforce planning for 1,400 seats
(C). Before writing, ask what the strongest true thing about each period is. The second is a
summary cut to a word target, which loses the connecting words and the one sentence with a
person in it; in one summary that sentence said how she judges a change, by what it does to the
people who use the system, and it was the line that made the paragraph hers (C).

Test: could this paragraph sit on someone else's résumé? If yes, it has failed.

## 3. Key skills

Two to four labelled lines of plain text, and one more line for tools (R, C).

```
Simplifying: making a complicated process simpler without weakening its controls; deciding from data what should change
Agreement: securing agreement between teams with competing priorities; preparing frontline staff for a change
People: coaching people into wider responsibilities; leading planning and operations teams
Product craft: prioritising a backlog by customer impact and risk; writing user stories and acceptance criteria
Tools: Jira, Confluence, Power BI, Excel
```

**This section says what the person is unusually good at.** It is read as "why this person",
so it leads with their strengths and is labelled by them. Build it in this order:

1. **Start from the person.** From everything they have said, find what recurs in two or more
   roles, what others rely on them for (a review, an award, "known for…" in their own old
   résumé), and what they did that the title did not require. Those are strengths. A strength
   that appears in one role only is a bullet.
2. **Then read the ad.** Mark which of its requirements those strengths meet. Each remaining
   requirement is either shown in a role already or is a gap to tell the person about.
3. **Entry requirements get one line at most, last.** What every holder of the title does
   (writing user stories, managing a backlog, leading a team) is craft. One "craft" line keeps
   the ad's terms present for a recruiter's search; the roles carry the evidence. A section
   made only of craft reads as the job ad with the person's name on it (C).
4. **Label each line by the strength**, in a word or two, using the plainest true name for it.
   A label that exists only because the ad has that heading tells the reader the list came
   from the ad. A label that is the right word and also appears in the ad is the right word:
   "Influence" names an ability, where "Agreement" names its result, and one draft chose the
   second to avoid a word the ads used (C).

The research's template says the skills lines mirror the ad (R). They do, in step 2 and in the
craft line. One person's list had been built by mirroring first, then given proof, then made
general, and at the end they said it no longer described their strengths and read "like a list
of items to tick off from a job role" (C). Mirroring is a check on the list, and the person is
its source.

- **Each item passes the same-title test.** Would it be on the résumé of most people with this
  title? Then it is craft: move it to the craft line or leave it to the roles. Would it be
  missing from most of them, and true of this person in more than one role? Then it leads (C).
- **Each item is something the person can do**, written as an action: "prioritising a backlog
  by customer impact and risk". A noun they work with ("epics, features and stories",
  "stakeholders", "risk") is a thing, and it sat in one draft's skills for a day only because it
  was true and matched an ad (C).
- **Each item is stated at the level it transfers.** A skill is the part of the work that
  goes with the person to the next job, so name the ability and leave out where it was used:
  no employer, product, system or data set. "Leading discovery from data and from users"
  carries; "leading discovery from access data and from the people who use the platform" is
  one application of it, and reads as someone who has done one thing. The person this skill
  learned it from put it as "I'm not a one-trick pony" (C).
- **Each item is shown below, and the proof stays below.** For every skill, name the bullet or scope paragraph that shows
  it happening. That check is made by reading down the page; it is not met by rewriting the
  skill to describe the bullet (C). A term in a skills list with no example behind it is the weakest form of
  evidence a ranking tool or a person can be given (R).
- **Use the ad's phrase inside the ability** where it is true, so the exact term is present for
  a recruiter's search without becoming a keyword list (R).
- **Tools are a list** and get their own line, each at the level actually used (R, C).
- No rating bars, no table, no columns (R).
- Certifications go under Education and certifications, where the issuer and status can be
  given.

The research reports suggest between 8 and 18 terms in this section. Treat that as a ceiling on
how much it holds, and let the two tests above decide what goes in.

## 4. Employer heading

```
### Example Bank, Sydney | Aug 2005 – Present
```

Several roles at one employer go under one employer heading, with each role dated separately
below it (R). Follow the heading with one or two first-person sentences: where they started, how
many times they were promoted or moved, and a positive reason for the most recent move (R, C).
If the summary already gave the reason for the move, leave it out here: one sentence of
progression is enough, and a second telling of the career is the first thing a reader skips
(evals).
Recognition that belongs to no single role can go here, worded as what was actually awarded and
to whom, and in the person's voice. A team award is a team award, and the person who lists it
was on the team: "I am proud that our work on the claims rebuild received the 2025 team award"
is true to both. "The claims rebuild received the 2025 team award" is true and reads as a
caption (C). Ask what the award was for and what the person did towards it; a line that says
what was recognised is worth more than the award's name, and "received an award" is as far as
the line can go until somebody asks (C).

- Keep the true start date. Application forms and employment checks collect the real dates, and
  a résumé that disagrees with the form costs more than the tenure does (R).
- Name a promotion as a promotion and a move between divisions as a move. This sentence is the
  place for it; "(promoted)" inside a title or date field can break a parser's reading of the
  field and has pushed the employer name onto its own line in a built page (R, C).
- When each role was at a different employer, the heading line is enough. Add a clause saying
  what the organisation is only when a reader would not know it.

## 5. Current role

```
#### Product Owner, Platforms | Example Bank | May 2025 – Present
```

The role line carries the official title, the employer's short name and the dates, in that
order, every time. Repeating the employer costs a human reader nothing and keeps each role
intelligible when a parser detaches it from the heading above (R). Keep the title exactly as
the employer records it; where it is obscure, add a truthful functional gloss after a dash (R).

**Scope line**, first person, present tense, one or two sentences and under about 35 words:
what exactly they own, inside what structure (team size, who they answer to), and how the work
runs. Leave out anything the summary or the employer sentences already said, such as what the
product is or how many people use it; a reader meets those lines seconds apart. Ongoing duties
live here: "I write the user stories and acceptance criteria" (C). State direct reports
separately from the size of a squad or team they work in but do not manage (R). A reader scans
for the bullets, so the scope line is the frame and the bullets carry the evidence (evals).

The research's shape for a role is a scope line and then result bullets: one or two sentences
of what the role is, three to five things that happened for the current role, two to four for
each role in the last 10 to 12 years, one line for anything older (R). That shape is what a
reviewer holding the research will check first.

**Responsibilities, what good looked like, and achievements.** A reader wants all three for a
role, and a résumé gives them without three headings. Responsibilities are the scope line.
Achievements are the bullets. What good looked like goes in a clause, in the scope line or in
the bullet it gives a yardstick to, and only where the person's sources state a measure:
"8 access issues reached production, the measure I watch for this backlog". A result with no
yardstick cannot be read as good or bad. Where the sources state no measure, say nothing; do
not invent a target, and do not print "nothing was tracked". Labelled blocks under each role
("Core responsibilities", "Success criteria", "Key achievements") read as a position
description, add about five lines a role, and map to no field a parser fills (C).

**When the scope line overflows.** It holds, in this order: what they own and the structure
they own it in; then one sentence of standing duties, where a method the ad names can sit
("in 2-week Scrum sprints"). Anything more moves down. A third duty becomes a bullet that
shows it happening once. How they use a tool becomes a bullet or a note on the tools line. A
result never goes in the scope line at all. A scope paragraph of three or four sentences is a
set of bullets that has not been written yet (C).

Work that is still in build goes in its own last bullet, marked as not yet released, with a
forecast worded as a forecast. Leave it out when space is short.

**Then 3 to 5 bullets** (R). Each bullet is one thing that happened:

- the situation in a clause, what they did, and what resulted, told so that the part a reader
  would care about comes first or last and never in the middle (`voice.md`, "Selling what is
  true");
- a detail only they would know, such as a system name, a volume, a trade-off they made (R, C);
- for the current role, one bullet or the scope line for each required item the summary or
  the key skills named, so the ad's terms sit in dated evidence. List those items before you
  finish and find each one under a role; a method or a tool that appears only in the skills
  list has nothing under it (R, evals);
- a figure where one was confirmed and it enlarges the reader's impression: a large scale, a
  result, an exact count of something hard. A range, a small team size, a duration or a tally
  of faults goes (`voice.md`, "Ranking the detail"). Where no result was measured, give the scope instead (how
  many people, sites, releases) and write no result figure (R);
- one headline and at most one supporting fact, ranked as in `voice.md`, "Ranking the detail";
- two sentences are fine when the second carries the result. One idea per bullet, where an
  idea is one story: a release, the fault found after it and the fix belong together, and two
  unrelated decisions do not;
- every bullet ends on what was different afterwards, or on the scale of the work when nothing
  was measured. A bullet that stops at the action ("Brought the two teams under one way of
  working.") is half a bullet: leave it as it is, and ask the person what changed (C).

Order the bullets by how much they matter to the target role, with the strongest first.

Test: is each bullet one thing that happened, with a detail only they would know?

## 6. Roles in the last 10 to 12 years

Each gets a role line, a scope line that gives purpose and scale, and 1 to 4 bullets of what
changed because they were there (R).

The scope line says what the role was for, who it served, and what the person was measured on
("I supported the head of the channel to deliver its service objectives across lending and
transactions, over phone and chat, for service quality within the assigned budget"), then its
scale and what made it complex: several channels or brands, inbound and outbound work, in-house
and outsourced sites. Complexity markers are scale, not decoration; one person asked for
"multi-channel" back because it indicates the complexity of the work (C). Check the noun of
every number: people or seats, staff or users. A duty the person mentioned in passing is not the
scope line; ask what the role was for (C). Two to four for the role before the current one, fewer as the roles get
older.

These roles carry the breadth the summary claims. If the summary says they came from
operations, an operations role here has to show operations at a scale worth claiming (C). Where
an older role supplies something the target role requires and the recent roles do not, give it
more room; where it does not, a scope line and one bullet is enough, and the person may ask for
less (C).

## 7. Earlier roles

Title, employer and dates, with at most one line of description. Keep the dates of any role
listed individually. For roles more than about 12 to 15 years old, one "Earlier roles" line
naming the titles is an accepted alternative on an external copy, as long as the employer's
true start date stays in the heading (R).

## 8. Education and certifications

One line each: the qualification and institution; the certification's full name with its
acronym, the issuer, and the year or status.

- Include the section only when there is something to list. An empty or padded section is worse
  than none (C).
- Leave the graduation year off a degree finished more than about ten years ago. It is the main
  age signal a machine reads (R).
- Date recent learning. A 2025 course shows currency (R).
- List a credential under the name on the certificate. A course attended without passing the
  assessment is professional development (R).
- A certificate is supporting evidence. In two counted samples of bank job ads, 0 of 11 and 2 of
  16 named one, and both of those called it "advantageous" (R).

## 9. Sections that are left out, and when to add one

| Left out by default | Add it when |
| --- | --- |
| Referees | The employer asks. "Referees available on request" is acceptable and spends a line |
| Professional development | There is dated learning from the last three years that the target role cares about |
| Awards, memberships, volunteering | It shows something the target role asks for and no role above shows it |
| A projects or achievements page | Not added. It pulls outcomes away from the role where they happened and pushes recent work off page one |

## 10. Worked example

An invented person. The figures show the shape and are not to be reused.

```markdown
# PRIYA NAIR

Melbourne, VIC | 0400 000 000 | priya.nair@example.com | linkedin.com/in/priya-nair-example | Australian citizen

## Professional summary

As Product Owner for claims lodgement at Harbour Mutual, I am responsible for how about 9,000 customers a month lodge a claim. I write the user stories and acceptance criteria and prioritise the backlog from call and claims data. I came to product from the claims floor, where I led 12 assessors, so I know which steps customers abandon and why. Most of my work is deciding which customer problem gets fixed first, from what the call data shows.

## Key skills

Customer problems: finding where customers get stuck, from data and frontline staff; deciding which fix goes first
People: coaching frontline staff into senior roles; preparing a team for a change before it lands
Product craft: prioritising a backlog by customer impact and cost; writing user stories and acceptance criteria
Tools: Jira, Confluence, Power BI

## Professional experience

### Harbour Mutual, Melbourne | Mar 2012 – Present

I started as a claims assessor and was promoted twice before moving into product in 2024, to fix the lodgement problems I had been hearing about from customers for years.

#### Product Owner, Claims Lodgement | Harbour Mutual | Feb 2024 – Present

I own the lodgement backlog in a product squad and work to the Head of Claims Product.

- Moved a fix for duplicate claims ahead of a planned photo-upload feature after call data showed about 400 duplicate lodgements a month. Duplicates fell to about 60 a month within 2 releases.
- Took the lodgement backlog through each release, coordinating the testing that the assurance team ran before it went live.
- Rewrote the lodgement questions with assessors, so that customers answer them in the order assessors need them.

#### Team Leader, Motor Claims | Harbour Mutual | Jul 2018 – Feb 2024

I led 12 assessors handling motor claims.

- Coached 3 assessors into senior roles.
- Prepared the team for a new claims system, writing the guidance and training that took it live without a backlog of claims.

#### Claims Assessor | Harbour Mutual | Mar 2012 – Jul 2018
```

What the example shows: the summary opens on a responsibility, names two things the role
requires, gives the difference (the claims floor) and ends on the judgement her work turns on,
which the first bullet shows happening once. The skills lead with what she is good at and end
on one craft line, and every skill has a line below that shows it. "Coordinating the testing
that the assurance team ran" keeps the scope exact. The figures that remain are a scale, two
results and a team she led; the squad size and the count of releases are left out because
they would make the work sound smaller. One bullet ends on the admin's opposite: what the
training achieved. There is no education section because the invented person has nothing to
list.
