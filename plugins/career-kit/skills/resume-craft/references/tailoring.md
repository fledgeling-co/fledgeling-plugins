# Suiting a résumé to a role, a reader and a market

Read this when tailoring to an ad, preparing an internal, external or public copy, or working
outside the one market this skill has research for. **R** marks a default from the research,
**C** one from a recorded correction; `evidence.md` has the source for each.

## Reading the ad

1. List what the ad says is required, separately from what it says is preferred. One documented
   ranking tool grades a résumé on whether it meets every required qualification, and the step
   from "most" to "all" is a whole grade (R).
2. For each required item, find the fact that shows it. Mark the item **shown**, **partly
   shown** or **not shown**.
3. For a shown item, use the ad's own phrase once in the summary or key skills and once in a
   sentence in the current or previous role where it happens. More repetition reads as stuffing
   to a person and adds nothing for a matcher that reads meaning (R).
4. For an item not shown, leave it out and tell the person. A panel reviewing one résumé against
   five roles concluded that two could not be served "by any wording, because the gap is in her
   experience" (C).
5. Use the target organisation's vocabulary for its operating model (squad, crew, tribe, release
   train) only where it names work the person did. Organisations differ, and the wrong one's
   words are noise (R).

Exact terms still matter in some systems: a recruiter searching an older applicant-tracking
system can search exact strings, so write a required phrase the way the ad writes it, including
its hyphenation, and write a title in full before abbreviating it (R, C).

Do not paste the ad's wording, hide keywords in white text, or address instructions to an AI
reviewer (R).

## One résumé or several

Ask the person. Both positions are in the evidence:

- **Several**: the research recommends one version per role family (product, delivery,
  people-leadership), because each family reads for different proof (R).
- **One**: an engagement that built five tailored versions from fragments found that nobody was
  reading any of them as a document, and replaced them with one written by hand. A panel then
  said which roles the one résumé could not serve (C).

When there are several, each differs in three places only: the summary, which skills appear,
and which bullets appear and in what order. Titles, dates and figures are identical in every
version (R, C).

What each role family reads for (R):

| Role family | The proof it wants |
| --- | --- |
| Product owner or manager | What they chose to build, defer or stop, how they prioritised, and what happened after release |
| Delivery or project lead | How many teams, which dependencies and risks, what planning and governance, what improved in flow or readiness |
| People leader | Direct reports as a number, coaching, performance, capability built, service quality |
| Change manager | Scope, readiness, communications, training, adoption and benefits |

## Internal, external and public copies

| | Internal application | External application | Public copy (a website download) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reader | Knows the organisation; may know the real scope | Knows neither | Anyone |
| Internal names | Kept, and explained once for another department | Explained in market terms, or replaced | Explained or removed |
| Figures | Whatever the person confirmed | Whatever is not confidential | Soften or drop each figure the person restricts |
| Contact line | Work email is usual | Personal email | No phone, no email |
| Scope wording | Exact; internal readers test it | Exact | Exact |
| Proof available only here | Internal awards and program names the panel will recognise | None | None |
| Reason for the move | One positive clause | Not needed | Not needed |

- Ask which figures may leave the organisation and which may be public, before writing either
  copy. A count that is fine on an internal application can breach a confidentiality rule on a
  public page (C).
- The public copy is the same document with those changes, kept in step with the source. After
  a fact changes in one copy, check the others and say which you changed (C).
- An internal reader's familiarity with the person's name is not familiarity with their work.
  Hold the internal copy to the same standard of evidence (R).
- Titles, employers and dates match exactly across the résumé, any online profile and the
  application form. Employers verify employment, and a mismatch is the thing a check finds (R).

## Use of AI in writing it

No report found an employer that detects AI authorship or rejects for it, and the surveys agree
that generic, interchangeable copy is what recruiters mark down (R). Some employers publish
rules about AI in applications; follow the one that applies. Keep the person's confidential
details out of any outside model: strip contact details before sending text for review (C).

## A market this skill has no research for

The section jobs, the voice and the tests travel. These defaults do not, and each needs checking
for the person's country and industry before it is applied:

1. Length (one page is the norm in some markets; an academic CV follows different rules).
2. Photo and personal details (expected in some countries, unlawful to ask for in others).
3. Whether a right-to-work line is expected.
4. Whether referees are listed.
5. Paper size and spelling.
6. First person in the summary (conventions differ; ask the person).
7. Which applicant systems the target employers run and what the ads there ask for.

Say in your reply that the defaults came from Australian banking research and name which of the
seven you checked. `career-kit:career-kit` stage 2 is the research procedure when the answer
has to be sourced.
