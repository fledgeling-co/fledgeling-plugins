# The interview guide

## Research the employer's own process first

How they interview is published more often than people expect: a careers site describes the
stages, a values page names what the behavioural questions test, and the ads themselves say
what the panel will ask about. Fetch those rather than writing generic advice, and cite them.

Record: the stages and who sits on each, the question format, the values by name, and anything
the employer says about how to prepare.

## Structure

1. **How this employer interviews** — the stages, the format, the values, and the shape an
   answer should take for that format.
2. **The person's stories** — eight to twelve, each with the facts attached. A story is the
   unit that gets reused across a dozen questions, so writing them once and pointing at them is
   what keeps the guide usable.
3. **Questions grouped by theme**, each with an example answer built only from confirmed facts.
   Thirty or so, drawn from the ads' requirements rather than a generic list.
4. **Per role: what to lead with.** Different ads reward different stories.
5. **The hard questions.** The gap in their experience, the thing that went wrong, why they are
   leaving, the figure they cannot evidence. These are the ones the guide exists for, and an
   honest answer prepared in advance is the whole deliverable.
6. **A practice plan.** What to say aloud, in what order, how many times.

## Example answers are made of confirmed facts only

Where an answer needs a detail nobody has supplied, mark it in place with a placeholder in
angle brackets, such as `<one sentence on what draws you to this team's work>`, and collect
those into the next round of questions. An example answer containing an invented specific is worse than a gap, because the person
will say it in the room.

## Keep it in step with the résumé

The guide states the same facts as the résumé, in the person's spoken voice. When a fact, a
verb or a title changes in the résumé, find it in the guide in the same sitting; an example
answer the person rehearses is the worst place for a claim they have since withdrawn. The
guide is also where an example cut from the résumé for space belongs, and where a question
raised by the résumé itself gets an answer: a web address printed on it, a gap it does not
mention, one document sent for different kinds of role.

## A practice page

Where the person will use it more than once, build the questions as a private page rather than
a document: search, a filter by role, a mode that blurs the example answers so they can try
first, and a per-question "practised aloud" checkbox saved in the browser.

That page hands back what they already told you and asks nothing, which makes it a different
artefact from the forms in stage 4 rather than a replacement for them.

If it is deployed anywhere, it carries every figure in it to anyone with the URL. A private
practice page holding restricted figures needs real protection, not an unlisted address.
Where the person chooses to leave it at an open address, soften the restricted figures in the
build, with a check that fails if one remains, and confirm what the live page serves.
