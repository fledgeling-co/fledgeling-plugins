# Typographic and Layout Standards for Professional Résumés in Corporate Australia (2026)

Across government guidance, major recruiters, large employers and applicant‑tracking‑system (ATS) documentation, there is a broad convergence on a **plain, single‑column, reverse‑chronological résumé layout**, using common sans‑serif fonts around 11‑point, clear section headings, succinct bullet points for achievements, and an overall length of two to three pages for experienced corporate applicants.[1][2][4][6][9][10][20] At the same time, there is no single codified “standard” layout: different authorities emphasise slightly different page counts, section orders or degrees of visual styling, and typography research informs but does not fully determine résumé specification.[7][16][18][19] For an experienced professional applying to corporate roles in Australian banking in 2026, the safest and most broadly accepted option is a **single‑column A4 résumé**, in Word and PDF formats, with contact details at the top of the body (not in headers or footers), Calibri or Arial at 11‑point for body text, line spacing around a Word multiple of \(1.2\)–\(1.5\), generous white space, and strictly ATS‑compatible layout without tables, columns or graphics.[4][7][11][14][15][20] Where more narrative or senior‑level detail is required, a slightly longer CV (up to three or four pages) is accepted by many Australian corporate and public‑sector employers, provided that it remains tightly written, factual and easy to scan.[1][2][8][10][20] Typography and legibility research support these practical conventions by showing that adult readers prefer 11‑point type, moderate line spacing of about two points above solid text, and line lengths of roughly 50–75 characters, which implies relatively wide margins and restrained use of dense paragraphs for optimal readability.[16][17][18][19] Overall, the evidence supports specifying one primary ATS‑safe layout and one slightly more expansive corporate CV layout for banking professionals, while recognising that details such as exact margins, heading sizes and paragraph spacing remain matters of informed judgement rather than regulated standards.[7][16][18][20]

## Structural Conventions in Australian Résumés

### Section Order and Headings

The core question in structural design is how to organise sections and headings so that an experienced banking professional’s résumé is immediately intelligible to both corporate hiring managers and ATS systems. Government guidance, recruiter advice and employer documents converge on a simple section structure: contact details, a brief summary or profile, skills and attributes, work history in reverse chronological order, education and qualifications, and optional additional sections such as licences, memberships or referees.[1][2][4][5][6][8][9][10][20] The Job Jumpstart article “Building your resume” recommends starting with a “brain dump” of key information, then organising into contact details, skills and achievements, work experience, education and courses, qualifications and licences, and referees, with each of these categories forming a section in the actual résumé.[5] DEWR’s example résumé further illustrates sections labeled “About Me,” “Skills and Attributes,” “Work History,” “Education History,” “Licences and Accreditations,” and referees, signposting a structure that is particularly common in Australian government and care‑sector applications.[4] This supports the inference that a standard résumé used in Australian corporate contexts should adopt comparable headings, perhaps with minor renaming such as “Professional Summary” instead of “About Me,” but retaining the overall order.[4][20]

The APS “Cracking the Code” materials also implicitly endorse this structure by listing the information expected in a CV: personal details, education, work experience, other experience and referees.[1][2] The APS guidance states that “Most CVs are between two and six pages long” and that they “should usually include” personal details, education, work experience, other experience and referees.[1][2] Each of these is treated as a section, and the description suggests an order starting with personal details at the top, followed by education, then work experience, then other relevant activities, and finally referees.[1][2] I Work For NSW’s “Writing your application” page reinforces a similar structure for NSW government roles, saying that a résumé is “a summary of your qualifications, experience, skills, qualities” and should include personal details, a profile or summary, education, work experience, relevant information such as specific skills or awards, and referees.[6] Putting these public‑sector templates together yields a structural pattern that strongly resembles corporate expectations, with the principal difference being that corporate roles may emphasise the professional summary more heavily and sometimes place education after work experience for mid‑career applicants.

Recruiter advice reinforces the primacy of work experience, particularly arranged in reverse chronological order. Randstad writes that content “needs to be clear, concise, and easy to read, with information arranged in reverse-chronological order, starting with your most recent job history and education.”[9] Robert Half’s FAQ similarly suggests beginning with contact details and a brief professional summary, followed by a list of key skills, then work history in reverse chronological order, and finally education and qualifications.[10] Westpac’s CV guide emphasises employment history as the central narrative, advising that “Your employment history should be outlined in chronological order, starting with your most recent role, working back in time,” and that company name, role titles and time period should be clearly included, with a brief role summary and achievement‑focused bullet points.[20] These consistent statements support a high‑confidence conclusion that, for corporate banking roles, the résumé should place the work‑experience section relatively early—after contact details and professional summary and either before or after a skills summary—using headings such as “Professional Experience,” “Employment History” or similar.

The question of precise section order is not absolutely settled. APS and DEWR guidance often list education ahead of work experience, reflecting that many applicants may be early‑career or applying to government roles where formal qualifications are tightly specified.[1][2][4] Corporate recruiter advice, by contrast, often places work experience before education, especially for experienced professionals.[9][10][20] No source explicitly prescribes a different structure for banking versus other corporate roles, but the Westpac CV guide’s emphasis on career narrative and achievements supports the view that work experience should be central and prominent for banking professionals.[20] Consequently, a reasonable, evidence‑aligned section order for an experienced corporate banking résumé would be: contact details; professional summary; key skills; employment history in reverse chronological order; education and qualifications; additional credentials or licences; professional memberships; and referees.[4][6][9][10][20] This ordering respects both government and corporate guidance and the practical importance of recent roles.

### One Column or Two: Layout Simplicity and ATS Constraints

A critical structural decision is whether to use a single column layout or a two‑column design, particularly as contemporary résumé templates often offer sidebars, split columns and graphical features. The strongest evidence on this point comes from ATS documentation and interpretive guides describing parse failures. Greenhouse support explicitly notes that it cannot parse resumes larger than 2.5 MB and that complex structures can cause unsuccessful parsing.[14] ATSPrep’s guide on Greenhouse states that “A columned layout and complex layouts using tables both appear on the official list of parse failure causes,” and recommends that candidates “Let the document read top to bottom in one pass” and avoid layouts for which the reading order would have to be explained.[11] ResumeAdapter’s analysis of Greenhouse similarly lists “columns, tables, headers and footers, contact details in a header or text box, graphics and photos and word art, an image instead of a document, spaced-out letters, bare company names, and abbreviated job titles” as published parse‑failure causes.[15] These consistent statements from ATS‑oriented sources make it clear that, for any employer using Greenhouse or similar parsing systems, a multi‑column résumé introduces a real risk that the content will not be correctly read or indexed.

Although SAP SuccessFactors documentation does not explicitly discuss layout, its emphasis on parsing textual resumes in DOCX and TXT formats, and its separate handling of image‑based resumes uploaded as TIFF, JPG, BMP or GIF, implicitly favours simple text‑based layouts without complex graphics.[12][13] The recommended file types, and the fact that résumé parsing is triggered by uploading a document for text extraction, suggest that a standard single‑column format will be most robust for ATS parsing.[12][13] No government or recruiter source directly endorses two‑column layouts; instead, they emphasise clarity, ease of reading and simple headings with bullet points.[4][6][9][10][20] Westpac’s guide goes further by advising candidates to “Keep your CV limited to words” and to “Avoid using any photos, pictures or gimmicks, use a plain, clean layout with minimal styling.”[20] This corporate employer advice aligns strongly with ATS guidance and points away from graphic or two‑column templates.

Given this convergence, the safest and best‑supported conclusion is that a **single‑column layout** is the standard and preferred structure for a résumé or CV in the corporate Australian job market, including banking. This layout should allow the document to be read from top to bottom in a single logical flow, with clear section headings and no sidebars or second columns.[4][6][9][10][11][14][15][20] Two‑column layouts might be acceptable in some creative industries or where applications are emailed directly to hiring managers rather than submitted through ATS portals, but they are empirically discouraged by ATS vendors and by large employers in the financial sector. For an experienced banking professional whose résumé must travel through both human readers and ATS, a single column is effectively definitive best practice.

The question of whether any of the cited sources themselves present two‑column examples, in contradiction to their text, is constrained by the limited visibility of embedded templates in the snippets. DEWR’s example résumé and Job Jumpstart’s templates are described textually with headings and bullet points, and there is no indication that they use sidebars or two columns; on the contrary, their instructional tone suggests conventional single‑column layouts.[4][5] Westpac’s CV guide emphasises plain layout and white space, and again, nothing in the text suggests a two‑column design.[20] Randstad and Robert Half, in their online advice, describe resumes using sequential sections and bullet points, without referencing sidebars.[9][10] While it is possible that some recruiter websites offer visually stylised templates elsewhere, within the scope of the provided materials there is no example whose visible description contradicts the written advice regarding simplicity and clarity.

### Employer, Title and Date Layout, and Multiple Roles at One Employer

How employer names, job titles and dates are laid out is another core structural concern, especially for ensuring ATS parsing and human readability. Government and employer examples provide explicit textual patterns. DEWR’s example résumé shows work history entries in the form “2017 – Company name – Administrative Assistant” and “2015 – 2016 Company name – Sales Assistant,” followed by bullet points for duties.[4] These lines place the date range, employer and job title inline, separated by en‑dash characters, with the date at the left margin rather than right‑aligned, and job title appearing after employer.[4] The inclusion of a caring role as “2008 – 2015 Caring for children” further shows that non‑traditional roles can be listed in the same inline format.[4] Westpac’s CV guide, in describing employment history, advises that candidates include “Company name, role title(s), time period,” again implying a single line that includes all three elements, though without prescribing exact punctuation or alignment.[20] Randstad suggests listing “Job Title,” “Name of Employer,” and “Dates of employment,” followed by a list of key responsibilities and achievements.[9] Robert Half similarly recommends listing job title, employer, dates and major achievements in reverse chronological order.[10] None of these sources call for dates to be right‑aligned; rather, they assume that each job entry begins with or prominently includes dates, employer and title inline.

From ATS documentation, another important detail emerges: Greenhouse explicitly warns against abbreviated job titles and bare company names, advising candidates to “Spell job titles out in full” and to “Include the Inc., Ltd, LLC or Co. that belongs to the legal name” of employers.[11][15] This means that, in the inline layout, job titles should be written in full words (e.g., “Senior Relationship Manager” rather than “Sr. RM”), and employer names should be the legal or commonly searched form (e.g., “Westpac Banking Corporation” rather than an internal abbreviation).[11][15] For Australian banking roles, this implies that each employment entry should have a line of text including the full employer name, full job title, and the range of months and years worked, arranged in a manner that is both human‑readable and ATS‑parsable.

The question of whether dates should include months as well as years is not directly answered in the provided guidance. DEWR’s example uses years only (“2015 – 2016”), presumably for brevity.[4] Westpac’s guide refers to “time period” without specifying the granularity.[20] Recruiter advice tends to assume month‑and‑year ranges in practice, but no explicit sentences in the sources mandate this level of detail.[9][10] In the absence of a strict rule, the safest approach for an experienced professional in banking would be to list months and years (e.g., “Mar 2018 – Jul 2022”) for clarity and to align with typical ATS and recruiter expectations, while recognising that government examples sometimes use year‑only ranges.[4][20] This is a design decision made in light of common practice rather than a codified standard.

Handling multiple roles at one employer is also structurally important. Westpac’s CV guide mentions “role title(s)” in plural and suggests that the employment history section can include more than one title for the same company, but it does not explicitly demonstrate the visual pattern.[20] Randstad and Robert Half describe work experience entries individually, but do not directly address promotions within the same organisation.[9][10] APS and DEWR guidance focus on listing roles and achievements, rather than structural nesting.[1][2][4] In the absence of explicit instructions, the most widely accepted practice (and one that aligns with ATS parsing) is to list the employer once, followed by separate job‑title entries indented beneath, each with its own date range and bullet list of responsibilities and achievements. This creates a hierarchical structure that is logically clear to human readers and still reads top‑to‑bottom in a single column for ATS extraction. While this practice is strongly supported by recruiter experience and general résumé convention, the specific nested format is more a matter of professional consensus than directly mandated in any single cited sentence.

### Header and Footer Use, and Placement of Contact Details

Header and footer use is a structural choice that directly affects ATS parsing. Greenhouse’s support content and ATSPrep’s guide both warn explicitly that placing contact details in headers, footers or text boxes can cause parsing failures. The ATSPrep checklist emphasises, “Name, email and phone belong in the first lines of the page itself, not in a header, footer or text box. This is the correction Greenhouse asks for most explicitly.”[11] The Greenhouse support article on unsuccessful resume parses lists “Resumes with the name and contact information in the header, footer, or text box” among the reasons why a resume may fail to parse.[14] ResumeAdapter reiterates that “contact details in a header or text box” are one of the nine published failure causes.[15] Together, these statements create high‑confidence evidence that, for ATS‑driven recruiting systems, contact details must appear in the body of the document, directly at the top of the first page, rather than in Word’s header or footer structures.

Government and employer guidance, while not framed in ATS terms, align with this practice by emphasising the visibility of contact information at the top of the résumé. DEWR’s résumé tips urge candidates to “Include your name, phone number and email address on every page,” which implies placing contact information prominently in the body of the document, perhaps in a repeating header‑like section that is still part of the main text area.[4] Job Jumpstart similarly recommends including contact details (name, email and mobile number) early in the resume.[5] Westpac’s CV guide instructs candidates to make sure “your name and contact details are clearly visible at the top of the 1st page,” and suggests listing name, phone number, email address, LinkedIn profile and location.[20] None of these sources advocate using Word headers or footers for this information; they all focus on the first page’s main content. This convergence means that, in structural specification, the résumé should begin with a text block at the very top containing full name and key contact details, formatted as part of the body and replicated on subsequent pages if desired, but not placed in the header or footer fields.

Beyond contact details, headers and footers can in principle be used for page numbers or confidential markers without compromising ATS parsing, provided they do not contain essential candidate data. However, Greenhouse’s explicit caution about headers and footers suggests that even non‑critical content in these areas should be used sparingly, as complex header/footer structures can contribute to parse failures.[11][14][15] For an ATS‑optimised résumé, the simplest and safest approach is to avoid headers and footers altogether, or to keep them empty when uploading to ATS portals, and to rely instead on the main text area for all content. This eliminates potential sources of parsing error and adheres to both ATS and employer preferences for clarity.

### Page Count by Seniority and Role Type

The number of pages appropriate for a résumé or CV varies by seniority and sector. DEWR’s tips clearly recommend concision, stating, “Try to keep it to no more than 2 pages,” and linking résumé length to the level of work experience and the job applied for.[4] This guidance is directed at general job seekers, including those with limited experience, and is framed as a general best practice rather than a strict rule. Robert Half’s advice in the Australian market likewise says, “In most cases, your resume should be no longer than two to three pages,” acknowledging that experienced professionals may need more space but still constraining the document to a compact form.[10] The APS “Cracking the Code” guidance, however, notes that “Most CVs are between two and six pages long,” reflecting the more expansive nature of public‑sector applications, particularly for roles requiring detailed evidence against selection criteria and extensive employment histories.[1][2] The AFP SES Band 1 CV guide further specifies that for senior executive service roles, “Your CV should be a maximum of two (2) pages detailing your experience and education.”[8] This shows that very senior roles can still involve extremely concise CVs, especially where a separate pitch or selection‑criteria statement carries the narrative.

Westpac’s CV guide provides the most specific corporate nuance, distinguishing between résumé and CV length. It explains that “A: The major difference is length - a Résumé is a shortened, high level summary of a Curriculum Vitae (CV), it’s typically no longer than one page, a CV can comfortably stretch over 3-4 pages. The two terms have become interchangeable in Australia – but it is nonetheless a CV that is the commonly used format here.”[20] For corporate banking roles at Westpac, this suggests that a full CV of three to four pages is acceptable, perhaps even expected, for experienced professionals, while a one‑page résumé would be too brief for many mid‑career or senior candidates.[20] This employer perspective partially conflicts with DEWR’s more generic advice to stay under two pages, but the conflict is explicable: DEWR is addressing a broad job‑seeking audience, including early‑career candidates, whereas Westpac is targeting experienced corporate hires and emphasising the difference between résumé and CV formats.

Taken together, these statements indicate that there is no single definitive page‑count standard across all Australian employers. For an experienced professional in corporate banking, the evidence supports a high‑confidence conclusion that **two to three pages** is a safe and widely accepted range for a résumé or CV submitted to ATS‑driven portals and recruiters.[4][10][20] Westpac’s suggestion that CVs can comfortably stretch to three or four pages provides an upper bound for particularly complex careers or roles requiring extensive detail, but even in those cases the CV must remain concise, achievement‑focused and easy to scan, as Westpac warns against excessive detail and encourages brevity.[20] For senior public‑sector roles with separate pitch documents, a tight two‑page CV is often mandated, as in the AFP SES Band 1 guidance.[8] For junior or graduate roles, a one‑page résumé may be appropriate, but this is outside the scope of experienced banking professionals. Overall, the practical recommendation is a two‑page core résumé for ATS submission, with the option to extend to a third page where necessary, and possibly a four‑page CV for direct employer submissions that explicitly permit longer formats.[4][8][10][20]

## Typographic Norms: Typefaces, Sizes, Emphasis and Bullets

### Typeface Selection and Sans‑Serif Preference

Typeface choice influences both legibility and perceived professionalism. DEWR’s résumé tips provide explicit guidance, advising candidates to “Make sure your résumé is in an easy to read font – Calibri or Arial in size 11 are good choices.”[4] Calibri and Arial are both widely available sans‑serif fonts, designed for screen readability and common in Microsoft Office; DEWR’s recommendation positions them as default choices for Australian job seekers. Robert Half similarly advises choosing “a font that is professional, easy to read, and suitable for both digital and printed formats. Simple fonts such as Arial, Calibri, Aptos, or Times New Roman are commonly used.”[10] This recruiter guidance expands the set to include Times New Roman—a serif font—and Aptos, a newer sans‑serif typeface introduced in recent Microsoft Office versions.[10] However, the emphasis on simplicity and readability remains.

Typography research adds empirical support for sans‑serif choices, especially for younger readers and on screens. The Springer study on typographical elements reports that participants “performed better with sans-serif fonts combined with 1.5 line spacing, showing increased saccadic eye movements, decreased fixation durations and lower blink rates,” leading to improved reading speed and accuracy.[19] While this study focused on secondary school students and reading tasks, its findings suggest that sans‑serif fonts like Verdana and Helvetica, which were tested, can enhance readability at typical body sizes.[19] Baymard’s UX research, though primarily concerned with web interfaces, likewise assumes sans‑serif defaults in its line‑length guidance and emphasises accessibility.[18] The ERIC “Legibility of Print” document discusses typeface in general, noting that typographical factors such as style of typeface can influence legibility, but its specific favourite size finding refers to 11‑point type rather than font families.[16]

The Australian Government Style Manual, while not prescribing résumé typography, discusses readability broadly and recommends writing at a reading level equivalent to Year 7 (age 12–14) to maximise accessibility.[7] It notes that factors affecting readability include “letter, word and line spacing, text size, font choice and contrast, the logical flow of your ideas, the content’s tone, how you use visual aids, images or headings.”[7] This emphasis on font choice and contrast supports the use of clear, high‑contrast sans‑serif fonts for résumé body text, particularly given that résumés may be read on screens with varying resolutions and printed in grayscale.

Given these converging recommendations, the safest and most widely accepted typeface choices for an experienced professional’s résumé in Australian banking in 2026 are **Calibri, Arial or Aptos** for body text, with Times New Roman as an acceptable serif alternative if a more traditional look is desired.[4][10][19] The choice among these should be driven by consistency and readability: Calibri and Aptos are excellent for Word documents and on‑screen reading; Arial is highly legible in both digital and print; Times New Roman can be used for more conservative environments but may be marginally less legible at very small sizes compared to modern sans‑serifs. For ATS parsing, all of these fonts are rendered as plain text and thus equally acceptable; the main requirement is that the document contain actual text rather than embedded images. No source in the set explicitly endorses decorative or script fonts; on the contrary, Westpac warns against “gimmicks” and advocates a plain, clean layout.[20] Therefore, decorative fonts should be avoided in corporate résumés.

### Body Text Size, Heading Sizes and Name Emphasis

Body text size and heading hierarchy are central to typographic specification. DEWR explicitly recommends size 11 for résumé text, saying that Calibri or Arial in “size 11 are good choices,” which provides a concrete baseline for body text.[4] The ERIC “Legibility of Print” document reports that Paterson and Tinker “have established 11-point type as the favorite of adult readers,” indicating that readers prefer 11‑point body text for general prose.[16] Microsoft’s “More on Linespacing” article summarises Tinker’s research using 10‑point text but confirms that adding two points of linespacing yields optimal reading speed, implicitly supporting moderate sans‑serif sizes such as 10–12 points for body text.[17]

Recruiter advice also leans toward moderate body sizes. No source explicitly prohibits colour, but the combination of ATS guidance and employer preference for plain layouts suggests that a largely monochrome résumé is safest for banking roles.

## Density and Readability: Spacing, Margins and Line Length

### Line Spacing and Word Settings

Line spacing (leading) determines how dense text appears and strongly influences readability. DEWR’s résumé tips tell candidates to use easy‑to‑read fonts and to get someone to proof‑read for design, spelling and grammar, but they do not specify line spacing numerically.[4] Westpac’s CV guide instructs candidates to make the CV “easy to navigate and read – make sure it’s well structured with plenty of white space,” implying that line spacing should not be set to single‑line tight text but should allow comfortable breathing room.[20] The Style Manual emphasises that readability depends on letter, word and line spacing, and that these factors are critical in meeting accessibility standards.[7] However, it does not provide explicit numeric line‑spacing values.

Typography research fills this gap. Tinker’s studies, summarised in the ERIC document, indicate that for 10‑point type in a 19‑pica line width, “2-point leading seems to be the optimal,” and that adding more than two points yields diminishing legibility.[16] Microsoft’s “More on Linespacing” article interprets this for modern word processing, recommending setting line spacing in Word to a multiple of 1.2. It explains that this makes each line of 10‑point text 12 points tall and reports that “A statistically reliable speed advantage was found by adding 2 points of linespacing (multiple of 1.2). And adding more linespacing past 2 points did not further improve reading speed.”[17] The article presents a table showing that 2‑point additional linespacing produced a reading speed difference of +5.2% compared to control, while 4‑point leading only gave +2.8%.[17] This suggests that moderate extra spacing is optimal.

The Springer study found that sans‑serif fonts combined with 1.5 line spacing improved reading speed and accuracy among secondary school students.[19] Baymard’s accessibility guidance for web content notes that line height (spacing between lines of text) should be set at 1.5 times the font size for compliance with standards, with paragraph spacing at 2em, word spacing at 0.16em, and letter spacing at 0.12em.[18] These values are targeted at web CSS, not Word documents, but they demonstrate that relatively generous line spacing and paragraph spacing are beneficial for readability and accessibility.

Translating these findings to Word settings for a résumé suggests that line spacing should be set between **1.2 and 1.5** as a multiple of the font size, with **1.2** being the minimal increase recommended by Tinker/Microsoft and **1.5** offering additional comfort and accessibility in digital reading.[16][17][18][19] For 11‑point body text, a Word line spacing of 1.15–1.2 would be consistent with Tinker’s empirically optimal two points of leading, while 1.5 would be more generous, aligning with web accessibility guidelines.[16][17][18] Choosing between these is a design decision: a two‑page résumé with dense content might use 1.15–1.2 to maintain length, while a three‑page CV could afford 1.3–1.5 to increase white space.

Government and employer guidance implicitly favour moderate spacing. Westpac’s emphasis on “plenty of white space” suggests that very tight single spacing (Word’s “Single” at 1.0) is too dense for a CV.[20] The Style Manual’s mention of line spacing as a critical readability factor supports avoiding cramped text.[7] No source recommends double spacing for body text in résumés; such spacing would spread content excessively and likely exceed page limits. Therefore, a high‑confidence specification is that résumé body text should use a line‑spacing multiple in Word between **1.15 and 1.5**, with 1.2 as a widely supported optimum for reading speed and 1.5 as an upper bound for comfort and accessibility, balanced against page count.[16][17][18][19]

### Paragraph and Bullet Spacing

Spacing between paragraphs and between bullets also affects readability and document length. Government and employer guidance discuss white space qualitatively but not in numeric terms. Westpac’s CV guide encourages candidates to ensure “plenty of white space” and warns that relevance and brevity trump too much detail.[20] It advises candidates not to “get stuck on technicalities, wording, details and exact dates,” but instead to focus on crafting a clear, achievement‑focused narrative, which implies that paragraphs should not be crammed together but should be separated enough to let each contribution stand out.[20] DEWR’s example résumé and Job Jumpstart’s templates are described with section headings and bullet lists but do not specify exact spacing.[4][5] Recruiters such as Robert Half suggest using bullet points and clean formatting, which usually implies some spacing between bullets to avoid visual clutter.[10]

Baymard’s web accessibility guidance, while not résumé‑specific, proposes that paragraph spacing be set to 2em (twice the line height), which in Word terms would correspond to a “space after” setting of roughly twice the font size, and that line height be 1.5em.[18] This ensures that paragraphs are visually separated. In printed prose, Tinker’s findings suggest that adding more than two points of leading can reduce legibility in long lines, but paragraph spacing is a separate dimension.[16][17] Combining these insights, a reasonable recommendation for Word paragraph spacing would be to set “space after” to **3–6 points** for standard paragraphs, which equates approximately to 0.3–0.6 lines for 11‑point text, providing visible separation without excessive gaps.

For bullet lists, spacing between bullets can be slightly tighter than between paragraphs, as each bullet is part of a single logical list. A common practice is to use no additional spacing between individual bullets but to include a small space before and after the entire bullet list to separate it from surrounding paragraphs or headings. In Word, this can be achieved by setting the bullet list’s paragraph spacing to 0 points before and 3 points after, while the body paragraphs use 3 points before and after. Although no source provides these exact numbers, they align with Westpac’s call for white space and with web accessibility guidelines that encourage clear separation of textual units.[18][20] This is thus a medium‑confidence recommendation derived from general typographic principles.

### Space Before and After Section Headings, and Visual Hierarchy

Section headings, such as “Professional Summary,” “Skills and Attributes,” “Employment History” and “Education History,” require spacing before and after to create a clear hierarchy. Government and employer guidance emphasise headings as structural markers but do not provide numeric spacing values. APS “Cracking the Code” and DEWR’s résumé tips describe headings and several sections but focus on content.[1][2][4] Westpac’s CV guide notes that an effective CV should be “easy to navigate and read – make sure it’s well structured with plenty of white space,” implying that headings should not be crammed against the preceding text.[20] Recruiter advice from Randstad and Robert Half stresses using headings and spacing to ease scanning, but again without numeric parameters.[9][10]

From a typographic standpoint, a reasonable specification in Word would be to set **space before section headings** at **6–12 points**, and **space after** at **3–6 points**, depending on body font size and page length. For 11‑point body text and 13–14‑point headings, 12 points before and 6 points after provide clear separation while preserving vertical compactness. These values align qualitatively with Westpac’s emphasis on white space and the Style Manual’s focus on readability through spacing.[7][20] They also mimic common layout defaults in professional templates, where headings have roughly half a line of spacing above and below. Although no source offers explicit numeric spacing for headings, the inferred values are consistent with the need for visually distinct sections and efficient scanning.

### Page Margins and Line Length

Margins determine the width of the text column and thus the line length, which affects readability. None of the government, recruiter or employer sources provide explicit margin measurements for résumés. Westpac speaks of “plenty of white space” and a CV that is “easy to navigate and read,” but does not quantify margins in millimetres or centimetres.[20] DEWR’s tips focus on font and length, not margins.[4] APS and I Work For NSW similarly emphasise content and clarity.[1][2][6] This absence of explicit margin values is a genuine gap in résumé‑specific guidance.

Typography research, however, offers line‑length recommendations that can be translated into margin decisions. The ERIC “Legibility of Print” document, summarising Tinker and Paterson’s work, states that “The ideal line length would be seven or eight words, averaging about six character per word. This would be approximately 45 to 55 characters per line and would be ideal for the average reader.”[16] It also notes that “the upper limit for a line if it is to be truly effective is 27 picas,” and that longer lines become difficult to read.[16] Baymard’s UX research echoes this, stating that “The optimal line length for body text is 50–60 characters per line, including spaces… Other sources suggest that up to 75 characters is acceptable,” and recommending CSS `max-width` values to enforce this.[18] These findings imply that résumé text lines should be neither too short nor too long; lines around 50–75 characters are optimal.

On an A4 page, which is 210 mm wide, margins must be chosen to yield a text block width corresponding to approximately 45–75 characters per line at 11‑point Calibri or Arial. Typical Word defaults for A4 use margins of about 2.54 cm (25.4 mm) on each side, leaving a text width of roughly 159 mm. At 11‑point Calibri or Arial, each character is approximately 2.5–3 mm wide, depending on letter, which yields roughly 50–60 characters per line in a 159‑mm block. This is well within the recommended 45–75 character range.[16][18] If margins are narrowed to 1.5 cm (15 mm), the text width increases to 180 mm, potentially pushing line lengths beyond 75 characters and reducing readability for dense prose.[16][18] Conversely, margins wider than 3 cm (30 mm) would reduce text width to around 150 mm or less, making lines shorter and potentially wasting space in a constrained two‑page résumé.

Therefore, a high‑confidence, empirically grounded recommendation is to set **page margins** to approximately **2–2.5 cm** on all sides for an A4 résumé, yielding a text block width that produces line lengths in the optimal 50–75‑character range for 11‑point Calibri or Arial.[16][18] This corresponds to using Word’s standard “Normal” margins of 2.54 cm or slightly reduced margins of 2 cm to adjust page count, while avoiding very narrow margins for dense content. It aligns with Westpac’s requirement for white space and readability, even though Westpac does not specify the numbers.[20] For ATS parsing, margins have negligible direct impact; the main requirement is that content is in one column. However, extremely narrow margins can create long lines that are harder for human readers to follow, especially when scanning printed copies.

Line length in characters is directly related to margin and font size. With 11‑point Calibri on an A4 page with 2.54‑cm margins, typical lines will be within 50–70 characters, satisfying both Tinker’s 45–55‑character ideal and Baymard’s 50–60‑character optimum, while staying below the 75‑character upper bound.[16][18] This is particularly important for dense sections like employment history and skills lists, where long lines can become visually tiring. For banking professionals whose résumés may be read quickly by busy hiring managers, line length should make scanning easy; the recommended margin specification supports that goal.

## File Formats and ATS Compatibility

### DOCX versus PDF for ATS and Human Readers

File format choice influences how résumés are parsed by ATS and viewed by hiring managers. ATS documentation from SAP SuccessFactors and Greenhouse indicates that both DOCX and PDF are widely accepted, but that certain formats and layouts may be more reliable. SAP SuccessFactors’ “Working with Resume Parsing” page explains that, in the Candidate Profile tab, candidates are prompted to upload a resume for parsing and that they “can upload resume with DOCX, PPT, and TXT format,” as well as image types such as TIFF, JPG, BMP and GIF.[12] Another SAP help page on candidate attachments states that “Resume can be imported as part of the Profile import” and that “The following file types are supported: CSV, DOC, DOCX, GIF, JPEG, JPG, PDF, and TXT.”[13] This indicates that SAP’s recruiting module can handle both DOCX and PDF resumes, but the parsing engine is optimised for textual formats like DOCX and TXT, while PDFs and images may require additional handling.

Greenhouse’s documentation and interpretive guides provide more granular detail. ATSPrep’s Greenhouse guide notes that Greenhouse “Accepts PDF, DOC, DOCX, RTF, TXT” for resumes and that parsing stops above 2.5 MB.[11] The Greenhouse support article reiterates that “Greenhouse Recruiting can't parse resumes larger than 2.5MB” and lists complex layouts and headers/footers among the causes of parse failure.[14] ResumeAdapter’s analysis explains that “First it extracts text from your upload, which must be doc, docx, pdf, rtf or txt,” and that Greenhouse accepts uploads up to 100 MB but “cannot parse anything larger than 2.5 MB.”[15] It also lists specific failure causes, including columns, tables, headers and footers, contact details in a header or text box, graphics, photos, word art, and image‑only files.[15] Together, these statements show that both DOCX and PDF are acceptable in principle, provided that the file contains actual text and remains under the parse‑limit size, but that complex or graphical layouts are more likely to fail.

For human readers, PDFs offer consistent layout across devices and operating systems, preserving margins, fonts and spacing. DOCX files can reflow depending on software versions and default styles, but they are easier to edit and sometimes preferred by recruiters who want to adjust formatting. Recruiter advice from Robert Half acknowledges the need for both formats, recommending that candidates choose templates suitable for both digital and printed formats and keep design clean.[10] Westpac’s CV guide recommends saving the CV with a simple file name such as “FirstNameLastName.doc,” implying that Word format is acceptable and perhaps preferred for initial submissions, especially if internal systems convert or parse DOCX files.[20]

Given these considerations, the safest and most practical approach for an experienced banking professional is to maintain the résumé in **DOCX** format for editing and ATS uploads, and to generate a clean **PDF** version with embedded fonts for direct emailing and printing, checking that the PDF remains under 2.5 MB and contains selectable text, not scanned images.[11][12][13][14][15][20] When applying through ATS portals, candidates should follow the specific instructions in the job ad or portal: if DOCX is requested, they should upload DOCX; if PDF is allowed or preferred, they should ensure the PDF is text‑based and ATS‑friendly. The layout should be identical in both formats, with a single column, no tables or graphics, and contact details in the body. This dual‑format strategy offers high compatibility and robustness.

### Layouts Documented to Parse Correctly

ATS documentation gives clear guidance on which layouts parse correctly. Greenhouse, as interpreted by ATSPrep, states that resumes should avoid columns and tables and that the document should read top to bottom in one pass.[11] The ATSPrep checklist emphasises keeping the file under 2.5 MB, placing contact details in the body rather than header/footer, using one column with no tables, spelling job titles out in full, including company legal identifiers such as Inc. or Ltd, and avoiding spaced‑out letters and decorative headings.[11] The Greenhouse support article lists parse‑failure causes including “Complex resumes with tables, headers, and footers,” “Resumes with the name and contact information in the header, footer, or text box,” and “Resumes that have a columned layout,” along with oversized files.[14] ResumeAdapter’s breakdown confirms that columns, tables, headers, footers, graphics, photos, word art, image‑only files, bare company names and abbreviated job titles are all problematic.[15] These consistent statements provide high‑confidence evidence that ATS‑compatible layouts must be one‑column, text‑only, with clear headings and sections, and no complex structural elements.

SAP SuccessFactors documentation does not list layout failure causes, but its limitation to textual formats for parsing and its acceptance of PDFs and images for attachments suggest that plain text layouts in DOCX and TXT are safest for parsing, while PDFs and images may be less reliably parsed.[12][13] In general, ATS vendors design parsers to handle straightforward resumes with standard headings such as “Experience,” “Education,” “Skills” and “Summary,” and with consistent formatting for job entries (job title, employer, dates).[11][12][13][14][15]

Consequently, the documented ATS‑safe layout for resumes includes the following structural and typographic parameters: one column from top to bottom; contact details in the body at the top; section headings in plain text without spaced letters or decorative fonts; job entries where each line lists full job title, full employer name and dates; bullet points with simple dot symbols; no photos, logos, tables or multi‑column designs; and a file size comfortably below 2.5 MB, typically achieved by using plain text and avoiding embedded images.[11][14][15] This layout is fully compatible with Calibri, Arial or Aptos fonts at 11‑point, moderate line spacing and standard margins, as these typographic features do not affect parsing. For banking professionals applying through corporate ATS portals, adhering to these layout constraints is essential for ensuring that their résumé is correctly read and indexed.

## Synthesised Layout Specifications for Australian Banking Résumés

### Convergence and Divergence: Is There One Definitive Layout?

The core question is whether Australian guidance and evidence converge on a single definitive résumé layout or whether they support a small set of accepted layouts. On structural elements such as single‑column design, reverse‑chronological work history, inclusion of contact details at the top, use of section headings, bullet points and plain fonts, there is strong convergence across government, recruiters, ATS vendors and corporate employers.[1][2][4][5][6][8][9][10][11][14][15][20] DEWR, APS and I Work For NSW all describe CVs and résumés as multi‑section documents with personal details, education, work experience, other relevant activities and referees.[1][2][4][6] Randstad and Robert Half prescribe reverse‑chronological employment history, key skills sections and clean headings.[9][10] Westpac’s CV guide emphasises employment history in chronological order, starting with the most recent role, and stresses plain layout and white space.[20] ATS documentation from SAP SuccessFactors and Greenhouse reinforces the need for simple, text‑based, one‑column layouts without tables or graphics.[11][12][13][14][15] These consistent statements support a high‑confidence conclusion that a single‑column, reverse‑chronological, text‑only layout is the dominant and preferred résumé format in corporate Australia.

However, there is divergence on page count, degree of narrative detail and precise section ordering, particularly between generic government advice and corporate banking guidance. DEWR recommends keeping résumés to no more than two pages, while Westpac suggests that CVs can comfortably be three to four pages.[4][20] APS notes that most CVs are between two and six pages, reflecting the more expansive public‑sector practice.[1][2] Recruiters such as Robert Half suggest two to three pages.[10] This shows that page count is not governed by a single definitive rule; rather, it varies with context and seniority. Similarly, government CV templates often place education earlier and may emphasise selection criteria statements, whereas corporate templates focus on professional summary and work experience. ATS constraints apply uniformly across contexts, but employers interpret résumé and CV formats differently.

Typography research introduces another dimension of divergence: while Tinker’s work points to 11‑point type and 1.2 line spacing as favourites for adult readers, more recent web accessibility guidelines favour 1.5 line spacing and additional paragraph spacing.[16][17][18][19] Résumé guidance does not directly cite these studies, leading to a situation where typography choices in résumés are informed by general design practice rather than codified standards. For example, DEWR recommends Calibri or Arial in size 11, but does not specify line spacing.[4] Recruiters and employers emphasise readability and white space, but do not provide numeric spacing values.[9][10][20] As a result, there is no definitive typographic specification for line spacing, margins or heading sizes; instead, there is a range of acceptable values consistent with legibility research and practical constraints.

Given this mixture of convergence and divergence, the most accurate answer is that there is **no single definitive layout**, but there is a very narrow band of **accepted layouts** that differ mainly in length and narrative emphasis, rather than in core structure or typographic style. For an experienced professional applying to corporate roles in Australian banking in 2026, three closely related layouts emerge as robust options: an ATS‑optimised two‑page résumé; a slightly more expansive corporate CV of two to three pages; and a senior‑level narrative CV of three to four pages used where employer guidance explicitly permits longer documents. All three share the same one‑column, text‑only, reverse‑chronological structure and the same typographic baseline.

### Layout 1: ATS‑Optimised Two‑Page Résumé

The first layout is an ATS‑optimised résumé designed to fit on two A4 pages and to parse cleanly in systems like Greenhouse and SAP SuccessFactors. Its specification, synthesised from government guidance, recruiter advice, ATS documentation and typography research, is as follows.

The **page size** is A4, \(210 \times 297\) mm, standard in Australia. **Margins** are set to approximately **2.54 cm** (Word’s “Normal”) on all sides, yielding a text block width that produces line lengths of roughly 50–70 characters at 11‑point Calibri or Arial, consistent with legibility research’s recommended 45–55 characters per line and Baymard’s 50–60‑character optimal range.[16][18] Margins can be slightly reduced to **2 cm** if necessary to accommodate content without exceeding two pages, but narrower margins are discouraged.[16][18][20]

The **typeface** is Calibri, Arial or Aptos for body text, with a size of **11 points**.[4][10][16] DEWR explicitly recommends Calibri or Arial in size 11, and ERIC research identifies 11‑point as favourite for adult readers.[4][16] The **candidate’s name** at the top is set in the same font family at **18–20 points**, bold, to ensure prominence without shallow typography.[4][10][20] **Section headings** such as “Professional Summary,” “Skills and Attributes,” “Employment History” and “Education” are set at **13–14 points**, bold or semi‑bold, with sentence case (e.g., “Employment History” rather than all caps), and colour kept to black or a very dark grey for printing and ATS clarity.[4][7][20]

Line spacing in Word is set to a **multiple of 1.15–1.2** for body text, consistent with Tinker’s recommendation of two points of leading and Microsoft’s finding that 1.2 line spacing improves reading speed.[16][17] This ensures comfortable readability while keeping content within two pages. Paragraph spacing is set to roughly **3 points after** for body paragraphs, and bullet lists use the same line spacing with minimal additional spacing between bullets, plus **3 points before and after** the list to separate it from surrounding text. Section headings have **12 points before** and **6 points after** to create clear visual separation and a navigable hierarchy.[18][20] These spacing values are chosen to achieve Westpac’s goal of “plenty of white space” and the Style Manual’s emphasis on accessible line spacing.[7][20]

The **structure** follows a consistent section order tailored to experienced banking professionals: on page one, contact details at the very top of the body, including name, phone number, email, LinkedIn URL and city or state (not placed in headers or footers), followed by a two‑to‑three‑sentence **Professional Summary** that matches the role and employer; a concise **Key Skills** section listing technical and behavioural skills relevant to banking (e.g., credit analysis, regulatory compliance, stakeholder management), formatted as bullet points; and the start of the **Employment History** section.[4][5][6][9][10][11][14][15][20] On page two, Employment History continues, followed by **Education and Qualifications** (highest degree first, then other relevant courses), **Licences and Accreditations** (e.g., RG146, CFA, relevant industry certifications), **Professional Memberships** (e.g., FINSIA, CPA Australia), and **Referees**, each listed with name, title, organisation and contact details, as recommended by DEWR and Westpac.[4][5][20]

Each **employment entry** begins with a line containing job title, employer and dates of employment. For example, “Senior Relationship Manager – Westpac Banking Corporation – Mar 2018 – Jul 2022.” Dates are inline, not right‑aligned, and written with months and years. Beneath this line, bullet points describe key responsibilities and achievements, emphasizing quantifiable outcomes and aligned with recruiter advice to focus on achievements.[4][9][10][20] Multiple roles at the same employer are presented under a single employer heading, with separate job‑title lines and date ranges, indented bullets under each. This structure reads top‑to‑bottom in one column and avoids tables or complex formatting.

There are **no photos, logos, tables, columns, word art or graphical elements**. The document uses only plain text and simple horizontal rules if necessary, but these are kept to a minimum.[11][14][15][20] Contact details are in the body, not in headers or footers, and the file size is kept well below **2.5 MB** by avoiding images, ensuring successful parsing in Greenhouse.[11][14][15] The file is saved as DOCX for ATS submission and converted to PDF for emailing or printing, with both versions sharing identical content and layout.[12][13][20]

This ATS‑optimised layout suits applicants applying through large corporate ATS portals and recruiters, where automated parsing and high‑volume screening dictate strict adherence to plain, text‑only standards. It balances legibility, brevity and compatibility for experienced banking professionals.

### Layout 2: Corporate CV for Banking Roles (Two to Three Pages)

The second layout is a corporate CV intended for direct submissions to banking employers like Westpac, where employer guidance permits longer CVs and emphasizes narrative career storytelling. It shares the core structure of Layout 1 but allows for **two to three pages** and slightly more narrative detail, while still maintaining ATS compatibility.

The **page size** remains A4, and **margins** are set to **2–2.54 cm** on all sides, as in Layout 1, to maintain optimal line length and white space.[16][18][20] **Typeface** is again Calibri, Arial or Aptos at **11‑point** for body text, with name at **20 points** bold, and section headings at **14 points**.[4][10][16][20] Line spacing can be slightly more generous, at a Word multiple of **1.2–1.3**, or up to **1.5** if the CV extends to three pages and content is reasonably spaced.[17][18][19] Paragraph spacing can be increased modestly, to **6 points after** paragraphs, and headings can have **12 points before and after**, enhancing white space.[18][20]

The **section order** is similar but includes more narrative emphasis. Page one begins with contact details and name, then a longer **Professional Summary** of three to five sentences, articulating career trajectory, current role, key strengths and career objectives, as Westpac suggests summarising experience and articulating career objectives, especially when transitioning environments.[20] A **Career Highlights** or **Key Achievements** section may follow, listing three to five major accomplishments across roles, in bullet form. Next comes **Skills and Attributes** tailored to the job advertisement, as DEWR and Job Jumpstart recommend matching skills to employer keywords.[4][5][20] Employment History then begins, listing roles in chronological order starting with the most recent, as Westpac directs.[20]

Page two continues Employment History and may include additional sections such as **Projects**, **Leadership Experience** or **Community Involvement**, as relevant. Education, licences, memberships and referees are placed on page two or three depending on space. Each section is clearly headed and separated by white space, consistent with Westpac’s emphasis on context and contributions, DEWR’s guidance on including paid and unpaid work, and recruiters’ focus on achievements.[4][9][10][20]

Bullet points under each role can be slightly more narrative, describing context and problem‑solving, as Westpac encourages, but they remain concise and achievement‑focused.[20] The CV remains free of tables, columns and graphics, ensuring ATS compatibility even if submitted through portals.[11][14][15] Colour use is minimal, and fonts and spacing adhere to the same principles as Layout 1, but with slightly more room for narrative.

This layout suits experienced banking professionals applying directly to major employers or via recruiters who expect more detailed CVs. It leverages Westpac’s guidance that CVs can stretch to three or four pages while retaining clarity and white space, making it appropriate for mid‑senior roles with complex experience.[20] It remains ATS‑safe by preserving one‑column, text‑only structure.

### Layout 3: Senior Executive Narrative CV (Three to Four Pages, Employer‑Permitted)

The third layout is a narrative CV intended for senior executive roles where employer guidance explicitly allows longer documents and where the applicant’s career story spans multiple decades. It is used primarily when employers like Westpac or public‑sector agencies indicate that CVs of three to four pages are acceptable or expected.[1][2][8][20]

The **page size** remains A4, with **margins** of **2.54 cm** or slightly wider (**3 cm**) to create more white space and shorter lines for dense narrative, still within legibility bounds.[16][18][20] **Typeface** is Calibri, Arial or Times New Roman at **11‑point** for body text, with name at **20–22 points** bold and headings at **14–16 points**.[4][10][16][20] Line spacing may be set at a Word multiple of **1.3–1.5**, aligning with web accessibility guidelines and the Springer finding that 1.5 line spacing with sans‑serif fonts improves comprehension.[18][19] Paragraph spacing can be **6–12 points after**, and headings spaced **12–18 points before and after**, enhancing section separation.[18][20]

The **structure** expands to include sections such as **Executive Summary**, **Core Competencies**, **Career Overview**, **Key Leadership Roles**, **Board and Committee Positions**, **Selected Achievements**, **Education**, **Professional Development** and **Community and Industry Engagement**. Public‑sector examples like APS and AFP SES CV guidance focus on summarising experience and achievements concisely and aligning CV with work level standards, which can guide senior banking CV structure.[1][2][8] Each major career stage is described in paragraphs, with bullet points for key outcomes, and sections for board roles and external contributions.

Dates, employers and job titles are presented inline as in Layout 1 and 2, but narrative paragraphs may accompany them to articulate strategic responsibilities and impact. ATS compatibility is maintained by avoiding tables, columns and graphics and by keeping contact details in the body.[11][14][15] File size remains under 2.5 MB by using text only, even across four pages.

This layout suits senior executives applying to roles where detailed context, governance experience and external leadership must be documented and where employer guidance explicitly allows longer CVs. It remains within the convergent constraints—one column, plain fonts, reverse chronological career narrative—but diverges in length and narrative richness.[1][2][8][20] It may be overkill for standard corporate banking roles and is thus reserved for clearly senior positions.

## Areas of Genuine Uncertainty and Recent Trends

Several aspects of résumé layout and typography remain unsettled despite the available guidance. Exact **margin values** and **heading sizes** are not specified in any of the sources; recommendations in this report for 2–2.54 cm margins and 13–14‑point headings are inferential, based on legibility research and common practice, rather than codified standards.[16][18][20] No source offers a sentence such as “Margins should be 2 cm on all sides,” leaving margin choices to designer judgement. Similarly, the **precise numeric line spacing** for résumés is not mandated; DEWR and Westpac discuss white space qualitatively but do not say “Use 1.5 line spacing.”[4][20] Tinker/Microsoft research supports 1.2; web accessibility guidelines support 1.5; résumé practice lies between these.

The **inclusion of months in date ranges** is also not explicitly addressed. DEWR’s example uses year‑only ranges; Westpac mentions time periods without specifying months; recruiter practice often uses months and years but without explicit written rules.[4][20] As a result, month‑and‑year ranges are a widely adopted convention rather than a requirement. The **treatment of multiple roles at one employer** is similarly guided by general résumé standards rather than explicit sentences in the sources; nested employer headings with indented role entries are common but not mandated in the cited documents.

The **use of colour** beyond black and grayscale is another area of uncertainty. Westpac advises avoiding photos and gimmicks and using a plain, clean layout, which suggests minimal or no colour; ATS documentation warns against graphics and word art but does not mention colour specifically.[11][14][15][20] Many modern templates use muted colour accents for headings; whether these affect ATS parsing is unclear, as ATS reads the underlying text regardless of colour. Consequently, colour use remains a matter of judgement and employer preference, with a conservative bias toward monochrome for banking roles.

The **trajectory of practice** shows increasing attention to ATS compatibility and accessibility. Greenhouse’s explicit parsing limits and failure causes were published and clarified over recent years, highlighting practical issues like file size, headers/footers and columns.[11][14][15] SAP SuccessFactors documentation similarly confirms support for text formats and separate handling of attachments.[12][13] Web accessibility guidelines, as reflected in Baymard’s research, have influenced line‑spacing and spacing norms, encouraging more generous spacing and clear separation of text units.[18] The Australian Government Style Manual’s emphasis on Year 7 reading level and readability factors reflects a broader trend toward accessible writing, which in turn influences résumé design.[7]

At the same time, some traditional distinctions between résumé and CV have blurred. Westpac notes that “The two terms have become interchangeable in Australia – but it is nonetheless a CV that is the commonly used format here,” indicating that the market increasingly treats résumé and CV as synonyms, with CV lengths and structures being normal in corporate hiring.[20] This convergence has reduced the emphasis on one‑page résumés, particularly for experienced professionals, and increased the acceptability of two‑ to three‑page documents.

## Conclusion

Synthesising guidance from Australian government agencies, public‑sector employers, major recruiters, ATS documentation, large banking employers and typography research, the evidence supports a clear, though not absolutely rigid, set of best practices for résumé layout, structure and typography in the corporate Australian job market in 2026. For an experienced professional applying to banking roles, the dominant and safest layout is a single‑column A4 résumé, in Word and PDF, using Calibri, Arial or Aptos at 11‑point for body text, line spacing around 1.2 in Word, margins around 2–2.54 cm, section headings for contact details, professional summary, skills, employment history, education, licences, memberships and referees, and bullet points for responsibilities and achievements.[1][2][4][5][6][9][10][11][14][15][16][17][18][19][20]

There is no single definitive layout; instead, there are two or three closely related accepted layouts differing mainly in length and narrative depth. An ATS‑optimised two‑page résumé is ideal for initial submissions through corporate portals and recruiters, balancing brevity and detail. A slightly longer two‑ to three‑page corporate CV suits direct submissions to banking employers who expect more narrative and context, as Westpac’s guidance suggests. A three‑ to four‑page executive CV is appropriate for senior roles where employer instructions explicitly allow longer documents. All of these layouts share the essential features of plain, text‑only, one‑column structure, reverse‑chronological employment history, clear section headings, concise bullet points, and the absence of tables, columns, graphics and header‑embedded contact details, ensuring ATS compatibility and human readability.[4][6][9][10][11][14][15][20]

Key unsettled areas include precise numeric margin and spacing values, heading sizes, month inclusion in date ranges, and acceptable levels of colour use. These are matters of informed typographic judgement rather than regulation. Nonetheless, legibility research on type size, line spacing and line length provides strong guidance that supports the choices recommended here: 11‑point body text, moderate line spacing, and line lengths around 50–75 characters with margins of approximately 2–2.54 cm.[16][17][18][19] For banking professionals, adherence to these norms will produce résumés that are both accessible and compelling, meeting the expectations of corporate employers, public‑sector agencies and ATS systems alike.

In applying these specifications, candidates should always read job advertisements and employer instructions carefully, tailor their summaries and skills to the role, and ensure that their résumés are error‑free, factual and backed by real achievements, as DEWR, APS, Westpac and recruiters consistently advise.[1][2][4][9][10][20] Within the narrow band of accepted layouts, thoughtful tailoring and clear storytelling are what differentiate successful applications in the competitive corporate Australian banking market.
