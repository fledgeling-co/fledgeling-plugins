# Empirical Foundations for Defensible Product‑Positioning Decisions and Decision Aids  

The core finding of this review is that there is no single “magic” positioning framework or decision aid that is both theoretically elegant and empirically validated across markets; instead, defensible positioning work combines a small number of clearly differentiated territories derived from robust customer‑need frameworks such as Jobs‑to‑Be‑Done and Outcome‑Driven Innovation, tested empirically using a layered sequence of survey and behavioural experiments, and then chosen using decision aids that emphasise transparency and sensitivity analysis rather than spurious precision. This synthesis draws on empirical marketing‑science work on conjoint and choice‑based experiments, outcome‑driven needs analysis, pricing research, multi‑criteria decision analysis, and startup failure post‑mortems to propose a concrete design for an AI assistant that generates positioning territories and an interactive decision aid. The weight of the evidence suggests that additive weighted scoring can be defensible only when its assumptions are made explicit, it is applied to a very small option set, and the tool helps users explore how rankings change under plausible weight ranges rather than outputting a single “answer.” At the same time, the evidence base for more exotic multi‑criteria methods such as AHP, PROMETHEE, TOPSIS, or VIKOR in strategic positioning contexts is surprisingly thin; where they have been evaluated, they exhibit documented pathologies such as rank reversal, inconsistency, and sensitivity to normalisation schemes, making them risky defaults for an automated agent.[11][12][13][18]  

Within that constraint, the most defensible design for your agent is to generate three to five positioning candidates anchored in distinct customer jobs and outcome segments, to treat each candidate as a testable hypothesis, and to require that any recommended “winner” has passed minimum empirical thresholds: at least one statistically powered message or concept test in the target audience, explicit willingness‑to‑pay evidence from convergent methods, and at least one behavioural validation such as a fake‑door or landing‑page experiment in the intended go‑to‑market channel.[6][7][8][9][10][19][20]

## Detailed Findings  

### 1. What does the empirical evidence say about producing a defensible product‑positioning decision?  

#### 1.1 Clarifying what “defensible” means in product positioning  

For the purposes of this review, a product‑positioning decision is **defensible** if it satisfies three criteria: it is grounded in an explicit, empirically testable theory of how the product creates value for a defined customer in a defined context; it is supported by empirical evidence from the relevant target audience that the proposed position is both understandable and attractive; and the decision process itself can be scrutinised and shown not to rely on arbitrary or unstable modelling assumptions. This definition aligns with both the JTBD/ODI emphasis on customer‑defined outcomes and the marketing‑science focus on predictive validity of research methods.[1][2][8][15][16][20]  

From an epistemic standpoint, defensibility requires that positioning decisions can, in principle, be falsified by data. A positioning statement such as “the easiest way for freelancers to get paid” is defensible only if “easiest” is tied to measurable outcomes (for example, number of steps, error rate, time to payment) and the company can show that its product delivers materially better performance on these metrics than alternatives, or that target customers perceive it as doing so.[1][2][15] This emphasis on measurable outcomes is central to Outcome‑Driven Innovation, which defines customer needs as desired outcomes that can be quantified in terms of importance and satisfaction, and then treats positioning and innovation as efforts to improve those outcomes.[1][2][15]  

A defensible decision process also requires transparency about the assumptions embedded in any decision aids used. For example, a weighted‑sum scoring model implicitly assumes that overall utility is a linear additive function of criterion scores and weights, \(U(x) = \sum_i w_i s_i(x)\), and that criteria are independent; if these assumptions do not hold, the model’s recommendations may not reflect actual preferences.[11][12][13][18] Empirical work in multi‑criteria decision analysis shows that violations of these assumptions can lead to rank reversal and other paradoxes, where the “best” option changes when irrelevant alternatives are added or when scores are rescaled.[11][12][13] A defensible positioning decision therefore uses such models cautiously, as tools for structuring thinking rather than as mechanical arbiters.  

Finally, a defensible positioning decision must survive contact with the market. This means that the decision is not only well‑reasoned and empirically supported ex ante, but also monitored ex post, with feedback loops that allow adjustment if early market signals diverge from expectations. Studies of startup failures underscore that many founders make strategic bets, including positioning choices, without adequate information‑seeking and then fail to adjust when evidence accumulates that their assumptions were wrong.[19] A defensible process is one in which the agent—and the human team—can show that they sought relevant data, subjected their hypotheses to empirical tests, and adjusted their course when warranted.  

#### 1.2 Positioning and category‑design frameworks with empirical or documented validation  

##### 1.2.1 Jobs‑to‑Be‑Done and Outcome‑Driven Innovation  

Jobs‑to‑Be‑Done (JTBD) theory posits that customers “hire” products and services to get specific “jobs” done; the relevant unit of analysis is the underlying job or process, not the product category.[1] This conceptual shift is not just rhetorical; it has methodological consequences. Strategyn’s JTBD framework, for example, defines a market as a group of people and the job they are trying to get done, and then decomposes each job into a universal job map of eight process steps: define, locate, prepare, confirm, execute, monitor, modify, and conclude.[1] Each step is associated with multiple desired outcomes, which are phrased as metrics such as “minimise the time to …” or “minimise the likelihood that …”.[1][15]  

Outcome‑Driven Innovation (ODI), developed by Tony Ulwick and popularised by Strategyn, operationalises JTBD by representing customer needs as these desired outcomes and then quantifying the degree to which each outcome is underserved or overserved.[1][2][15][16] The ODI process involves: defining the customer and the core functional job in solution‑independent terms; uncovering customer needs by using qualitative research and the universal job map to elicit 50–150 desired outcomes per market; conducting quantitative surveys to rate the importance and current satisfaction of each outcome; identifying underserved outcomes (high importance, low satisfaction); and then segmenting customers based on patterns of unmet outcomes.[1][2][15] The segments discovered this way are often invisible to demographic or behavioural segmentation alone, because they are defined by patterns of unmet needs rather than demographics.[1][2][15]  

Empirical validation of ODI comes from two kinds of evidence: vendor‑reported track records and independent case studies. Strategyn claims that products developed using ODI have an 86% commercial success rate, compared to an industry average of 10–30%.[15][16] The 86% figure is derived from more than 400 ODI projects over 30 years and represents the fraction of products that met or exceeded their commercial targets within three years of launch, according to client‑reported data.[15][16] A critical analysis by Myles (a practitioner and independent analyst) examines this claim and concludes that while the figure is not independently audited and may be subject to selection bias, the underlying mechanism—using quantitatively validated, underserved outcomes to guide product and positioning decisions—is consistent with broader research on why products succeed or fail.[16] The analysis notes that general product success rates in the literature, when cancelled projects are included in the denominator, are indeed in the 15–30% range, so a materially higher success rate among ODI‑guided projects is plausible if those projects are systematically better aligned with validated customer needs.[16]  

A peer‑reviewed case study in the MDPI journal Sustainability applies ODI to hotel services, demonstrating how outcome‑based analysis can reveal specific service opportunities.[2] The authors define job‑to‑be‑done in the context of hotel stays and represent customer needs as desired outcomes, such as maximising safety when paying deposits or maximising the possibility of avoiding waiting in the lobby.[2] They collect data on the importance and satisfaction of these outcomes and compute service opportunity scores based on the gaps, identifying which outcomes are both important and poorly satisfied.[2] The study shows that ODI’s outcome‑gap metric can systematically prioritise service innovations and strategic directions. Although the paper is a single case study with limited generalisability, it meets the inclusion criteria as a peer‑reviewed empirical application and provides evidence that outcome‑based need representation and scoring can be operationalised and linked to concrete service improvements.[2]  

The key empirical takeaway for your AI agent is that JTBD/ODI frameworks offer a structured way to generate positioning territories that are anchored in measurable customer outcomes rather than arbitrary value propositions. By defining each positioning candidate as “our product helps [segment] get [job] done by improving [set of outcomes]”, and by tying those outcomes to quantifiable importance and satisfaction scores, the agent can generate hypotheses that are both testable and comparable across options.[1][2][15][16] While the exact success rate claims should be treated cautiously, there is convergent evidence that outcome‑driven methods reduce the risk of building solutions for non‑existent or low‑priority problems.[2][16][19]  

##### 1.2.2 Category design and category‑making  

Category design is the deliberate creation and shaping of a new market category—rather than competing within existing categories—with the aim of becoming the de facto leader of that category.[4][17] Russell McGuire, writing in a practitioner context, distinguishes three challenges in category‑making: defining the category (clarifying what is in and out of scope), establishing the category (helping customers recognise the problem and the unique way the new category solves it), and owning the category (maintaining leadership once others enter).[4] Category design frameworks, such as those discussed in Techstars’ “Create New Markets With Category Design,” emphasise focusing on the problem, coining a category name, starting new conversations, and developing a point of view that frames the market on terms favourable to the category creator.[4][17]  

While these frameworks are conceptually compelling, the empirical evidence base for category design is limited. There is a relative paucity of peer‑reviewed studies that systematically evaluate the success rates of intentional category‑creation efforts versus more incremental positioning strategies. Critiques of related frameworks such as Blue Ocean Strategy (BOS) highlight broader concerns about the empirical foundation of “create uncontested market space” narratives. A recent article in The International Journal of Business and Management critically examines BOS’s concepts of value innovation, competitor elimination, and customer acquisition, concluding that the model lacks a clear implementation protocol and has limited scientific validation.[5] The authors argue that while BOS is attractive in principle, its practical applicability is constrained by vague procedures and a lack of rigorous empirical testing; they recommend further research and refinement to enhance its practical utility.[5] This critique meets the inclusion criteria as a peer‑reviewed conceptual analysis and underscores that many category‑creation frameworks, BOS included, have not been validated in a way that would satisfy scientific standards.[5]

##### 1.2.3 Brand‑positioning statement research and message frameworks  

Traditional brand‑positioning work often culminates in a positioning statement, which articulates the target customer, frame of reference, point of difference, and reason to believe. While much of the literature on positioning statements is conceptual, there is a growing practice of empirically testing these statements—or their derivative messages—before full commitment. SurveyMonkey’s guidance on developing positioning in marketing, though a vendor resource, summarises standard practice: first identify current market position and competitive landscape; then perform competitor positioning analysis; then create a positioning statement by answering who the target customer is, what category the product falls into, what benefits and differentiators it offers, and what proof supports these claims; finally, test the positioning by gathering feedback from targeted market segments.[3]  

SurveyMonkey’s LaunchPad for message and claims testing provides more concrete methodological detail.[9] It uses monadic testing, where each respondent sees only one message in isolation, to avoid comparison bias.[9] Respondents rate messages on attributes such as clarity, credibility, relevance, and uniqueness, and the platform generates scorecards, significance tests, and driver analyses to identify which attributes explain performance differences.[9] While these tools are promotional and not peer‑reviewed, they codify widely accepted research practices—monadic concept testing, attribute‑level diagnostics, and use of significance testing—to evaluate positioning and messaging options.[9][SECONDARY: promotional]  

The empirical evidence from marketing‑science research aligns with this practice: monadic concept tests, when properly designed and sampled, can provide reliable measures of comprehension and appeal, and can predict subsequent performance in the market, especially when combined with other methods such as conjoint analysis.[8][20] However, their predictive power depends heavily on sampling target‑representative respondents, using realistic stimuli, and measuring outcomes that correlate with real behaviour, such as purchase intent anchored in realistic contexts or preference shares derived from choice tasks.[8][20]  

For your agent, the key point is that brand‑positioning statements should not be treated as purely internal documents. Instead, each candidate statement should be translated into testable messages and subjected to structured empirical evaluation in the target audience. A defensible agent would therefore couple its generation of positioning territories with automatic generation of testable messages and a recommended message‑testing protocol (for example, monadic tests with specified sample sizes and attribute metrics).[3][9]

#### 1.3 Empirical testing of positioning and messaging candidates before commitment  

One of the central design questions for your AI agent is how to validate positioning and messaging candidates before recommending commitment. The empirical literature and practitioner practice converge on a layered approach: use survey‑based methods to elicit preferences and understandings, then complement with behavioural experiments such as fake‑door tests or landing‑page experiments.  

##### 1.3.1 Message testing and monadic concept evaluation  

Message testing is the practice of exposing respondents to candidate messages or creative executions and measuring their reactions on dimensions such as clarity, relevance, credibility, uniqueness, and purchase intent.[9] SurveyMonkey’s LaunchPad, as an example of commercial practice, uses a monadic methodology in which each respondent sees only one message, thereby avoiding the comparison bias that arises when multiple options are seen side‑by‑side.[9] Respondents rate the message on multiple attributes, and the platform provides automated scorecards, statistical significance tests, and key‑driver analyses indicating which attributes drive overall performance.[9]  

Monadic testing is grounded in decades of concept‑testing research, which shows that when stimuli are evaluated individually, ratings are less influenced by relative comparisons and better reflect absolute perceptions.[8][20] However, monadic tests can be less efficient in terms of sample size because each concept requires its own group of respondents; split‑sample designs and sequential monadic designs are often used to balance cost and precision.<INFERENCE from="[Standard concept‑testing practice and the monadic methodology description in [9]]">For your agent, recommending monadic or sequential monadic message tests with sample sizes on the order of 100–200 respondents per key segment per concept is a defensible default for early‑stage positioning evaluation.</INFERENCE>[8][9][20]  

The validity of message testing depends strongly on the alignment between the test context and the real decision context. For example, asking respondents how “appealing” a message is in a survey is less predictive than embedding messages in realistic choice tasks where respondents must choose between competing offers, prices, and messages.[8][20] Thus, message testing is best seen as a diagnostic tool to screen and refine candidates, rather than as a sole basis for final positioning decisions.

##### 1.3.2 MaxDiff, conjoint, and choice‑based methods  

MaxDiff (Maximum Difference scaling) and conjoint analysis are two central tools for quantifying preferences among attributes, features, or messages.[8] Sawtooth Software, a leading provider of conjoint tools, explains that conjoint analysis is designed to understand how consumers value multiple attributes with multiple levels—such as brand, price, and features—and to predict preference for thousands of potential product combinations.[8] In contrast, MaxDiff is used when the goal is to prioritise a list of items—often 12 to 50—on a single dimension such as importance or preference.[8]  

In MaxDiff, respondents are shown sets of items and asked to indicate the most and least preferred (or important) item in each set; statistical models then recover a relative importance score for each item.[8] This makes MaxDiff ideal for ranking features, benefits, or messages; it has been shown to produce more discriminating and reliable importance scores than simple rating scales, which suffer from scale‑use bias and limited discrimination.[8] For positioning work, MaxDiff can be used to prioritise value propositions or benefit statements within or across positioning territories.  

Conjoint analysis, particularly choice‑based conjoint (CBC), presents respondents with sets of hypothetical product profiles constructed from combinations of attribute levels and asks them to choose the most preferred option in each set.[8][20] Statistical models then estimate part‑worth utilities for each attribute level, allowing prediction of choice probabilities for new combinations.[8][20] A key advantage of conjoint is that it captures trade‑offs between attributes, including price, making it a powerful tool for testing positioning territories that differ in feature bundles, value propositions, and prices.[8][20]  

The external validity of conjoint, especially choice‑based conjoint, has been examined in several studies. One well‑known article on “Real world performance of choice‑based conjoint models” reports that CBC models, when properly specified and estimated (for example, with hierarchical Bayes or latent‑class models), can predict market shares of new products with reasonable accuracy, though some systematic biases are observed.[20] The authors analyse multiple datasets comparing CBC predictions to actual market outcomes and find that, while absolute share predictions can deviate, relative preferences and rank orders are often well captured; they also analyse market share bias under different estimation approaches.[20] This article meets inclusion criteria as a peer‑reviewed empirical evaluation of conjoint’s predictive performance and supports the use of conjoint as a defensible tool for forecasting relative positioning performance.[20]  

Sawtooth’s knowledge‑base article emphasises that conjoint is particularly appropriate when the research goal is to understand trade‑offs across multiple attributes with multiple levels, whereas MaxDiff is better suited to ranking items on a single attribute (for example, feature importance).[8] This is consistent with broader marketing‑science guidance and indicates that your agent should steer users toward conjoint when positioning territories differ in multiple dimensions (for example, target segment, primary benefit, feature bundle, price), and toward MaxDiff when the primary decision is which benefits or messages to emphasise within a given territory.[8][20]  

A defensible validation workflow would therefore include, for each serious positioning candidate, either a choice‑based conjoint study that embeds that position as a distinct profile (or set of profiles) against competitors, or at least a MaxDiff study to quantify the relative appeal of its core benefit claims. ##### 1.3.3 Willingness‑to‑pay elicitation: Van Westendorp and Gabor‑Granger  

Pricing is tightly coupled to positioning; a “premium” position at a budget price or a budget position at a premium price will be incoherent. Two widely used survey‑based methods for eliciting willingness to pay (WTP) are the Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter and the Gabor‑Granger method.  

The Van Westendorp method, developed by Peter Van Westendorp in the 1970s, asks respondents four questions: at what price is the product too cheap to be of good quality; at what price is it a bargain; at what price does it start to get expensive but still acceptable; and at what price is it too expensive to consider.[6] By aggregating responses and plotting cumulative distributions, researchers identify a range of acceptable prices and sometimes a point of “optimal price” where perceived cheapness and expensiveness curves intersect.[6] Sawtooth’s description of Van Westendorp emphasises that it is a price‑perception tool, not a demand forecasting tool; it assumes that consumers can reliably report their perceptions of fair and unacceptable prices, but it does not model purchase likelihood or quantity demanded.[6] They explicitly note that, without the Newton‑Miller‑Smith extension, Van Westendorp does not directly predict purchase behaviour or sales volume at different price points, and therefore must be combined with other analyses for sales forecasting.[6]  

The Gabor‑Granger method, by contrast, shows respondents a series of price points for a product and asks at each price whether they would buy the product.[7] If the respondent says yes at a given price, the next question uses a higher price; if they say no, the next uses a lower price.[7] The highest price at which the respondent says yes is treated as their maximum willingness to pay.[7] Aggregating across respondents yields an estimated demand curve: for each price point, the percentage of respondents willing to buy at that price can be computed, and by multiplying by an assumed customer base, projected revenue at each price can be estimated.[7] The optimal price is then the one with the highest projected revenue.[7]  

While both methods are widely used and easy to implement, they have documented limitations. Van Westendorp’s reliance on direct price‑perception questions means it is vulnerable to hypothetical bias, strategic responding, and anchoring on the ranges provided; it also assumes that respondents can meaningfully distinguish between “too cheap,” “bargain,” “expensive but acceptable,” and “too expensive,” which may not hold for all product categories.[6] Gabor‑Granger, while more behaviourally framed (“would you buy?”), still relies on hypothetical purchase intent and can be influenced by the starting price, step size, and the limited set of price points offered; its yes/no structure also compresses nuanced preferences into binary responses.[7] Neither method, on its own, has the predictive track record of conjoint‑based WTP estimation, where price is one of several attributes in choice tasks that more closely resemble real decisions.[8][20]  

For your agent, the defensible stance is to treat Van Westendorp and Gabor‑Granger as supporting tools for defining plausible price ranges and understanding price perceptions, not as primary evidence for positioning decisions.[6][7] A recommended practice would be to use Van Westendorp early to bound the feasible price range, then use Gabor‑Granger or, better, conjoint analysis with price as an attribute, to estimate demand and revenue implications across recommended positions, and to require that any premium positioning territory is supported by WTP evidence showing that a non‑trivial fraction of the target segment is willing to pay at the proposed price.<INFERENCE from="[Method descriptions and limitations in [6][7][8][20]]">An AI agent that presents Van Westendorp or Gabor‑Granger results as definitive would be misleading; instead, it should explicitly communicate their limitations and encourage triangulation with choice‑based methods.</INFERENCE>  

##### 1.3.4 Fake‑door and landing‑page experiments  

Survey‑based methods capture stated preferences and perceptions; behavioural experiments capture what customers actually do when exposed to positioning, messaging, and pricing in realistic settings. Fake‑door testing is a lean product‑development method that validates demand by presenting UI elements for features or offers that do not yet exist, such as buttons or menu items, and measuring click‑through rates and subsequent behaviour.[10] When users click the fake door, they are informed that the feature is not yet available and may be invited to join a waitlist; the click data indicates genuine interest.[10]

#### 1.4 Structured decision aids: failure modes and alternatives  

Your agent must decide whether to retain a weighted‑sum scoring model for evaluating positioning territories or adopt a more “defensible” decision aid. The empirical literature on multi‑criteria decision analysis (MCDA) identifies several failure modes in commonly used methods, especially when applied mechanistically.  

##### 1.4.1 Weighted‑sum models and their limitations  

The simplest MCDA method is the weighted‑sum model (WSM), where each option’s score is a weighted sum of its scores on each criterion: \(U(x) = \sum_i w_i s_i(x)\). This model is attractive because it is simple, transparent, and easy to implement; however, it rests on strong assumptions: linear, additive utility; independence of criteria; and meaningful, commensurate scales for the criterion scores.[11][18]  

Empirical and theoretical work shows several ways in which WSM can mislead. First, it is sensitive to normalisation: if criterion scores are rescaled (for example, from 0–10 to 0–100), the relative weights and contributions can change; care must be taken to normalise scores properly, often to a 0–1 range.[11][12] Second, when criteria are correlated or overlapping, additive aggregation can double‑count effects, inflating the importance of certain dimensions. Third, weight elicitation from decision‑makers is itself subject to biases: anchoring on round numbers, overemphasis on salient criteria, and instability over time. Behavioural decision research, though not explicitly cited in the provided sources, has repeatedly shown that stated preferences and weights are constructed on the fly rather than retrieved from stable utilities.<INFERENCE from="[General behavioural decision‑making literature and the formal properties discussed in [11][12][13][18]]">These issues suggest that WSM, if used uncritically, can produce rankings that are more reflective of modelling artefacts than of robust preferences or realities.</INFERENCE>  

O’Shea et al. (2026), in a ScienceDirect article on weight stability intervals for MCDA, discuss methods for assessing how changes in criterion weights affect rankings and propose rank‑reversal‑free methods such as SPOTIS (Stable Preference Ordering Towards Ideal Solution).[11] While the details are not fully visible in the snippet, the focus on weight stability and rank‑reversal avoidance underscores that naive weighting can produce fragile rankings.[11] This article meets inclusion criteria as a recent peer‑reviewed methodological contribution and suggests that weight‑sensitivity analysis is a necessary component of any defensible MCDA application.[11]  

Given these limitations, a weighted‑sum model in your agent should not be abandoned outright—its simplicity is an advantage—but must be augmented with features that make its assumptions and fragility visible. For example, the agent can compute weight stability intervals or, at least, plot how the ranking of territories changes as weights vary within plausible ranges, highlighting where the “winner” is robust or where minor weight changes flip the ranking.[11][18]  

##### 1.4.2 Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP): rank reversal, inconsistency, and critiques  

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) is a widely used MCDA method that decomposes a decision into a hierarchy of goals, criteria, and alternatives, and then uses pairwise comparisons to derive weights and scores.[12][13] AHP’s mathematical foundation involves eigenvector calculations and consistency indices; it has been praised for providing a structured approach to complex decisions.[13] An MDPI paper on AHP in quality decision‑making argues that AHP “guarantees quality because it has a powerful mathematical base” and is suitable for evaluating and selecting alternatives.[13] The authors also note, however, that as the size of the pairwise comparison matrix grows beyond three, inconsistency issues arise and increase exponentially as the number of criteria and alternatives grows; they highlight that if the consistency ratio (CR) exceeds 0.1, the user is considered inconsistent, placing blame on the user rather than the method.[13] This underscores that AHP can become unwieldy and prone to inconsistency when many criteria or options are involved.[13]  

Rank reversal is another well‑documented issue in AHP. Wang and colleagues, in a ScienceDirect article, propose an approach to avoiding rank reversal in AHP by normalising eigenvector weights of alternatives using their maximum rather than their sum.[12] They reference earlier work by Belton and Gear, who showed that adding or removing alternatives can change the ranking of existing alternatives due to the way AHP normalises weights.[12] Rank reversal undermines the intuitive expectation that the relative ranking of two options should not depend on the presence of irrelevant alternatives; its occurrence suggests that AHP’s outputs can be artefacts of representation and normalisation choices rather than stable reflections of preference.[12]  

Combined AHP‑PROMETHEE approaches have been proposed to mitigate some of these issues. For example, an MDPI article on portfolio performance ranking describes combining AHP for weight derivation with PROMETHEE, an outranking method, for ranking alternatives; the authors note that such combinations have been used in various fields.[14] However, these hybrid approaches add complexity and still lack extensive external validation in real‑world strategic decisions.[14]  

For your agent, AHP is likely overkill and potentially misleading for choosing among three to five positioning territories. Pairwise comparisons may impose unnecessary cognitive load on users, and rank‑reversal and inconsistency issues make it risky to present AHP rankings as definitive.[12][13][14] A simpler weighted‑sum model with explicit sensitivity analysis is more transparent and easier to explain, aligning better with the goal of defensibility.
