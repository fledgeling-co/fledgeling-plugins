## Detailed Findings

### 1. What does the empirical evidence say about producing a defensible product-positioning decision—which frameworks hold up, how candidates are validated before commitment, and where structured decision aids mislead?

#### 1.1 Decision specification for the rebuilt skill

**(High Confidence)** The skill should separate **territory generation**, **market validation** and **strategic selection**. Combining them in one weighted score makes speculative internal judgments commensurable with observed customer behaviour and allows strong scores on minor criteria to compensate for fatal failures such as no demand, no credibility or no feasible delivery.

| Number in live comparison | **Three finalists plus incumbent/status-quo control** | Limits multiple comparisons and gives every candidate adequate sample rather than conducting an underpowered tournament. |
| Primary generation axis 1 | **Category distance:** existing category → subcategory/niche → new category | Strong discrepancy can produce a subtype rather than differentiation inside the main category; category spanning has strongly heterogeneous effects. **(Medium Confidence)** A useful five-territory generation set is:

1. Existing-category, functional-benefit baseline.
2. Existing-category, economic or risk-reduction benefit.
3. Narrow subsegment or urgent-use-case position.
4. JTBD/outcome or user-identity position.
5. New-category hypothesis.

**(High Confidence)** Do not vary every component simultaneously in the first message test. Hold proof quality, offer scope, visual execution and price constant where possible; otherwise, a winning page cannot identify whether the result came from category framing, benefit, proof or price.

#### 1.2 Which positioning and category frameworks hold up?

##### Customer-perceived positioning effectiveness

**(High Confidence)** Fuchs and Diamantopoulos conducted two within- and between-subject experiments comparing four real-brand positioning strategies on favorability, differentiation and credibility. Benefit-based and surrogate/user positions generally outperformed feature-based positions, but no positioning type dominated every outcome. The studies were limited to a small set of strategies and primarily high-involvement automobile contexts. `<MISSING_DATA>Exact study sample sizes, coefficients and p-values were not exposed in the accessible publisher record; the full article or author dataset is required.</MISSING_DATA>` [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1108/03090561011079873) ([portal.fis.tum.de](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/evaluating-the-effectiveness-of-brand-positioning-strategies-from/))

**(High Confidence)** Their subsequent programme used seven studies to validate a holistic four-dimensional measure—dissimilarity, uniqueness, favorability and credibility—and reported better prediction of purchase intention or perceived superiority than common attribute-based approaches. `<MISSING_DATA>Study-level N, incremental R² and individual confidence intervals were unavailable in the accessible full record.</MISSING_DATA>` [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00892.x) ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00892.x))

**(Medium Confidence)** In a hierarchical Bayesian analysis of 19,682 respondents, 337 brands, 25 countries and eight product/service categories, perceived quality and uniqueness were, on average, more important to purchase intention than leadership or growing-popularity claims; their effects weakened when many competing brands were perceived to possess the same benefit. The outcome was stated purchase intention rather than observed purchase. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2016.05.002) ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167811616300519?utm_source=openai))

`<INFERENCE from="Fuchs and Diamantopoulos 2010/2012; van der Lans et al. 2016">The strongest generalisable territory template is: a legible frame of reference, a valued benefit or outcome, a perceptible difference from alternatives, and credible evidence that the company can deliver it.</INFERENCE>`

##### JTBD and outcome-driven innovation

**(Medium Confidence)** JTBD’s strongest defensible use is problem discovery: organising interviews around the circumstances, desired progress, alternatives and tradeoffs surrounding behaviour. It does not by itself establish segment size, relative preference, WTP or causal sales impact.

**(Low Confidence)** Strategyn’s retrospective track-record study contacted representatives of 43 client initiatives. Among the 21 that led to product launches, sponsors classified 18 as successful, yielding the promoted 86% success figure. Success could be defined using the sponsor’s chosen metric—revenue, market share, satisfaction or ROI—and no contemporaneous non-ODI control was used. [strategyn.com](https://strategyn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Innovation-Track-Record-Study-Strategyn-1.pdf) ([strategyn.com](https://strategyn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Innovation-Track-Record-Study-Strategyn-1.pdf?utm_source=openai))

`<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>[ODI proponents interpret 18/21 sponsor-rated successes as evidence of unusually high predictive validity. The absence of prospective registration, consistent outcome definitions, a matched comparison group, failed-launch denominator and independent auditing means the same observations are compatible with selection, survivorship and retrospective classification bias.]</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>`

**Verdict:** **Use JTBD/ODI to generate and articulate outcomes; never let an “opportunity score” qualify a territory without independent market tests.**

Its reports define the “category king” as the company that achieves category dominance as measured by market capitalisation, and then report that such selected leaders obtain 76% of the category’s market capitalisation. The underlying work screened approximately 702,000 firms, reduced 28,810 transactions to 2,694 usable transactions and used public market capitalisation, acquisition price or latest private valuation as interchangeable value measures. #### 1.3 How should positioning and messaging candidates be tested before commitment?

##### Recommended validation sequence

**(High Confidence)** The methods form a hierarchy; they are not interchangeable:

1. **Qualitative discovery:** identify situations, alternatives, language and possible outcomes.
2. **Randomised monadic message test:** test comprehension, relevance, favorability, uniqueness and credibility.
3. **MaxDiff/BWS:** rank claims, outcomes or proofs *inside a stable frame*.
4. **CBC/conjoint or discrete choice:** quantify tradeoffs among realistic offers with opt-out/status quo.
5. **WTP validation:** use real or incentive-compatible price commitments where possible.
6. **Randomised landing-page/fake-door experiment:** test qualified behavioural response.
7. **Paid pilot or staged launch:** test value delivery, retention and repeatability.

##### Message and concept testing

**(High Confidence)** Use a randomised monadic design for initial positioning territories. Comparative ratings in which every participant sees every territory are efficient but increase demand effects, contrast effects and preference construction. A final direct choice can follow the monadic evaluation.

**(High Confidence)** Predeclare one primary outcome, ideally a composite or ordered set containing:

- correct unaided category/use-case comprehension;
- customer-perceived favorability;
- uniqueness/dissimilarity;
- credibility;
- relative choice against the incumbent/status quo;
- a behavioural CTA where ethical and feasible.

**(Medium Confidence)** Product trial is not invariably necessary for early message discrimination. In a six-cell randomised toothpaste line-extension concept test, adding product trial did not interact with the tested positioning statements on the six main criteria. Transferability to unfamiliar, high-risk or technically complex products is low. `<MISSING_DATA>Accessible publisher text did not expose N or test statistics.</MISSING_DATA>` [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0737678296000975) ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0737678296000975?utm_source=openai))

##### Sample-size and significance defaults

**(High Confidence)** Sample size must be determined by the **smallest effect that would change the decision**, not by a generic “100 responses is enough” rule. For a two-arm comparison of means with two-sided \(\alpha=.05\) and 80% power:

- detecting \(d=.40\) requires approximately **100 respondents per arm**;
- detecting \(d=.30\) requires approximately **175 per arm**.

`<INFERENCE from="standard normal-approximation power formula, alpha .05, power .80">These values are calculated design examples, not empirical universal thresholds.</INFERENCE>`

**(High Confidence)** For three finalists plus a control, this implies approximately **400 respondents** to detect effects around \(d=.40\), or approximately **700 respondents** to detect effects around \(d=.30\), before allowance for attrition, multiple comparisons or segment analysis. Use Dunnett-style control comparisons or another predeclared multiplicity correction.

**(High Confidence)** “Statistically significant” is not sufficient. Define a smallest economically or strategically worthwhile effect before collection. A territory should pass only when the confidence interval supports that practical threshold—not merely when \(p<.05\).

##### MaxDiff/best–worst scaling

**(High Confidence)** MaxDiff is appropriate for ranking messages, outcomes, objections or proof points that respondents understand on a common basis. It is inappropriate as the sole category-level decision because its scores are relative: it can identify the least-bad item even when none is attractive, and ordinary designs do not measure absolute demand.

**(Medium Confidence)** A systematic review of 165 health-object BWS studies found samples ranging from 15 to 9,289. In the 2017–2021 subset, 65.6% provided no sample-size justification; only 5.3% used a formal calculation. This demonstrates that common practice is not a defensible sizing standard. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9363399/) ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9363399/?utm_source=openai))

**(Medium Confidence)** BWS has often produced more reliable and predictively valid rankings than rating scales in semantic-measurement comparisons, but that does not establish purchase or market validity. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29322399/) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29322399/?utm_source=openai))

**Recommended default:** `<INFERENCE from="BWS reviews and technical simulation evidence">Use design simulation whenever possible; otherwise target at least 250 qualified respondents per decision-relevant segment for aggregate claim ranking, and do not report unstable small subgroups.</INFERENCE>` Include an absolute acceptance item or anchored “none is compelling” option after the MaxDiff exercise.

##### Conjoint and choice-based methods

**(High Confidence)** CBC is most defensible when territories can be represented as realistic, mutually comparable offers and when respondents face the actual competitive set, price and a status-quo/none option. It is weaker when entirely different category narratives are decomposed into artificial attributes that buyers would never encounter independently.

**(Medium Confidence)** The Johnson–Orme minimum heuristic is:

\[
n \ge \frac{500c}{ta},
\]

where \(t\) is tasks per respondent, \(a\) alternatives per task and \(c\) the largest number of levels for an attribute—or the largest product of levels for any two attributes when interactions are central. **(High Confidence)** A defensible conjoint study should also include:

- realistic price levels;
- an opt-out/status-quo choice;
- one or more holdout tasks;
- attribute-level balance and limited overlap;
- tests of scale heterogeneity and respondent quality;
- segment-specific precision checks;
- no interpretation of survey-derived WTP as equivalent to market WTP.

##### Van Westendorp, Gabor–Granger and willingness to pay

**(High Confidence)** Van Westendorp estimates perceptions of “too cheap,” “cheap,” “expensive” and “too expensive.” Its crossing points are not purchase probabilities, demand curves or profit optima. `<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>Independent criterion-validity evidence showing that standard Van Westendorp crossing points predict realised purchase prices was not located.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>`

**(High Confidence)** Gabor–Granger can estimate a stated purchase-intent curve across specified prices, but it remains vulnerable to hypothetical bias, starting-price anchoring, sequential consistency pressure and omission of competitors. Randomising price order or using a between-subject price design reduces—but does not eliminate—these problems.

**(Medium Confidence)** In one Gabor–Granger modelling study, a latent-class spline achieved holdout correlation \(r=.636\), compared with \(r=.603\) for a linear price model; the difference was significant, standard error of the difference .005, \(p<.001\), but the predictive gain was only about 5%. Store-share validation supported external validity but produced only modest gains over alternatives. [sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167487098000063) ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167487098000063?utm_source=openai))

**(High Confidence)** Schmidt and Bijmolt’s meta-analysis included 77 studies reported in 47 papers, producing 115 effect sizes; hypothetical and real WTP samples contained 24,347 and 20,656 observations respectively. Hypothetical WTP exceeded real WTP by 21% on average. Contrary to conventional wisdom, indirect methods produced about 10 percentage points more hypothetical bias than direct methods in the full model. Predicted overstatement for specialty goods was 28% for direct methods and 40% for indirect methods. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6?utm_source=openai))

**Verdict:** Van Westendorp may define an exploratory reference-price range; Gabor–Granger may compare price points if order is randomised; neither should be a commitment gate. Use deposits, refundable preorders, paid pilots, randomised market prices, BDM or incentive-aligned choice when the decision is consequential.

##### Fake-door and landing-page tests

**(High Confidence)** Fake doors improve on pure surveys because clicking, signing up or attempting a transaction is observed behaviour. They still measure response to an ad, page and CTA—not realised product value, retention, expansion, implementation friction or willingness to pay after use.

**(High Confidence)** A click is a weak commitment. The preferred endpoint is the highest ethical friction available: qualified application, scheduled call, procurement information, card-authorised preorder, refundable deposit or paid pilot. The post-click page should immediately disclose product availability and avoid deceptive scarcity or false claims.

**(High Confidence)** Randomisation is essential when comparing territories. Separate campaigns launched at different times or on different channels confound message with audience mix, auction conditions and seasonality.

##### Methodological Comparison: validation methods

| Method | Valid use | Minimum defensibility condition | Known failure mode | Commitment status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qualitative JTBD interviews | Discover situations, language, alternatives and outcomes | Purposeful sample, disconfirming cases, transparent coding and saturation assessment | Interviewer reconstruction; memorable-event bias; no prevalence estimate | **Discovery only** |

#### 1.4 Where weighted scoring and MCDA mislead

##### Weighted-sum models

**(High Confidence)** A weighted sum is valid only when:

1. criteria scores are on defensible interval value scales;
2. weights represent tradeoffs over the actual best-to-worst ranges, not abstract “importance”;
3. additive preferential independence is plausible;
4. no noncompensatory veto is being hidden;
5. uncertainty and correlation among criteria are modelled or stress-tested.

**(High Confidence)** Normalising each criterion against the current candidate set can make a candidate’s score depend on which irrelevant alternatives happen to be present. Divisive normalisations used in weighted sums can produce rank reversal under some decision matrices. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1002/mcda.1806) ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mcda.1806?utm_source=openai))

**(High Confidence)** The UK government’s MCDA guidance explicitly warns that weights should reflect the value of the swing between the worst and best observed consequences, rather than criterion “importance” in isolation. [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-multi-criteria-decision-analysis/use-of-multi-criteria-decision-analysis-in-options-appraisal-of-economic-cases) ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-book-supplementary-guidance-multi-criteria-decision-analysis/use-of-multi-criteria-decision-analysis-in-options-appraisal-of-economic-cases?utm_source=openai))

##### AHP

**(High Confidence)** AHP can reverse the relative order of existing options after an alternative is added or removed because its distributive mode renormalises local priorities against the available set. Proposed ideal, multiplicative and rank-preserving variants reduce particular reversals but do not erase the broader dependence on model structure and elicitation. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dss.2005.12.002) ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016792360500179X?utm_source=openai))

**(High Confidence)** The 1–9 semantic ratio scale should not be interpreted as a psychologically exact measurement of value ratios. Pairwise comparisons can expose inconsistency, but a low consistency ratio does not establish that the judgments are unbiased, that the hierarchy is correct or that the resulting strategy predicts market success.

##### Weight-elicitation and preference-construction bias

**(High Confidence)** Jacobi and Hobbs elicited utility-company employee weights using hierarchical and nonhierarchical value trees and found evidence consistent with criterion-splitting and anchor-and-adjust bias; the same substantive objectives received different effective weights when represented differently. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1287/deca.1070.0100) ([pubsonline.informs.org](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/deca.1070.0100?utm_source=openai))

**(Medium Confidence)** Experimental comparison of AHP, best-worst method, SMART, swing weighting and point allocation found equalising bias across methods; AHP and BWM displayed less equalising bias than SMART, swing and point allocation, and hierarchical structuring reduced the measured bias. `<MISSING_DATA>The accessible record did not expose the experiment’s exact N or all effect coefficients.</MISSING_DATA>` [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2262) ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bdm.2262?utm_source=openai))

**(Medium Confidence)** In an apartment-choice experiment involving nine attributes, models using five elicited weighting methods exceeded an equal-weights model, and rank-order-centroid weights were modestly superior. Test–retest reliability of overall evaluation was nevertheless only modest. A later empirical evaluation using real-world data found rank-order-centroid performance relatively poor, contradicting its strong simulation record. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1995.1066) ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597885710667?utm_source=openai)) [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1287/DECA.2022.0456) ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1287/DECA.2022.0456?utm_source=openai))

`<CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>[Some experiments find that structured elicited weights outperform equal weights and that particular techniques reduce specific biases. Other work finds poor real-data performance, rank reversal and strong sensitivity to representation. There is no empirically dominant weighting method for one-off founder positioning choices.]</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>`

##### What performs better for three to five strategic options?

**(High Confidence)** No alternative has demonstrated universally superior out-of-sample positioning decisions because strategy choices are rarely repeated with observable counterfactual outcomes. `<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>No controlled evidence establishes that AHP, BWM, ELECTRE, PROMETHEE, SMAA or minimax regret consistently selects more successful product positions than all competing methods.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>`

**(High Confidence)** The more defensible architecture is therefore **set-valued and robustness-oriented**, not a replacement point score:

1. **Hard gates/vetoes:** eliminate options that fail minimum demand, credibility, deliverability, compliance or economics.
2. **Natural-unit consequence table:** retain conversion, CAC, sales-cycle length, gross margin, evidence quality and other consequences in their original units.
3. **Dominance screening:** eliminate options that are no better on any relevant consequence and worse on at least one.
4. **Ordinal or interval tradeoffs:** elicit only ranges or rankings that the user can defend.
5. **Stochastic/imprecise-weight analysis:** report how often each option ranks first across plausible weights and data uncertainty.
6. **Scenario regret:** show the loss if assumptions about category adoption, sales cycle, WTP or reachability are wrong.
7. **Pairwise “even swaps”:** ask which explicit improvement on one consequence would compensate for a loss on another.
8. **No-decision output:** if results overlap, specify the experiment with the highest expected decision value.

`<INFERENCE from="rank reversal evidence, weight-bias experiments and government swing-weight guidance">For three to five options, this architecture is more defensible than replacing weighted sums with a more elaborate point-ranking formula, because it exposes rather than conceals dependence on contested assumptions.</INFERENCE>`

#### 1.5 How should beachhead or segment prioritisation be validated?

**(High Confidence)** `<MISSING_DATA>No prospective study was located that validates a commonly used startup “beachhead score” against later segment-level revenue, retention or survival while controlling for company quality and execution.</MISSING_DATA>`

**(Medium Confidence)** Adjacent new-product research supports using competitive product advantage, market orientation, upfront homework, strategic fit, technical feasibility and risk/reward in project screening. Stage-Gate scorecards typically include six to ten such factors. Much of this evidence is correlational benchmarking and retrospective success/failure classification rather than prospective scoring validation. [pmi.org](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/winning-new-product-development-projects-8049) ([pmi.org](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/winning-new-product-development-projects-8049?utm_source=openai))

**(High Confidence)** Rigid gate controls can inhibit learning in novel projects by increasing inflexibility, particularly under technological or market turbulence. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.72.1.118) ([journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.72.1.118?utm_source=openai)) Product- and market-testing gates were judged the most important gates by experienced UK and Dutch NPD managers, although this was a managerial assessment rather than a randomised comparison. **(High Confidence)** Beachhead scoring goes wrong when:

- broad TAM receives a precise high score despite weak reachability evidence;
- “pain” is inferred from interview enthusiasm rather than costly incumbent behaviour;
- WTP is based on stated prices rather than commitments;
- access, buying authority and sales-cycle length are guessed;
- the same facts contribute to correlated criteria such as pain, urgency, WTP and value;
- large numbers of minor advantages compensate for a must-have failure;
- evidence confidence is folded into the score invisibly;
- segment-specific product or onboarding costs are omitted;
- evaluators see the candidate identity before fixing criteria and thresholds.

**(High Confidence)** The skill should replace a single segment-attractiveness score with an **evidence ledger**:

| Segment consequence | Preferred evidence |
|---|---|
| Reachable account volume | Named-account count or reproducible query, not top-down TAM |
| Urgency | Trigger events, current spend, workaround cost, missed-revenue or risk records |
| Access/CAC | Observed contact-to-meeting and meeting-to-opportunity rates |
| Buying authority | Identified decision maker, budget owner and procurement route |
| WTP/ACV | Paid pilot, deposit, signed LOI with price and timing, or randomised price test |
| Time to value | Measured pilot implementation and outcome timing |
| Delivery burden | Integration hours, service load, support cost and exceptions |
| Retention/expansion | Cohort usage, renewal or credible leading indicators |
| Referenceability | Customer permission and adjacency to other target accounts |
| Evidence quality | Source provenance, sample, date, uncertainty and whether behaviour was observed |

#### 1.6 What distinguishes positioning that survives market contact?

**(High Confidence)** Strong causal case evidence is scarce because company narratives are selected after success, positioning changes coincide with product, price and channel changes, and failed efforts are underreported. `<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>Post-hoc corporate cases cannot isolate the causal effect of positioning from execution, product improvement, funding, timing or distribution.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>`

**(Medium Confidence)** The recurring properties of positions that survive are:

1. **Legibility:** buyers can place the offer in a useful comparison set.
2. **Moderate distinctiveness:** the position is meaningfully different without requiring excessive cognitive or market education.
3. **Benefit orientation:** it foregrounds a valued functional, economic, risk or identity outcome rather than an undifferentiated feature.
4. **Credibility:** proof, capabilities and delivery experience support the claim.
5. **Behavioural validation:** customers take a costly or inconvenient action.
6. **Economic viability:** price acceptance, CAC, sales cycle and delivery burden fit the business.
7. **Iteration:** the company treats positioning as a revisable market hypothesis.

`<INFERENCE from="Sujan and Bettman 1989; Fuchs and Diamantopoulos 2010/2012; van der Lans et al. 2016; Golder and Tellis 1993; WTP and intention-behaviour meta-analyses">Positions fail when novelty is mistaken for value, survey preference is mistaken for demand, credibility is compensable in a score, or category education costs are omitted.</INFERENCE>`

#### 1.7 Pre-commitment evidence thresholds

**(High Confidence)** The skill should use the following decision states.

| Gate | Evidence required | Pass condition | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem/segment reality | Interviews plus independent behavioural, transactional or operational evidence | The job/problem and current alternative are observed, not merely endorsed | Return to discovery |
| 2. Perception validity | Powered randomised monadic test | Primary positioning outcome exceeds control by the predeclared worthwhile margin; no credibility/comprehension veto fails | Rewrite or discard |
| 3. Relative choice | Holdout direct choice or CBC with status quo/none | Candidate is superior or credibly non-inferior to incumbent on the defined decision outcome | Do not advance |
| 4. Price/economics | Real or incentive-compatible price evidence | Lower uncertainty bound supports break-even unit economics | Reprice, redesign or reject |
| 5. Behavioural demand | Randomised fake-door/landing-page or sales outreach test | Lower confidence bound for qualified conversion exceeds the predeclared viable rate | No commitment |
| 6. Delivery | Paid pilot or staged release | Time-to-value, usage and delivery burden meet predefined thresholds | Narrow promise or product |
| 7. Robustness | Weight/scenario sensitivity and evidence audit | Winner does not depend on an implausibly narrow assumption set; no dominated option selected | Run value-of-information test |
| 8. Replication | Holdout audience, channel, period or second cohort | Direction and economically material magnitude reproduce | Mark provisional, not recommended |

**(High Confidence)** A territory can be labelled:

- **Recommended:** all gates pass, including at least one revealed-behaviour test and one independent replication or holdout.
- **Conditionally recommended:** perception and choice pass, but paid/behavioural or delivery evidence remains incomplete.
- **Promising hypothesis:** qualitative and survey evidence only.
- **Not recommended:** fails a veto, loses to control, fails economics or is highly assumption-sensitive.
- **No decision:** evidence is underpowered or candidates are practically tied.

### 2. What is the current state, and what is the strongest supporting evidence for it?

**(High Confidence)** The current state is that no named positioning framework has independently demonstrated a repeatable, causal ability to choose successful market positions. The strongest evidence instead supports a **methodological stack**:

1. consumer-based measurement of favorability, uniqueness/dissimilarity and credibility;
2. benefit/outcome rather than feature-only framing;
3. category legibility with context-dependent distinctiveness;
4. realistic choice and price tests;
5. observed behavioural commitments;
6. staged market learning;
7. robustness analysis rather than a single deterministic score.

**(High Confidence)** The strongest evidence classes are:

- **Meta-analysis:** category spanning has a near-zero average effect with extreme contextual heterogeneity; hypothetical WTP overstates real WTP materially. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.70079) ([sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smj.70079?utm_source=openai)) [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6?utm_source=openai))
- **Randomised experimental synthesis:** even substantial changes in intention translate into smaller behavioural changes. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/) ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536643/?utm_source=openai))
- **Historical market evidence:** pioneers often fail and later early leaders frequently capture more durable share. [people.duke.edu](https://people.duke.edu/~moorman/Marketing-Strategy-Seminar-2015/Session%205/Golder%20and%20Tellis.pdf) ([people.duke.edu](https://people.duke.edu/~moorman/Marketing-Strategy-Seminar-2015/Session%205/Golder%20and%20Tellis.pdf?utm_source=openai))
- **Multi-study construct validation:** customer-perceived positioning effectiveness is multidimensional and predicts intentions better than attribute-only approaches. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00892.x) ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/j.1540-5885.2011.00892.x))

**(Medium Confidence)** `<INFERENCE from="the evidence hierarchy above">The scientifically defensible product is therefore not an automated “positioning oracle.” It is an evidence-producing workflow that can decline to recommend, identifies the assumption separating the finalists and prescribes the next discriminating experiment.</INFERENCE>`

### 4. What changed recently, and what is the trajectory?

**(High Confidence)** The most material recent change is the 2026 category-spanning meta-analysis. Earlier literature was often summarised as a general “categorical imperative” or spanning penalty; the synthesis shows an average effect effectively equal to zero and directs attention to boundedness, audience, product-versus-producer level and simultaneous-versus-sequential spanning. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.70079) ([sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smj.70079?utm_source=openai))

**(High Confidence)** The 2020 WTP meta-analysis overturned a common methodological presumption: indirect hypothetical methods were not more accurate on average and displayed greater bias after controlling for other factors. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6) ([link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6?utm_source=openai))

**(Medium Confidence)** Recent MCDA work has shifted from proposing yet another exact ranking formula toward documenting elicitation bias, imprecise preferences, normalisation sensitivity and robustness. Experimental work published in 2022 found measurable equalising bias across all five tested weighting methods, despite relative differences among them. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.2262) ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bdm.2262?utm_source=openai))

**(High Confidence)** Online experimentation research increasingly stresses power, minimum detectable effects, repeated-user interference, peeking, novelty effects and multiple metrics. Large traffic does not rescue an experiment whose unit of randomisation, primary metric or economic threshold was selected after results were observed. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2023.2257237) ([tandfonline.com](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/00031305.2023.2257237?utm_source=openai))

`<INFERENCE from="the 2020–2026 methodological evidence">The trajectory is away from framework-authority and precise internal scores, and toward triangulation: qualitative discovery, preference experiments, costly behavioural signals, uncertainty-aware decision support and continued post-launch learning.</INFERENCE>`

## Recommended Next Steps

1. **Prospectively benchmark the rebuilt skill against historical and live decisions.**  
   **Rationale:** Assemble past positioning decisions with contemporaneous evidence—not retrospective stories—and test whether the skill’s recommended, conditional and rejected territories predict later conversion, revenue, retention and implementation burden. This is the most direct route to validating the agent itself.

2. **Run simulation-based design work for five territories, three finalists and one control.**  
   **Rationale:** Simulate expected effect sizes, B2B respondent scarcity, segment heterogeneity, MaxDiff/CBC designs and landing-page conversions. Produce sample-size and cost ranges rather than hard-coding generic \(N\) thresholds.

3. **Prototype an evidence-gated robustness aid alongside the existing weighted scorer.**  
   **Rationale:** Compare outputs from the current weighted sum with hard gates, dominance, interval swing weights, rank acceptability and scenario regret. Log every rank reversal and identify which criteria, normalisations or weights caused it.

4. **Develop a prospective category-frame experiment.**  
   **Rationale:** For the same product and audience, randomise existing-category, subcategory and new-category frames while holding benefit, proof, design and price constant. Measure unaided comprehension, qualified CTA, acquisition cost and follow-through. This directly tests the highest-risk strategic axis.

5. **Create a preregistered evidence standard for “recommended.”**  
   **Rationale:** Before running research, specify the primary outcome, economically meaningful effect, veto thresholds, power, multiple-comparison method, break-even conversion, replication condition and robustness test. This prevents the agent or user from redefining success after seeing results.
