Dossier · Field report · August 2026 · Luke Rhodes

It buys attention.It does not buy understanding.

Scrollytelling's central promise is the one thing the research does not support. Across independent studies with different populations and different material, the format reliably improves engagement, perceived clarity and cognitive load, and reliably fails to improve measured comprehension. That is not a reason to stop building these pages. It is a reason to stop selling them as comprehension aids and start treating them as attention budgets.

Five deep-research backends were run on one brief about how newsroom graphics desks actually build evidence-dense scroll reports. All five were read end to end before a single line of this page was designed. Where they contradicted each other — and they did, four times, including once on a number one of them invented — the contradiction is on the page rather than averaged away.

01The reader you actually have

Design for the fiftieth percentile, not the finisher

Every structural rule below falls out of one distribution. Chartbeat instrumented roughly two billion pageviews and then looked at what happened on a single publisher's articles. The shape is unkind and it has not moved.

State 01

161 people arrive. That is the only number in this figure that is entirely yours. Everything after it is attrition you can shape but not stop.

State 02

61 of them leave immediately — 38%, before anything you wrote has a chance to work.

State 03

That leaves 100 engaged readers. About five of them never scroll a single pixel; web-wide the figure is closer to one in ten.

State 04

The median engaged reader stops at about half the page — roughly 50% on Slate, roughly 60% across the web.

You are at the gauge reading in the left margin.
State 05

Only a quarter get past the 1,600th pixel of a 2,000-pixel article — about twenty-five people, or one in six of the original 161.

Takeaway

A structure that withholds the finding until state twelve is built for readers who never arrive. Lead with the conclusion; use the scroll to prove it, not to delay it. This is why the four figures that matter on this page are printed above the fold.

The structure that survives that distribution

Segel and Heer derived three narrative structures from 58 published narrative visualisations, arranged on an author-driven to reader-driven axis. The martini glass — a narrow authored stem, then an opening into exploration — is the correct default for an evidence page, because it puts the argument where the median reader still is and the data where the persistent reader can find it.

One caution about the bowl of the glass. Three field experiments found that adding an introductory story to an exploratory visualisation did not increase subsequent exploration; in two follow-ups only 40.9% and 41.5% of qualifying sessions traversed every narrative section in order. Exploration is something you offer, not something narrative causes.

Sections, not one long pin

The newsroom vocabulary is more granular than the research taxonomy: analysing 50 published scrollers, Oesch, Renner and Roth grouped the techniques into graphic sequences, animated transitions, panning and zooming, scrolling through movies, and show-and-autoplay. Their motive was cost — these pages remain expensive to build, and a shared vocabulary is what makes them repeatable.

What both literatures agree on operationally is the rhythm: ordinary document flow, then a bounded visual episode, then back to document flow. The ONS ships exactly this and pre-renders the HTML so the article survives without JavaScript and stays indexable. Pinning the entire page instead sacrifices orientation, accessibility and mobile resilience at once.

One claim per scroll state — and what that is not

Operationally: one claim id per state, a caption that summarises it in a sentence, a visual delta that changes only the encodings supporting it, object identity preserved across states, and reverse scrolling that restores the previous state. If a caption contains two independent propositions, split the state.

It is a production discipline, not a validated law. Three of the five backends flagged that explicitly, and no controlled study tests the phrase. Keep the rule; do not cite a study for it. On state counts, words per step and pin duration the panel returned nothing usable at all — topic complexity, device height and chart literacy confound every number anyone has proposed.

What is measured is parallelism. Across 42 professional narrative visualisations, sequences that repeat structural relationships between adjacent states showed memory and preference benefits. Keep scales, positions and objects stable unless changing one of them is itself the evidence — which is why the figure above never rescales.

02The motion budget

Animation is admissible, or it is decoration

The animation literature looks contradictory until you notice that the two sides are measuring different things. It resolves cleanly, and the resolution is a build rule rather than a compromise.

Where it measurably helped

  • Object trackingAnimated transitions reduced tracking error across every tested transition type, at p<0.001, with 24 participants. Object-tracking animations ran 1.25s, value changes 2s.
  • Value estimationImproved for some transitions — but only with a common scale. Axis rescaling increased both errors and "unknown" responses.
  • Perceived engagementAnimated transitions beat static visuals on engagement at p<.001 with 240 participants — while average comprehension sat at about 4 out of 5 with no meaningful difference between conditions.

Where it measurably hurt

  • LearningApparent advantages over static graphics turned out to be confounds: the animated conditions carried more information. After a week, participants who studied text improved while those who studied animation declined.
  • AnalysisAnimated trends were the least effective condition for analysis; static traces were faster and small multiples more accurate, though animation was preferred for presentation.
  • More stagingExtreme staging was worse than direct animation for donut value changes (p=0.024); stacked bars showed no effect at all (p=0.224).
  • SuspenseContrasting narratives raised surprise and interest — and raised recall error, without shifting attitudes.

This motion lets the reader perceive ______ that would otherwise require a difficult mental comparison.If the blank is energy, delight or immersion, it is decoration

That sentence is the entire test. It is an inference the panel assembled from three separate findings rather than a published rule, and it is labelled as one here — but it is the cleanest operational form of "animate between two states of one encoding; never animate as a substitute for a static explanation."

Three tiers, not one library

Browsers scroll on a separate process and deliver scroll events asynchronously, which is why scroll-synced animation was historically so hard. CSS scroll and view timelines integrate with the animation system and can run off the main thread; a GSAP timeline cannot. That mechanism is the only part of this argument with a primary source behind it.

GSAP's own documentation converts almost directly into lint rules: never animate the pinned element itself, because it invalidates the measurements; an ancestor with transform or will-change breaks position:fixed and that is a browser behaviour rather than a bug; under scrub, child durations are proportions of scroll distance, so you slow an animation by extending the end, never by lengthening durations. And one prohibition worth stating on its own: normalizeScroll() forces scrolling onto the JavaScript thread, which is scrolljacking with a nicer name.

This page No GSAP was loaded

Every effect here is either a discrete state change or a scroll-progress transform. Both tiers above GSAP cover that, and the one continuously updating element — the depth gauge — runs on a CSS scroll timeline where the browser has one. Loading a main-thread motion library to animate things the platform already animates would contradict the table directly above it. If a later page in this series needs genuine scrub, GSAP is the right answer for that page.

States render from their id, or they do not exist

Readers skip across states faster than animations complete. The Pudding's post-mortems name this directly, alongside two other constraints that cost real production time: size steps in pixels computed from window.innerHeight rather than vh, because mobile browser bars change viewport height mid-scroll; and never use hover-only content, because stray taps during scrolling fire accidental triggers and touch has no hover at all. Newsday documented text being clipped by exactly the viewport-height problem.

The rule that follows: every state must render completely from its id, never from a prior animation having finished. No claim may exist only inside an animated intermediate frame.

03Provenance

Nobody is going to check your sources

This is the finding that should change how the citation layer is designed, and it is usually read backwards.

The temptation is to conclude that citations do not matter. The correct conclusion is narrower and more useful: the citation layer is not built to be clicked. It is built so the claim-to-source bond is inspectable, and so the page cannot quietly overclaim. Those are different design targets and they produce different interfaces.

What the trust evidence actually supports is thin but consistent. A single transparency feature had almost no effect on perceived credibility; a bundle of them — inline footnotes, author information, story type, method disclosure, published standards — produced small but statistically significant improvements on four of fifteen measures with 1,183 participants, and the design cannot say which indicator did the work. Source attribution shifts perceived credibility and interacts with prior trust in the named institution, which means a prestigious citation is a credibility cue and not proof that your claim represents it.

If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it.Archie Tse, New York Times graphics, Malofiej 24

Tse's rule caps the whole design. Any architecture where a source exists only inside a hover state has, by that rule, no sources. So the layer is deliberately redundant, in three parts, all three required:

Every marker on this page opens on hover, on keyboard focus and on tap; Escape closes it and returns focus to the marker. Each source in the registry at the foot lists every claim that leans on it, so the bond reads in both directions. The one number this design cannot borrow: Wikipedia's Page Previews produced a 31% rise in overall page interactions, but that measured article previews, not citation previews, and transferring it here would be exactly the kind of overreach this section is about.

Underneath the interface sits the part that actually prevents overclaiming: a claim graph compiled before any design happens. Each claim carries its id, exact text, confidence, whether it is direct or inferred, its source ids, the specific passage that supports it, and its scope. The build fails if a quantitative or attributed claim has no source, if a cited source supports only a nearby proposition rather than the claim itself, or if something assembled by reasoning is rendered as an empirical finding — the three moves narrative design makes at the data, visual, annotation and interaction layers when nobody is checking. Practitioners already treat data-source identification as non-negotiable; what they do less often is bind the source to the clause.

04Non-negotiable

The floors you do not get to set

Two of the constraints on this page are externally fixed. Neither is a matter of taste, and one of them is law in four jurisdictions.

Underneath those, two numbers worth holding: the frame budget is 16.7ms at 60fps, of which roughly 10ms remains after the browser's own rendering work, and any main-thread task over 50ms is formally a long task. Self-contained does not mean one enormous file — it means one complete semantic document. The Washington Post's eclipse scroller carried more than 30MB of images before it adopted lazy loading and sub-10KB thumbnails.

The accessibility floor is legal, and it is asymmetric

2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide is Level A — mandatory under ADA Title II and III, Section 508, EN 301 549 and the European Accessibility Act — and it covers any auto-starting motion running over five seconds in parallel with other content. 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions is AAA and explicitly covers scroll parallax; W3C's own technique notes that element movement beyond the essential movement of scrolling can trigger vestibular reactions.

The architectural choice that follows is reduced-first, and the reason is failure direction. Build the static composition as the baseline and add motion only inside prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference; a browser that does not understand the query ignores the block and keeps the static page. Motion-first fails open. And the reduced branch is a narrative mode in its own right, not every duration set to zero afterwards: parallax becomes fixed positioning, camera travel becomes cuts or annotated stills, scrubbed morphs become discrete states. Every fact survives.

This page What reduced motion actually changes

The depth gauge stops travelling and becomes a printed scale with a numeric readout. Step highlighting switches instantly instead of crossfading. Nothing else on the page moves in the first place, so nothing else changes — and no number, chart or claim is only visible in a moving state. There is also a persistent, always-visible motion toggle in the gauge rail, because a control that appears on hover is not a control.

05Editorial tension

Where the panel disagreed with itself

Five backends were given one brief. They did not come back with one answer. Averaging them would have produced a smoother page and a less honest one, so the four live disagreements are printed here instead — including the one where a member asserted a number that does not appear to exist.

1. A prevalence figure with no epidemiology behind it

One member asserted That roughly 35% of adults over 40 experience some form of vestibular disorder, presenting it as the reason prefers-reduced-motion matters — sourced to a design blog.
Another member flagged That the same figure circulates widely through accessibility guides without a traceable epidemiological citation, and should not be treated as load-bearing.

Carried The mechanism, which is well sourced: W3C states that element movement beyond the essential movement of scrolling can trigger vestibular reactions. Dropped The number. It does not appear on this page as evidence, and the accessibility rules above stand without it. The same member also described scrollytelling as "quantitatively proven to increase reader engagement and data retention" — a claim the three studies in section 02 directly contradict.

2. Does GSAP ScrollTrigger actually hurt INP?

Position A Main-thread animation competes with other JavaScript; moving simple timelines to CSS scroll timelines improves INP measurably.
Position B Animating only transform and opacity, respecting reduced motion and keeping active triggers under roughly thirty produces no measurable harm and can improve INP.

Status Every specific claim on both sides traces to vendor-adjacent or commercial blogs. The widely repeated "≤30 active triggers" figure has no traceable empirical basis, and no published benchmark compares INP across the three tiers on mid-tier mobile hardware. Carried Only the mechanism, which is primary-sourced: CSS scroll timelines run off the main thread, GSAP timelines do not. Do not tune against a number that does not exist.

3. Scroll, or a stepper?

For the stepper A 180-participant comparison found slideshow layouts produced better comprehension, and linking prose to the marks that support it improved engagement and recall. An 85-participant study found clicking easier and more intuitive than its scrollytelling condition.
For the scroll A 240-participant study found no significant engagement difference between stepper and scroller. Scrolling is nearly automatic where a stepper demands aiming and conscious effort, and every scroll study to date is desktop-only.

Unresolved, and stated as such The 85-participant study's authors noted their slow fade-out/fade-in implementation differed from normal newsroom scrolling, and their knowledge checks were near ceiling and could not distinguish comprehension at all. Scroll is a delivery mechanism here, not a conviction.

4. Does the format help people understand?

The optimistic reading With 454 participants, scrollytelling beat plain text on cognitive load, engagement, willingness to adopt and perceived clarity — and the same visualisation shown without narrative scaffolding performed slightly worse.
The measured reading In the same study, comprehension accuracy and confidence matched every other format, and differences in perceived understanding, transparency and trust were small and statistically inconclusive.

The pattern repeats Immersive variants of four data stories were rated more interesting and more persuasive, and no more understandable or trustworthy. A format that raises persuasiveness without raising understanding is rhetoric charged to the reader's battery. Engagement is not comprehension, and this page does not claim otherwise about itself.

06Failure modes

Four ways these pages become untrustworthy

Scrolljacking, which is not the same thing as scrollytelling

The line matters for a build spec: scrollytelling monitors scroll and changes content; scrolljacking manipulates the scroll mechanics. The first is the pattern, the second is the anti-pattern, and they are routinely conflated.

NN/g's usability testing found the majority of participants experienced at least mild disorientation, and on a fully scrolljacked page every participant was disoriented — some read it as a broken page, re-scrolled repeatedly, switched to dragging the scrollbar, refreshed, or left. Short scrolljacks preserved orientation but created an illusion of completeness: readers assumed the page had ended. Mobile amplified every problem. A 2026 controlled study with 20 participants found no speed benefit and significantly lower accuracy and satisfaction.

The mechanics were argued a decade earlier and reach the same verdict from the other direction: snapping requires inferring swipes from wheel events, which buys hypersensitivity, dropped gestures, a lost scrollbar, lost keyboard control and no rubber-banding. Keep wheel, touch, momentum, scrollbar, keyboard and history behaviour native. All of it.

Mobile, which costs two or three redesigns

The most quoted engineering observation in the corpus is also the least glamorous: getting a graphic to work on all platforms is expensive, and you should expect to redesign it two or three times to work on both desktop and mobile. That is the documented cost, not a failure. Stack instead of scroll when transitions carry no meaning, when steps read fine standalone, or when mobile needs a different chart type — the test for keeping it scrolly is whether the transitions are genuinely meaningful.

Charts, which readers cannot catch for you

Deceptive charts produced interpretations 58.5% to 129.5% larger than controls across 330 participants, and an inverted axis made 97.5% respond incorrectly. Across five studies, 83.5% showed a truncation effect and instruction did not eliminate it. Chart familiarity, visual ability and education showed no protective correlation, and readers have near-universal difficulty spotting construction errors at all.

Reader sophistication is not a mitigation. Axis integrity has to be enforced at generation time.

Every chart validated at build: zero baseline for length encodings or an explicit break, honest aspect ratio, area encoding area rather than radius, consistent intervals, named units, a legend or direct labels, uncertainty shown where it exists, and no dual axes implying correlation. And an editorial title stating the conclusion rather than a descriptive label — across 393 visualisations with eye-tracking on 33 participants, titles and supporting text materially drove recognition and recall, and recognisable objects appeared in 74% of the most memorable third against 8% of the least.

Sameness, which is measured and has a measured cause

This one usually gets argued as taste. It is not. Web design became less homogeneous between 2003 and 2007, then dramatically more so: layout similarity distance fell 44% from 2010 to 2019 across roughly two million pairwise image comparisons, in both colour and spatial layout, and the strongest correlate was shared use of a small number of frameworks and libraries.

The consequence for anyone producing many pages is exact. The invariant that reads as sameness is layout skeleton and motion signature, not palette — so re-colouring the previous report fixes nothing. What can be reused safely is the hidden infrastructure: the chrome, the citation component's behaviour, the auditor, the focus styles, the claim-graph shape. What cannot: hero composition, section silhouette, chart grammar, illustration system, transition metaphor, typography, palette.

The precedent worth copying is the Financial Times', which built reusable story patterns around specific reader questions rather than generic layouts, and introduced each one only after it had already worked on a prominent story. The pattern earned its reuse by working once. And a counterweight, because distinctive is not the same as arbitrary: cartoon styling and hand-drawn fonts have been found to reduce perceived credibility, while subject-derived, recognisable imagery improves memorability. Commit hard to a subject-native visual argument; never to novelty.
