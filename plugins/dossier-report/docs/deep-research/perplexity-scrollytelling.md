# Building Evidence-Dense Scrollytelling Reports: Narrative Structures, Motion, Trust, and Failure Modes

Single-page, evidence-dense scrollytelling reports sit at the intersection of narrative journalism, data visualization, and interaction design, and the practitioners who build them have converged on a set of recognizable but still evolving patterns for structure, animation, and trust-building. Drawing from foundational work on narrative visualization, especially Segel and Heer’s taxonomy of genres and the martini-glass structure, contemporary newsroom practice around scrollytelling, and recent empirical studies on data storytelling, this report argues that effective pages are typically organized as tightly authored, linear stems that gradually open into more exploratory segments, with each scroll “state” or panel carrying a single conceptual payload that is visually and textually reinforced.[1][5][11][14] Empirical evidence from controlled and quasi-experimental studies indicates that multimodal, narrative data stories can significantly improve comprehension, recall, and engagement compared with conventional text or static graphics, but these gains are contingent on careful pacing, limited cognitive load per step, and motion that serves continuity rather than spectacle.[3][11][20] Trust and credibility hinge less on whether AI or automation is involved in production and more on visible provenance practices: persistent, fine-grained data-source disclosure; judicious use of hyperlinks; clear uncertainty communication; and UI patterns that make verification possible without derailing the reading flow.[6][11][14][15] On the implementation side, GSAP-based scroll-linked animation has emerged as a de facto motion layer, but must be constrained by explicit Core Web Vitals budgets for LCP, INP, and CLS, while Three.js/WebGL is best reserved for tasks that are intrinsically spatial or otherwise impossible to render convincingly in 2D, implemented via progressive enhancement with HTML-first fallbacks.[7][8][18] Accessibility requirements, codified in WCAG’s guidance on “animation from interactions” and vestibular disorders, demand robust support for `prefers-reduced-motion`, non-scroll interactions, and alternative paths that preserve content without motion, and these constraints are often at odds with naive scrolljacking patterns that research has shown to harm user control, comprehension, and task success.[9][10] Finally, the recurring failure modes—over-structured scrollytelling that traps readers, performance collapse on mobile, misleading or under-labeled charts, visually homogeneous templates that erode perceived authorship, and claims whose evidentiary chains are opaque—can each be mapped to specific design and engineering anti-patterns; these, in turn, can be turned into concrete rules that an automated report-generation system must satisfy or test against for each page it produces.[7][8][10][11][13][17]  

## Conceptual Foundations of Narrative Visualization and Scrollytelling

### Defining Narrative Visualization and Data-Driven Stories

The contemporary idea of narrative visualization, in the sense used by newsroom graphics desks and independent data journalists, is grounded in the notion of telling a structured story through visual representations of data rather than merely presenting charts or dashboards.[1][14] Segel and Heer define narrative visualizations as visualizations that employ rhetorical and storytelling devices—such as ordering, highlighting, and annotation—to guide readers through data in ways analogous to traditional narratives.[1] A more recent systematic review by Ojo and colleagues synthesizes definitions across the literature and proposes that a data-driven story is a series of related events in a meaningful context, constructed around data in order to facilitate understanding and decision-making.[14] This definition is important for automated systems because it emphasizes events, context, and decision relevance, all of which must be recoverable from the underlying research corpus and reflected in the visual and textual structure of the output page.[14]

Within this framework, the key distinction is between explanatory and exploratory experiences. Segel and Heer characterize explanatory visualizations as author-driven, with a prescribed sequence, heavy use of messaging and annotation, and limited interactivity, whereas exploratory visualizations are reader-driven, with high interactivity and many possible paths.[1] The Quanthub summary of author-driven versus reader-driven data storytelling echoes this distinction: author-driven stories emphasize clear, focused messages, linear ordering, and constrained interactivity, while reader-driven narratives emphasize flexible exploration, modular content, and extensive user control.[2] For the single-page scrollytelling reports of interest here, most successful examples occupy a hybrid space: they begin in a strongly author-driven mode to establish context and key claims, then gradually allow more exploratory interactions, such as filters or drill-downs, without ever becoming fully open-ended dashboards.[1][11][14]

The idea that a story is “data-driven” does not imply that the story emerges automatically from the data; instead, it reflects editorial choices about which relationships, trends, and outliers to elevate, and which metaphors or scenes to use to make these accessible.[1][11] The YourDataStories framework, for instance, explicitly separates stages of discovery, assistance, insight, leverage, and trust, where insight involves both visualizations and automatically generated narrative text, while trust involves transparency around sources and rights.[14] For an automated system that must generate scrollytelling pages from a research corpus, these conceptual distinctions imply that it needs not just charting capabilities but also a narrative planner that can choose which data events constitute the “related events in a meaningful context,” and a trust layer that surfaces provenance in a form readers can act on.[1][11][14]

### Narrative Genres and the Martini-Glass Structure

Segel and Heer identify seven genres of narrative visualization—magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, and video—each with different balances of narrative control and interactivity.[1] Among these, the interactive slide show and certain magazine-style layouts with scroll-triggered panels are closest to the scrollytelling form used in long web articles, because they present discrete scenes that the reader progresses through sequentially, often with animation linking states.[1][5] To describe the high-level narrative structure of many such pieces, Segel and Heer introduce the martini-glass pattern, in which the story begins with a narrow, linear “stem” that all readers follow, then opens up into a wider “bowl” where readers are free to explore interactive views or alternate paths.[1] In this structure, the author-driven segment functions as a jumping-off point for reader-driven exploration, and the transition between them is typically marked by a change in interaction density or visual openness.[1]

The martini-glass pattern is particularly relevant for scroll-driven pages because scrolling itself can become the mechanism for stepping through the stem, with each scroll state representing a fixed narrative step, and then later scroll segments yielding to more open layouts with embedded controls and less rigid sequencing.[1][5][11] The scrollytelling study by Oestreich and colleagues, which analyzes 50 examples to derive a vocabulary of “scrollers,” effectively documents how these martini-glass stems are visually and interactively realized.[5] They identify techniques such as graphic sequences, animated transitions, panning and zooming, scrolling through videos, and auto-playing animated content as core building blocks, and they show how these are combined to create sequences where visual changes are tightly linked to scroll progression.[5] For an automated generator, the martini-glass abstraction offers a simple but powerful control structure: it can plan a fixed series of panels for the linear stem and then decide whether and where to switch into more reader-driven modules, such as interactive maps or expandable evidence sections, based on topic and corpus complexity.[1][5][14]

Scrollytelling itself has been recognized in the research literature as a distinct genre within data-driven storytelling. The systematic review on telling stories with data notes that the term was adapted from long-form journalism and emphasizes vertical scrolling combined with multimodal content, usually with a defined endpoint rather than infinite scroll.[14] The practitioner survey on evaluating narrative visualizations likewise refers to “scrolly telling” as a common pattern in news media sites, where content reveals itself progressively as the reader scrolls vertically.[11] However, practitioners interviewed in that study describe scrollytelling as a double-edged sword: it can induce undivided attention, but when overused it creates an “overly structured experience” that constrains user freedom and leads to drop-off when the path feels too long or rigid.[11] This ambivalence is a crucial constraint for any automated scrollytelling system: it must be capable of producing pieces that use scroll-linked structure judiciously, without falling into the trap of turning every topic into a long, tightly scripted “ride” that frustrates users who want to skim, search, or jump to specific evidence.[5][10][11]

### Scrollytelling in Newsroom Practice

The scrollytelling vocabulary study by Oestreich and colleagues offers a rare systematic look at how newsrooms and creative agencies actually implement scroll-driven stories.[5] By analyzing 50 examples, they identify granular “scrollers”—fine-grained units of scroll-triggered behavior—and group them into five standard techniques: graphic sequences, animated transitions, panning and zooming, scrolling through movies, and showing or auto-playing animated content.[5] Graphic sequences involve sequences of static or lightly animated visuals that change with scroll; animated transitions smoothly morph or move elements between states; panning and zooming change the viewport over a larger graphic; scrolling through movies uses scroll to control video playback; and auto-playing animated content brings in pre-rendered motion that is synchronized with narrative segments.[5] Their goal is to give designers and developers a shared vocabulary, which implicitly acknowledges that scrollytelling is still expensive and often built ad hoc, and that reusable patterns are needed.[5]

The practitioner survey on evaluating narrative visualizations adds qualitative depth to this picture. Designers and developers interviewed there report that scrollytelling is frequently used in news media to guide readers through complex data stories, and that its strength lies in its ability to control pacing and focus attention through progressive disclosure.[11] At the same time, they express concern that scrollytelling can be “overly structured” and can cause readers to lose interest if they have to scroll too much without feeling in control.[11] Some describe experiences where users stop halfway through a visualization because the path feels too long or repetitive, underscoring the need for careful sectioning and the inclusion of shortcuts or alternative navigation paths.[11] These comments align with usability findings from Nielsen Norman Group’s research on scrolljacking, which show that altering the normal pace or direction of scrolling can harm user control, discoverability, and task success.[10] Together, these studies suggest that for an automated system, scroll-linked narrative should be treated as a powerful but risky device that must be governed by explicit rules about length, pacing, and opportunities for exit or bypass, rather than as a default page skeleton.[5][10][11]

A further pattern visible in newsroom practice is the pairing of scrollytelling narratives with persistent or recurring visual motifs that anchor the story, such as a map that gradually zooms or filters, or a central chart that updates with each scroll panel. Segel and Heer note this in their examples of annotated charts and partitioned posters, where a main visual is revisited with different highlighting or annotation layers as the story progresses.[1] The scrollytelling vocabulary likewise shows that many scrollers are variations on highlighting or transitioning within a fixed visual frame rather than constant scene changes.[5] For an automated generator, this suggests a bias toward reusing a small number of visual frames across multiple scroll states, updating them to reflect different aspects of the data, rather than instantiating a new chart or illustration for every paragraph; this not only reduces implementation complexity but also supports comprehension by making changes comparable across states.[1][5][11]

## Narrative Structures and Production Patterns for Single-Page Scroll-Driven Reports

### Author-Driven Linear Stems and “One Idea per Scroll”

Author-driven narratives in data storytelling follow a linear, structured path, with a clear beginning, middle, and end, and a predefined ordering that reflects the author’s perception of importance and logic.[1][2] In practice, the linear stems of scrollytelling pieces resemble this author-driven mode: each scroll step or screenful typically introduces a single idea or claim, often supported by a focused visual and brief explanatory text.[1][5][11] While the phrase “one idea per scroll” is more of a practitioner mantra than a formal rule, it can be grounded in insights from both cognitive psychology and narrative visualization research: minimizing the number of new concepts per panel reduces cognitive load, and the strong coupling of text and visual on each screen reinforces the intended message.[1][11][17]

Segel and Heer’s analysis of annotated charts and magazine-style narratives shows that effective pieces often rely on short textual annotations near data points, combined with progressive highlighting of specific regions or series as the narrative unfolds.[1] ProPublica’s design principles for news apps and graphics also emphasize that designers should make the most important editorial element—the “nut” of the story—the most noticeable thing on the page, using visual hierarchy to focus attention.[17] In a scroll-driven context, this translates into each scroll state having a clear focal point, whether it is a highlighted line on a chart, a single sentence summary, or a dominant image, with supporting details kept nearby but visually subordinate.[11][17] When automated generation is involved, a planner can operationalize “one idea per scroll” by partitioning the underlying analysis into atomic claims or findings and ensuring that each scroll state introduces at most one of these, with supporting evidence either inline or accessible via expandable controls.[1][11][14]

The Quanthub comparison of author-driven and reader-driven narratives underscores that author-driven stories are valuable when complex information needs to be distilled and when the author wants to ensure that key points are understood in a particular order.[2] For evidence-dense reports, this is often the case: the underlying research corpus may contain conflicting evidence, multiple hypotheses, and nuanced caveats, and a purely exploratory presentation risks readers either missing critical context or misinterpreting charts.[11][13][14] A linear stem that walks through definitions, methods, key findings, and then implications can prevent premature conclusions and set up later exploratory segments where readers can drill deeper into data or methods if they choose.[1][11][14] For an automated system, this suggests that regardless of topic, pages should begin with a short sequence of mandatory panels that establish problem framing, data scope, and main findings, before offering any free-form exploration, with each of these panels obeying the “one idea per scroll” constraint.[1][11]

### Hybrid Structures: Martini Glass, Interactive Slideshow, and Drill-Down

The martini-glass structure formalizes a hybrid narrative that begins in an author-driven mode and transitions into reader-driven exploration.[1] In scrollytelling pages, the linear stem maps naturally to the “stem” of the martini glass, while the “bowl” corresponds to sections where the reader controls filters, zoom levels, or alternate views.[1][5][11] Segel and Heer note that once the author’s intended narrative is complete, the visualization opens up to a reader-driven stage, with features such as highlighting, filtering, and path-choosing available.[1] The key for automated systems is to decide where the linear stem should end and what forms of exploration are appropriate for the topic and data. For instance, a report on geographic disparities may end its stem with a nationwide map, then allow readers to select regions or demographic groups; a report on model architectures might end with a comparative chart and then permit axis re-scaling or model-filtering.[1][5][14]

Interactive slideshows represent another hybrid structure, where each “slide” is a fixed state but the reader can move forward or backward at will, sometimes with thumbnail overviews or progress indicators.[1] Many scrollytelling pages mimic slideshow behavior by snapping sections to the viewport as the user scrolls or by using scroll as a shortcut for “next slide.”[5][10] The Nielsen Norman Group’s analysis of scrolljacking warns that when designers change the pace or direction of scrolling, they risk conflicting with users’ mental models of continuous scroll, leading to disorientation and frustration.[10] They suggest that scrolljacking is only beneficial when it progressively discloses valuable information and breaks down complex concepts, and that even then it must be used sparingly and below the fold.[10] For an automated system that might be tempted to implement slideshow-like snapping or bespoke scroll physics via GSAP ScrollTrigger, this implies strict rules: avoid altering scroll direction, minimize changes to scroll rate, and reserve any snapping behavior for self-contained segments that clearly communicate their boundaries and allow standard scrolling elsewhere.[7][10][16]

Drill-down structures emerge in sections where readers can move from aggregate views to more detailed breakdowns, often via clicking or tapping on elements rather than scrolling alone.[1][11] The YourDataStories framework’s “insight” stage envisions explanatory visualizations combined with social features, while the “leverage” stage involves sharing conclusions and discussing validity.[14] In practice, evidence-dense pages often incorporate drill-downs in methods or appendix sections, where clicking on a chart element reveals raw data distributions, confidence intervals, or sensitivity analyses.[11][13] For automated generation, drill-down segments can be treated as optional modules attached to key findings, triggered by click rather than scroll, so that readers who want surface-level understanding are not forced through deep technical material, but those who wish to verify or challenge claims have a clear path.[11][14][15] This modular view aligns well with Quanthub’s description of reader-driven narratives presenting content in smaller, self-contained units that readers can access independently.[2]

### Section Pacing and Progressive Disclosure

Pacing in scrollytelling refers to how quickly new ideas, visuals, and interactions are introduced as the reader scrolls. Practitioners interviewed in the narrative visualization evaluation survey stress the importance of progressive disclosure, where content reveals itself gradually in response to scroll, but caution that if the sequence is too long or dense, readers will lose interest.[11] One participant notes that you do not want readers to stop halfway through because they no longer care, which is a recurring concern in long interactive pieces.[11] The scrollytelling vocabulary study complements this by showing that different scroller types can be used to adjust pacing: for example, a graphic sequence with small visual changes between steps feels slower and more deliberate, while a scroll-through-movie segment with rapid visual motion can convey more material in fewer scroll units.[5] For an automated system, it is not enough to decide how many panels a story should have; it must also decide the “cadence” of new information per panel and per screenful.

Empirical work on digital storytelling in education suggests that multimodal narratives can sustain attention and motivation more effectively than traditional instruction, but that engagement gains are connected to emotional and cognitive factors such as enjoyment, perceived utility, and clarity.[3] In a quasi-experimental study of online digital storytelling for Grade 7 EFL learners, the storytelling condition led to significantly greater improvements in authentic listening comprehension and higher post-intervention engagement across behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions.[3] Interview data from the same study attribute these gains to factors like greater enjoyment, clearer understanding through multimodal cues, and increased confidence.[3] Although this work is not about scrollytelling specifically, it reinforces the idea that pacing should maximize clarity and emotional resonance rather than sheer density of information per screen, and that supportive cues—like visual redundancy and consistent structure—can make complex material feel more manageable.[3][11][20]

From a design-principles perspective, ProPublica’s guidance on graphics emphasizes ruthless clarity: eliminate unnecessary elements, use white space to direct attention, and group related elements proximally so that readers can easily connect text and data.[17] Applied to scrollytelling, this suggests that each section should be tightly scoped and visually uncluttered, with ample whitespace around the main visual and text so that the reader’s eye is not overwhelmed.[11][17] Automated generation can enforce this by limiting the number of separate visual components per panel, constraining text length per panel, and ensuring that annotations are positioned near the data they describe.[11][17] It can also calculate approximate reading time for each linear stem and set thresholds to avoid excessively long “rides,” perhaps by detecting opportunities to move supporting material into collapsible sections or appendices, thus preserving readable pacing for the majority of readers.[11][14]

### Implications for Automated Scrollytelling Generation

Taken together, these structural patterns imply a concrete planning model that an automated system like a Claude Code skill must embed. At a minimum, the generator needs to: first, segment the research corpus into logically ordered narrative units—definitions, methods, key results, caveats, implications—mapped to discrete scroll states; second, decide whether the story warrants a martini-glass structure with a linear stem followed by interactive exploration, or a shorter linear-only or modular hybrid; third, allocate one main idea or claim per scroll state, with a single primary visual and concise supporting text; and fourth, decide where drill-down interactions and optional modules should live relative to the linear path.[1][2][11][14] This planning can be guided by heuristics extracted from the literature, such as maximum recommended scroll length for tightly scripted sequences, the need for early establishment of data provenance and uncertainty, and the importance of giving readers ways to bypass or re-enter the linear path.[10][11][13]

Additionally, the system must factor in the cost of animation and 3D in both performance and cognitive terms. Every decision to make a panel animated rather than static, or to use scroll-triggered transitions rather than simple cut transitions, should be tied to a narrative justification: for instance, motion that reveals change over time, spatial relationships, or causal chains can support understanding, whereas motion that merely decorates or distracts is more likely to harm comprehension and frustrate users.[4][5][10][11] These trade-offs become clearer when we consider empirical evidence on comprehension, recall, and engagement, which we address next.

## Empirical Effects of Narrative Visualization, Animation, and Scrollytelling

### Data Storytelling and Information Recall

The central promise of narrative visualization is that readers will understand and remember data better when it is presented as a story rather than as isolated charts or tables. Zdanovic and colleagues investigated this by examining how data storytelling influences the ability to recall information.[20] In their experimental setup, participants were exposed to either a narrative presentation of data or a more traditional presentation, and recall was measured afterwards.[20] Their findings show that data storytelling can indeed improve recall, although the magnitude and nature of the effect depend on factors such as the complexity of the material and the specific narrative devices used.[20] This aligns with broader cognitive theories suggesting that stories provide scaffolds for memory by organizing information into causal and temporal structures.[14][20]

The systematic review on telling stories with data synthesizes multiple studies and concludes that narrative elements such as sequencing, character-like entities (e.g., countries, companies, or models), and explicit causal framing generally improve comprehension and recall compared with non-narrative presentations.[14] However, the review also notes that many studies are small-scale and context-specific, and that results vary depending on the domain and audience.[14] For practitioners building scrollytelling pages, and for automated systems emulating their work, the practical takeaway is that organizing evidence into a coherent narrative sequence is likely to aid recall, especially for non-expert audiences, but that this benefit is not automatic and can be undermined by poor design or excessive complexity.[11][14][20] The “one idea per scroll state” discipline discussed earlier can be seen as an implementation of this principle: by ensuring that each panel has a single, memorable message, the system increases the likelihood that readers will later recall the key points.[1][11][17]

### Multimodal Storytelling, Comprehension, and Engagement

Empirical work on digital storytelling in educational contexts provides more nuanced insights into how multimodal narratives affect comprehension and engagement. In the Guangzhou EFL study, online digital storytelling (ODST) was compared with conventional instruction over eight weeks, with Grade 7 students exposed to authentic spoken English as primary input.[3] The ODST class, which experienced multimodal stories combining audio, visual, and textual cues, achieved significantly greater improvements in authentic listening comprehension than the control class.[3] The effect sizes for engagement were medium to large across behavioral, emotional, and cognitive subdimensions, meaning that students in the storytelling condition were more attentive, more emotionally invested, and more willing to invest cognitive effort.[3] Interviews revealed that students found stories more enjoyable, felt that the visual scaffolds clarified meaning, and perceived greater utility in the effort required.[3]

While the domain differs from data journalism, the mechanisms are relevant. Visual scaffolds and paralinguistic cues can help readers integrate information from multiple channels, making complex ideas easier to follow.[3][14] Sustained attention and positive affect also appear to mediate comprehension: learners who enjoy the experience and feel more confident are more likely to persist with challenging material and to engage in effortful strategies like inference and self-explanation.[3] Narrative visualization can leverage similar mechanisms by using animation and visual redundancy to emphasize key points, providing contextual cues that support interpretation, and fostering a sense of progression and accomplishment as users scroll through the story.[1][5][11][14] For an automated scrollytelling system, this suggests that even in technical domains, multimodal cues—such as synchronized textual highlights and visual changes—can aid understanding and engagement, provided they are not overwhelming or gratuitous.[4][5][11]

### Evaluation of Narrative Visualizations in Practice

Despite these promising findings, the practitioner survey on evaluating narrative visualization reveals that many teams rely on informal and ad hoc methods to assess comprehension and engagement.[11] Interviewed practitioners report that the most common evaluation method is informal group discussion, with colleagues providing feedback based on their experience and intuition.[11] Formal user testing, A/B experiments, and quantitative metrics are used less frequently, often due to resource constraints or tight deadlines.[11] This ad hoc approach means that many design decisions about scrollytelling structure, animation, and interaction are guided more by craft and convention than by systematically measured outcomes.[11]

The same survey, however, compiles and categorizes evaluation heuristics into three high-level categories: composition, reader experience, and credibility and trust.[11] Composition includes principles such as clear structure, appropriate visual hierarchy, and concise messaging; reader experience covers aspects like interactivity aligned with user goals, appropriate pacing, and avoidance of fatigue; credibility and trust involve clear data-sourcing, honest representation of uncertainty, and avoidance of misleading rhetoric.[11] Practitioners agree that identifying data sources is non-negotiable for trust, and that “scrolly telling” should be used judiciously to avoid over-constraining the experience.[11] For automated systems, these heuristics can be converted into programmatic checks, such as ensuring that every chart is accompanied by visible source information, that interactive elements are only introduced where they add clear value, and that the length and density of scrollytelling sequences stay within empirical comfort ranges.[11][14]

A separate systematic mapping study on narrative information visualization similarly notes that evaluating narrative visualizations presents unique challenges because traditional assessment methods are insufficient for capturing both storytelling effectiveness and user engagement.[12] The authors highlight that many studies focus on either visualization quality or narrative impact, but not both, and that there is a need for multi-method approaches combining behavioral metrics, comprehension tests, and qualitative feedback.[12] For an automated generator, this implies that offline evaluation against synthetic metrics (like number of charts or words per panel) is unlikely to capture actual reader experience; instead, iterative deployment with instrumentation for scroll-depth, dwell time, interaction rates, and perhaps inline comprehension checks will be necessary to refine generation rules, even if the initial rule set is grounded in current literature.[11][12][20]

### Animation and Scroll-Linked Transitions: When They Help

Animated transitions between data states can support understanding by preserving object constancy and making relationships between views explicit.[4] In the context of data videos, Zhao and colleagues describe how designers distill a data story into a sequence of “data facts” and visually connect them through animated transitions, which help viewers follow changes over time, across categories, or between aggregations.[4] Their work proposes a design space for such transitions, including techniques like staged introduction of data, morphing between chart types, and orchestrated camera movements, all aimed at preserving continuity and highlighting relationships.[4] These techniques are conceptually similar to the animated transitions used in scrollytelling, where scroll position controls the progression of the animation and ensures that viewers can scrub back and forth to inspect changes.[5][16]

Scroll-driven animations, enabled in recent browsers by CSS features such as `animation-timeline: scroll()`, offer performance advantages over JavaScript-based scroll-triggered animations because they run on the compositor thread and avoid main-thread jank.[16] As CSS-Tricks notes, scroll-driven animations differ from scroll-triggered ones in that the animation’s progress is directly tied to scroll position, pausing and reversing with the scroll direction, which gives users a sense of direct manipulation and can enhance understanding of relationships.[16] For instance, a bar chart that grows or shrinks as the user scrolls through time can make trends more salient and memorable, especially if each change is small and continuous rather than abrupt.[4][16] For an automated system, adopting scroll-driven CSS animations where possible can improve both performance and comprehension by providing smooth, responsive transitions that align with users’ mental model of scroll as a time or sequence axis.[7][16]

The scrollytelling vocabulary study also emphasizes that animated transitions are used to maintain narrative cohesion when moving between visual states.[5] By smoothly animating changes, designers help readers understand that they are seeing the same underlying data in a new configuration, rather than an entirely new dataset.[4][5] This is particularly important when switching between chart types or scales, or when zooming into a subset of data; without transitions, readers may struggle to connect the dots.[4][5][11] Automated generation can incorporate this by defaulting to animated transitions when changing parameters like axes, filters, or zoom level within the same visual frame, while using simpler cut transitions when the narrative genuinely shifts to a new object or dataset.[4][5][16]

### Animation and Scroll-Linked Transitions: When They Hurt

Despite these benefits, motion can also harm comprehension, cause discomfort, and degrade performance if misused. Nielsen Norman Group’s scrolljacking study, based on usability testing of multiple websites, finds that altering the normal pace or direction of scrolling contradicts user expectations and leads to significant threats to user control and freedom.[10] Participants in their study experienced disorientation, difficulty predicting the outcome of scroll actions, and frustration when scrolljacked sections forced them through content at a pace or sequence they did not choose.[10] The study concludes that scrolljacking should be avoided in most cases, recommending that if it is used at all, it should be reserved for below-the-fold content that showcases critical information, with careful calibration of scroll rate and clear separation from normal-scrolling sections.[10] For an automated scrollytelling system, this implies that any deviation from normal scroll behavior—such as snapping, non-linear mapping between scroll and content, or horizontal scroll substitution—should be rare, explicitly justified by narrative needs, and subject to strict constraints.

WCAG 2.2’s guidance on “animation from interactions” adds another dimension by focusing on users with vestibular disorders or sensitivity to motion.[9] The success criterion 2.3.3 states that motion animation triggered by user interaction must be able to be disabled, unless the animation is essential to the functionality or information being conveyed.[9] The rationale is that animations, especially those triggered by scrolling, can cause dizziness, nausea, or headaches for people with vestibular disorders, particularly when non-essential motion like parallax scrolling is used.[9] The guidance suggests three main strategies: avoid unnecessary animation, provide a user control to turn off non-essential animations, and honor system-level prefers-reduced-motion settings.[9] For scroll-driven reports, where motion is central to the experience, this requires that all non-essential scroll-linked animations be suppressible without loss of content, and that essential animations be designed to minimize potentially triggering movement, such as large field-of-view changes or background-foreground parallax.[9][10]

Performance-related harms are also significant. Hon Tran’s article on Core Web Vitals for animation-heavy sites highlights how heavy JavaScript animation libraries like GSAP and Three.js can delay Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), harm Interaction to Next Paint (INP), and cause Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) if not carefully managed.[7] For instance, running heavy JavaScript before the hero content paints can push LCP beyond the recommended 2.5 seconds, and scroll or pointer handlers that hog the main thread can increase INP beyond 200 milliseconds, making interactions feel sluggish.[7] Entrance animations that alter layout properties instead of transforms can cause CLS above 0.1, leading to visible layout jumps.[7] These performance issues not only harm user experience but also indirectly impair comprehension by making pages feel unresponsive, increasing abandonment rates, and fragmenting the reading flow.[7][8] Automated systems using GSAP ScrollTrigger must therefore adhere to strict performance budgets, limiting the number of concurrent animations, prioritizing transform and opacity animations, and deferring non-critical JavaScript until after initial content has rendered.[7][8][16]

### Design Rules Derived from Evidence

Synthesizing these findings yields a set of design rules that can be encoded into an automated scrollytelling generator. First, narrative sequencing should follow an author-driven linear stem that introduces one main idea per scroll state, with each panel combining concise text, a primary visual, and clear annotation that emphasizes the intended message.[1][2][11][17][20] Second, hybrid structures like martini-glass narratives should transition into reader-driven exploration only after core concepts and claims have been established, and exploratory modules should be modular and optional rather than mandatory for basic understanding.[1][2][11][14] Third, animation and scroll-linked transitions should be used primarily to support continuity and illustrate change, preserving object constancy and making relationships between views explicit; motion that is decorative or gratuitous should be avoided.[4][5][11][16]

Fourth, scroll behavior must preserve user expectations: continuous, vertically oriented scroll with minimal hijacking, no changes in scroll direction, and limited use of snapping, especially above the fold.[5][10][11] Fifth, performance budgets derived from Core Web Vitals must constrain animation and 3D usage: LCP should remain under 2.5 seconds, INP under 200 milliseconds, and CLS under 0.1, with GSAP timelines and ScrollTrigger configured to animate only transforms and opacity, avoid layout thrashing, and run heavy scripts off the critical path.[7][8] Sixth, accessibility constraints require that all non-essential motion be suppressible based on user preferences, particularly `prefers-reduced-motion`, and that essential animations be designed conservatively to reduce vestibular risk.[9][10] Finally, all of this should be accompanied by rigorous provenance practices that make data sources, methods, and uncertainties visible and navigable, which we address in the next section.[6][11][13][14][15]

## Citation, Provenance, and Trust UX in Evidence-Dense Pages

### AI Provenance and Disclosure in Journalism

As AI systems increasingly participate in newswriting and data analysis, questions about provenance and disclosure become central to trust. Licenji and Hoxha’s systematic review of AI provenance and disclosure cues in journalism synthesizes empirical studies on how audiences respond when news is presented as “written by artificial intelligence.”[6] They distinguish between provenance cues, which indicate who or what authored the story, and disclosure cues, which reveal how AI was used, such as for drafting, translation, summarization, or image generation.[6] Across 47 studies, they find that effects on perceived credibility are predominantly null or conditional: there is no uniform “AI penalty,” and observed positive or negative effects depend on topic, cue wording, and study design.[6] Similarly, AI disclosure cues do not act as consistent “warning labels” that reduce trust; main effects are usually null or contingent on factors like the specificity of the disclosure and the presence of editorial oversight.[6]

These findings suggest that automated generation of scrollytelling pages does not automatically undermine credibility as long as transparency is handled thoughtfully. For practice, the authors recommend treating transparency as part of a broader accountability package: specifying the nature of AI involvement, indicating editorial review, and clearly communicating responsibility and correction processes.[6] For an automated system building evidence-dense reports, this implies that each page should include a provenance section explaining which parts of the pipeline were automated (e.g., narrative planning, chart generation, text drafting) and which were reviewed or edited by humans, where applicable.[6] If the system is fully automated, it should still describe its own methods and limitations, such as data coverage, model update dates, and known failure modes, so that readers can calibrate trust.[6][11][14]

### Data Source Identification and Trust

Practitioners in the narrative visualization evaluation survey overwhelmingly agree that identifying the source of visualized data is non-negotiable for trust.[11] The survey notes that prior research has shown that identifying data sources leads to greater trust in narrative visualizations, and that practitioners consider data-source referencing mandatory for every piece of work.[11] This aligns with the YourDataStories framework’s emphasis on a “trust” stage, where transparency about data sources, metadata, completeness, and respect for privacy and rights is crucial to user confidence.[14] For evidence-dense scrollytelling pages, this translates into two requirements: first, every chart or data-driven claim must be traceable to a clearly labeled source, ideally down to the dataset level; and second, the page should offer an overview of all sources used, including how they were selected and combined.[11][14]

In practice, data source identification can be implemented through inline labels, tooltips, footnotes, and end-of-page source sections. Practitioners favor proximity: ProPublica’s design principles stress that legends and explanatory text should be kept close to the data they describe, reducing clutter and easing comprehension.[17] This principle can be extended to source labels: placing a brief source tag directly below each chart (“Source: WHO, 2024”) helps readers associate data with provenance without leaving the flow of reading.[11][17] For more complex sourcing—such as multi-dataset composites or model-derived estimates—expandable details or hover tooltips can provide additional context, including links to original datasets, code repositories, and methodology descriptions.[11][13][14] Automated systems must ensure that these patterns are applied consistently, generating both short labels and detailed provenance entries for each visual and claim based on the research corpus metadata.[11][14]

### Hyperlinks, Inline Citations, and Perceived Credibility

Hyperlinks are another key mechanism for signaling and enabling verification. Johnson and Wiedenbeck’s experimental work, summarized in the “Hyperlinked World” article, finds that hyperlinks in a news story can increase perceived news credibility.[15] In one experiment, participants who read a news story with hyperlinks rated its credibility higher than those who read the same story without links, with a statistically significant though small effect size.[15] More interestingly, the study reports that hyperlinks particularly increase credibility in “value-framed” conditions, where the news frame emphasizes values or normative stakes.[15] This suggests that in evidence-dense reports, where normative claims about policy, ethics, or risk are common, linking key assertions to external sources can meaningfully boost perceived credibility.[15]

From a UX perspective, inline citations and hyperlinks must balance visibility with readability. If every sentence is decorated with long URLs or obtrusive footnote markers, readers may experience clutter and distraction; if sources are only listed at the end of the page, many readers will never see them and trust signals are weakened.[11][15][17] Effective patterns include brief inline markers that open tooltips with full citation details on hover or tap, and source sections that group references by claim or visualization rather than as a monolithic bibliography.[11][14][15] Automated systems generating pages from research corpora can leverage structured metadata to assign each claim to one or more sources and then render lightweight inline citation affordances—such as superscript numbers or icons—that reveal further detail on interaction, while also constructing a structured “Evidence and Sources” section that allows expert readers to audit the support for key claims.[11][14][15]

### What Makes Readers Actually Verify a Claim

While hyperlinks and source labels can increase perceived credibility, they do not guarantee that readers will click through to verify claims. The literature suggests that actual verification behavior is driven by a combination of factors: prior skepticism or interest, perceived stakes, cognitive effort required, and UI friction.[15] Johnson and Wiedenbeck’s experiments show increased information-seeking behavior when hyperlinks are present, but do not guarantee that every linked source is consulted.[15] Narrative visualization practitioners report that most readers will not dig into raw data or methodology unless motivated by high stakes or curiosity, but that expert or skeptical readers expect such material to be readily available.[11] The YourDataStories framework similarly distinguishes between discovery and trust stages, implying that only a subset of users will engage deeply with metadata, completeness, and anomaly details, but that providing these is essential for overall ecosystem trust.[14]

For an automated evidence-dense page, a reasonable design target is to make verification easy for those who choose to verify, while not forcing it on every reader. This can be achieved by making source affordances consistently visible but unobtrusive, such as small but legible source tags under charts and hoverable or tappable citation markers next to key claims.[11][15][17] Clicking or tapping should reveal structured information: source name, publication date, a brief description, and a link to the original, perhaps with an indication of how the data was transformed in the report.[11][13][14] Where multiple sources support or conflict on a claim, this should be indicated explicitly, possibly with a short note on the nature of disagreement, thereby signaling epistemic humility rather than overconfidence.[11][13][14] Automated generation can synthesize such structured provenance from the underlying corpus by associating each claim with the passages, datasets, and models that support it, then mapping this association into UI elements.[11][14][15]

### Provenance UX for Automated Report Generation

Bringing these strands together, an automated scrollytelling generator should treat provenance and trust UX as first-class design concerns. Each page should include, near its beginning, a brief methods and sourcing overview that describes at a high level what data and documents were used, how they were processed, and what role automation played.[6][11][14] Every chart and major claim should have an associated source set, with short labels visible in context and detailed metadata available via interaction.[11][14][15][17] Hyperlinks should be used judiciously in the narrative text, especially for normative or controversial claims, to signal that underlying support is available and to invite deeper inspection for those so inclined.[15] Where AI-generated content is involved, a concise disclosure should explain the nature of AI assistance and any human oversight, aligning with best practices from the AI provenance literature.[6]

For automated systems producing many pages across topics, consistency in provenance patterns builds trust, but excessive visual uniformity can make pages feel templated and less human-authored—a concern we return to later. One strategy is to keep provenance UI patterns consistent across pages while allowing topic-specific variation in layout, color, and illustration style, thereby balancing trust (through predictability) with a sense of authored specificity.[11][17] Another is to expose, in a dedicated “Evidence and Limitations” section, the system’s own blind spots, such as incomplete data coverage, contested sources, or potential biases, which is both an ethical imperative and a trust-building move grounded in transparency research.[6][11][13][14]

## Motion Layer and 3D: GSAP, ScrollTrigger, WebGL, and Performance Budgets

### Motion Stack Architecture: GSAP as Baseline

In practice, many modern scrollytelling pages in the news and creative agency space use GSAP (GreenSock Animation Platform) as their primary motion engine, often combined with the ScrollTrigger plugin to orchestrate scroll-linked behavior.[7][18] GSAP provides a high-level API for animating element properties, timelines for choreographing sequences, and plugins for integration with scroll position and WebGL contexts.[7][18] ScrollTrigger, in particular, allows developers to tie animations to scroll progress, pin elements, and scrub animations forward and backward in sync with scrolling, which is ideal for scrollytelling sequences where each scroll movement should advance a visual state.[18] The Cinematic Scroll case study describes using GSAP’s ScrollTrigger to create precise, scroll-linked animations that feel natural and responsive, controlling timing, easing, and scrubbing to align animation with user input.[18]

An automated generator using GSAP must, however, adopt a conservative motion budget informed by performance research. Hon Tran’s article on Core Web Vitals for animation-heavy sites offers concrete guidelines: developers should animate transforms and opacity rather than properties that trigger layout, avoid scroll or pointer handlers that perform heavy work on every event, and keep heavy JavaScript off the critical path so that hero content can render quickly.[7] For instance, they recommend rendering the Largest Contentful Paint element (often a hero headline or image) via server-rendered HTML and CSS, then attaching GSAP and other motion logic only after the page becomes interactive, to avoid delaying LCP.[7] For an automated system, these guidelines translate into generation rules: GSAP scripts should be loaded with `defer` or equivalent, primary hero content should be static at first paint, and most animations should be composed of transform and opacity tweaks rather than layout-affecting properties.[7][8]

CSS scroll-driven animations, where supported, can supplement or replace portions of GSAP behavior, especially for simple parallax or fade-in effects.[16] As CSS-Tricks notes, scroll-driven animations offload work to the browser’s compositor, reducing main-thread overhead and improving smoothness.[16] An automated system could adopt a layered approach: use CSS scroll-timeline for simple, ubiquitous effects like fade-ins and slight translations, while reserving GSAP ScrollTrigger for more complex, choreographed sequences, such as multi-step data transitions or interactions with Three.js scenes.[7][16][18] This division reduces JavaScript load and simplifies performance tuning, which is critical when generating pages at scale across topics and devices.[7][8][16]

### Core Web Vitals and Interaction Constraints

Core Web Vitals—especially LCP, INP, and CLS—are both user-experience metrics and ranking signals, and their thresholds impose concrete performance constraints on scrollytelling pages. Hon Tran summarizes the recommended thresholds as LCP under or equal to 2.5 seconds, INP under or equal to 200 milliseconds, and CLS under or equal to 0.1.[7] For animation-heavy sites, the primary threats are heavy JavaScript blocking hero paint (hurting LCP), scroll or pointer handlers and animation loops hogging the main thread (hurting INP), and entrance animations or lazy-loaded media causing layout shifts (hurting CLS).[7] Utsubo’s guide to WebGL and Three.js site SEO reinforces these points, arguing that “HTML-first” rendering and progressive enhancement are essential: meaningful page content must exist as crawlable markup before any renderer boots, and 3D bundles should be deferred or offloaded to web workers where possible.[8]

For an automated generator, these constraints can be encoded into budgets per page and per route. For example, the system might enforce that initial HTML markup contains all headings, section summaries, and at least one static illustration per major visual frame, ensuring that a meaningful representation of the page is available even if JavaScript fails or is delayed.[8][17] GSAP and ScrollTrigger scripts would be loaded after the main content, perhaps conditioned on viewport size or connection quality; heavy Three.js bundles would only be loaded when needed and could be dynamically imported to avoid blocking initial load.[7][8][18] Moreover, the system should avoid attaching expensive scroll event handlers directly and instead rely on either GSAP’s internal batching or CSS scroll-driven animations to minimize layout thrashing and main-thread work.[7][16] INP can be protected by minimizing JavaScript work during user interactions, using requestAnimationFrame batching, and pausing off-screen animations.[7][8]

To make these constraints actionable, an automated generator can maintain explicit performance budgets: for each page, maximum total animation duration, maximum number of simultaneous animated elements, maximum Three.js draw calls, and upper bounds on total JavaScript bundle size for motion and 3D libraries.[7][8] These budgets can be checked at build time, and pages that exceed them can fall back to simpler motion schemas or static alternatives, perhaps flagging the need for manual review. While such budgets are not specified in the cited work, the general principle of defining and enforcing performance budgets for 3D and animation-heavy sites is strongly recommended in both Hon Tran’s and Utsubo’s articles.[7][8]

### GSAP ScrollTrigger Patterns for Scrollytelling

GSAP’s ScrollTrigger plugin enables a variety of patterns that are particularly relevant to scrollytelling. The Cinematic Scroll case study describes using ScrollTrigger to synchronize the rotation of a 3D cylinder carousel and reactive particle systems with scroll position, creating an experience that feels like an interactive film.[18] More generally, ScrollTrigger allows pinning elements in place while scroll continues, animating properties in proportion to scroll progress, and linking multiple timelines to different sections of the page.[18] Combined with GSAP’s FLIP (First, Last, Invert, Play) technique, which animates layout changes by interpolating transforms rather than directly manipulating layout properties, designers can move elements between sections while preserving performance and avoiding CLS.[7]

For a generator, ScrollTrigger patterns can be abstracted into templates: pinned hero sequences where a single visual frame is animated through several states; stepper sections where each text block is associated with a particular visual substate; and “revealer” sections where elements fade and translate into view as they enter the viewport.[5][7][18] Each template would be parameterized by content type (chart, map, illustration), animation style (fade, translate, scale, highlight), and trigger points (e.g., start at top of viewport, end at bottom). Automated assignment of these templates should consider topic, corpus density, and performance budget: for dense quantitative material, pinned sequences with clear step indicators and modest animations may be preferable; for more illustrative or conceptual material, richer cinematic transitions might be warranted, within constraints.[5][7][18]

ScrollTrigger must, however, be configured to respect accessibility and user control. For example, when `prefers-reduced-motion` is set, ScrollTrigger timelines should be disabled or replaced with instantaneous state changes, ensuring that content remains accessible without motion.[7][9] Similarly, scrolljacking behaviors like changing scroll direction, dramatically altering scroll speed, or trapping focus within a pinned section should be avoided, as these have been shown to harm user control and task success.[10] Automated generation can enforce this by forbidding templates that involve horizontal scroll substitution or oversized pinned regions, and by ensuring that every ScrollTrigger-controlled section has clear boundaries where normal scrolling resumes.[7][10][16][18]

### WebGL and Three.js: When 3D Earns Its Place

Three.js and WebGL offer powerful possibilities for spatial and cinematic storytelling, but they come with significant performance costs and accessibility challenges. Utsubo’s 3D site SEO guide argues that many Three.js-heavy sites fail to rank or to perform adequately because they ignore Core Web Vitals and HTML-first principles.[8] They recommend rendering real content server-side, hydrating the canvas after initial paint, and using static images on mobile where the trade-off favors speed over animation.[8] They also emphasize that for many use cases, the “fix” is not to add more JavaScript but to reduce reliance on 3D where it is not essential, and to pre-render dynamic states into HTML where possible.[8] The Cinematic Scroll case study, while celebratory of 3D, also acknowledges the need for careful optimization: efficient render loops, texture compression, lazy loading, and object pooling are all described as necessary to maintain 60fps on modern devices.[18]

Given these constraints, a principled criterion for when 3D “earns its place” is whether the subject matter is intrinsically spatial or otherwise benefits from volumetric or continuous motion representations that cannot be adequately conveyed in 2D.[4][8][18] Examples might include structural relationships in neural network architectures, 3D geographic surfaces, or complex mechanical systems. For purely statistical or conceptual topics, 2D charts and illustrations are usually sufficient and far more performant.[1][4][8] Automated systems can implement this criterion by classifying topics and data types: if the underlying data includes 3D coordinates, spatial adjacency, or volumetric fields, and if the narrative involves interpreting these spatial relationships, then Three.js modules may be considered; otherwise, the system should default to 2D and static or lightly animated visuals.[1][4][8]

Even when 3D is justified, progressive enhancement is essential. Utsubo recommends shipping the page as HTML first, hydrating the 3D canvas after the load event, reserving canvas dimensions to avoid CLS, and using static image fallbacks on mobile.[8] They also suggest moving heavy shader compilation and geometry parsing into web workers using OffscreenCanvas, and enforcing 3D performance budgets (e.g., max textures, max draw calls) via continuous integration.[8] An automated generator can adopt similar strategies, ensuring that each 3D scene is accompanied by a static preview image and that text alternatives describe key information for screen readers and users with disabled JavaScript.[8][9] GSAP can then be used to coordinate camera movements or object animations with scroll, while respecting performance budgets and `prefers-reduced-motion`.[7][8][18]

### Progressive Enhancement and Fallbacks

Progressive enhancement is not just a performance strategy; it is also a trust and accessibility requirement. Utsubo’s HTML-first mantra—“every meaningful page state must exist as crawlable markup before the renderer ever boots”—captures this.[8] For scrollytelling pages, this means that the core narrative and its key evidence must be accessible in static or minimally interactive form, with JavaScript-based motion and 3D layered on top for capable devices and willing users.[7][8][9] Techniques include using `<noscript>` fallbacks inside canvas wrappers to describe content, setting explicit width, height, and aspect ratio on canvases and images to prevent CLS, and pre-rendering dynamic states into multiple HTML routes where deep linking is important.[8][9]

For an automated generator, progressive enhancement implies a two-phase rendering model. In the first phase, the system produces a fully functional, static HTML page: sections with headings, paragraphs, static charts or images (which can be SVG or PNG), and source labels, all accessible and navigable without JavaScript.[8][17] In the second phase, motion and 3D are layered in via data attributes and JSON configuration, which GSAP and Three.js scripts read to instantiate animations and interactive scenes.[7][18] If JavaScript fails or is blocked, the static layer still provides a coherent, evidence-dense report; if `prefers-reduced-motion` is set, the scripts detect it and either do nothing or substitute minimal state changes.[7][9] This architecture not only improves robustness and accessibility but also simplifies automated generation, since the static layer can be tested and validated independently of the motion layer.[7][8][9]

## Accessibility in Scroll-Driven and Animated Content

### Applicable WCAG Criteria and Vestibular Concerns

Scroll-driven and animated content must comply with Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), particularly the success criterion 2.3.3 on “Animation from Interactions.”[9] This criterion requires that motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless the animation is essential to functionality or information.[9] The intent is to protect users from harm or distraction due to motion, especially those with vestibular disorders who can experience dizziness, nausea, or headaches from animations like parallax scrolling or large-scale motion.[9] The guidance explicitly notes that scrolling-induced movement, beyond the essential movement of content entering the viewport, can trigger vestibular reactions, and therefore recommends avoiding unnecessary animation, offering a control to turn off non-essential animations, or honoring system-level reduce-motion preferences.[9]

For scrollytelling pages that rely heavily on scroll-triggered animations, this means that designers and implementers must identify which animations are essential—for example, those that directly convey changes in data or spatial relationships—and which are decorative.[9] Decorative animations must be suppressible, and even essential animations should be designed to minimize disorienting motion, such as sudden zooms, rotations, or complex parallax.[9][10] Automated generation can operationalize this by tagging animation templates as essential or non-essential based on whether they encode unique information, and by wiring global controls that disable non-essential animations when `prefers-reduced-motion` is detected or when the user toggles a “reduced motion” setting on the page.[7][9][16]

### Prefers-Reduced-Motion and Motion Controls

The `prefers-reduced-motion` media query in CSS allows developers to respect user preferences for less motion, as recommended in WCAG’s sufficient techniques.[9] The guidance suggests using this query to prevent motion or to switch to alternative presentations when motion is not essential.[9] For example, a page-flipping animation can be disabled when reduced motion is enabled, while still allowing navigation between pages.[9] In the context of GSAP and ScrollTrigger, Hon Tran’s performance article and related practices recommend wiring a global check for `prefers-reduced-motion` to disable or simplify animations, particularly entrance animations and parallax effects.[7] CSS scroll-driven animations can likewise be conditioned on this media query, allowing the browser to skip animation timelines while preserving content visibility.[9][16]

For automated scrollytelling generation, support for `prefers-reduced-motion` should be a default, not an option. The system can emit CSS that wraps all non-essential animations in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)` blocks, and configure GSAP initializers to check the media query before creating timelines.[7][9][16] Additionally, an explicit UI control—such as a “Reduce Motion” toggle—can be provided on the page, which users can activate regardless of system settings; this control can set a CSS class on the root and a JavaScript flag that motion scripts read to decide whether to animate.[9] This dual approach honors both system and in-page preferences, and is particularly important for readers who may need to adjust motion settings on a per-site basis.[9][10]

### Keyboard and Screen-Reader Access to Scroll-Triggered Content

Accessibility also demands that content revealed or changed by scrolling be accessible via keyboard and screen readers, not solely via pointer and scroll gestures. WCAG generally expects that all functionality available by mouse be available via keyboard, and that content be perceivable by screen readers.[9] For scrollytelling pages, this means that essential content should not be locked behind scroll triggers that only respond to pointer wheel events; instead, scroll-based state changes should also respond to keyboard navigation, such as Page Down, Space, or arrow keys, and to focus movement for screen readers.[9] It also means that when content is dynamically inserted or updated in response to scroll, appropriate ARIA attributes and live-region announcements may be needed to inform screen-reader users of changes.[9]

Automated scrollytelling generation can address this by ensuring that each scroll state corresponds to a semantic section or article element with a heading, which becomes part of the document’s linear reading order.[17] Scroll-triggered animations should be layered on top of this structural HTML, not replace it.[7][8] For instance, when a new panel enters the viewport and a chart updates, the underlying text and headings should already be present in the DOM and in the correct reading order, allowing screen readers to access them without relying on scroll events.[8][9] Keyboard users should be able to navigate between sections using standard keys, and any pinned sections should not trap focus or prevent progression.[9][10] Automated generation can enforce this by mapping narrative segments to semantic sections and by avoiding patterns where content only becomes present in the DOM when scrolled into view, which can confuse assistive technologies.[8][9]

### Mitigating Vestibular Disorders and Cognitive Overload

Beyond the technical implementation of `prefers-reduced-motion`, designers must consider how motion patterns affect users with vestibular disorders and those susceptible to cognitive overload. WCAG’s understanding document notes that parallax scrolling, where background and foreground move at different rates, is often non-essential and can be particularly triggering.[9] The guidance suggests avoiding such patterns unless essential, and always providing an option to disable them.[9] Nielsen Norman’s scrolljacking research also shows that unusual scroll behaviors, such as changes in direction or speed, increase cognitive load and disorientation, even for users without diagnosed vestibular issues.[10] This reinforces the principle that scroll-based interactions should be as predictable and linear as possible, with minimal deviation from platform norms.[9][10]

Cognitive overload can also result from overly dense scrollytelling sequences, where each scroll introduces many new elements, animations, or concepts. Practitioners in the narrative visualization survey describe scrollytelling that is “overly structured” and exhausting, leading to drop-off.[11] ProPublica’s design principles advocate for simplicity, focus, and the elimination of unnecessary elements to protect users’ attention.[17] Automated generation can mitigate overload by enforcing conservative limits on the number of animated elements per panel, the amount of text per panel, and the frequency of major visual transitions, as well as by inserting “rest” sections—panels with static content and minimal motion—between more intense sequences.[11][17] These constraints align with both accessibility and comprehension goals, ensuring that motion serves to clarify rather than overwhelm.[3][11][17]

### Accessibility Rules for Automated Generation

For a Claude Code-style generator, accessibility must be encoded as a set of non-negotiable rules that gate motion and interaction decisions. At minimum, the system should: first, generate semantic HTML with headings, sections, and landmarks that reflect the narrative structure; second, ensure that all essential content is present in the DOM at load and is accessible without JavaScript; third, wrap all non-essential animations in `prefers-reduced-motion` conditions and provide an in-page motion toggle; fourth, avoid scrolljacking, parallax, and unconventional scroll behaviors by default, only allowing them in tightly constrained, below-the-fold segments; and fifth, provide text alternatives and descriptions for all charts, images, and 3D scenes, either as caption text or ARIA descriptions.[8][9][10][17] These rules can be enforced at template and build stages, with automated accessibility tests verifying that key criteria are met before deployment.

## Failure Modes in Evidence-Dense Scrollytelling Pages

### Scrolljacking and Over-Structured Experiences

Scrolljacking—the practice of altering the normal behavior of scrolling—has been shown to cause more harm than benefit in most contexts. Nielsen Norman’s study reports that scrolljacking breaks user expectations about control and freedom, leading to issues with discoverability, attention, efficiency, and task success.[10] Participants struggled to predict how much content a scroll action would reveal, found it difficult to locate specific information, and expressed frustration when sections forced them through content in a fixed sequence.[10] The study advises against putting important content inside scrolljacked sections and recommends limiting scrolljacking to below-the-fold segments that showcase already-present calls to action, optimizing scroll rate to minimize user effort, and avoiding changes to scroll direction.[10]

Practitioners in the narrative visualization survey echo these concerns, describing “scrolly telling” as sometimes “overly structured,” constraining the user experience and forcing readers through a long linear process.[11] When readers are required to scroll continuously through a complex visualization without opportunities to skim, jump, or exit, they may abandon the piece halfway.[11] For automated systems, generating scrolljacked sections that trap users within pinned sequences or demand precise scroll behavior is therefore a major failure mode, especially if essential evidence or claims are only present in those sections.[10][11] Mitigations include limiting pinned sequences to short spans, providing alternate navigation (e.g., a table of contents or jump links), and always ensuring that core claims are also available in static sections outside scrolljacked zones.[10][11]

### Performance Collapse on Mobile and Low-End Devices

Performance failures are a second major failure mode. Hon Tran’s analysis of animation-heavy sites shows that naive use of GSAP, Three.js, and other libraries can significantly harm Core Web Vitals, increasing LCP, INP, and CLS beyond acceptable thresholds.[7] Heavy JavaScript that runs before the hero element paints can delay LCP, while intensive scroll handlers and animation loops can degrade INP by making interactions unresponsive.[7] Entrance animations that modify layout properties or load media without reserved dimensions can cause CLS, leading to visible layout jumps.[7] Utsubo similarly notes that many WebGL-heavy sites are effectively invisible to search engines and painful for users on mobile because they lack HTML-first content, defer all rendering to expensive 3D contexts, and ignore the need for static fallbacks.[8]

An automated scrollytelling system that indiscriminately applies GSAP timelines and Three.js scenes to every topic will quickly encounter these performance cliffs, especially on mobile devices and slower connections.[7][8][18] Users may experience blank screens while canvases initialize, choppy scroll as animation loops compete for main-thread time, and jarring shifts as fonts and media load late.[7][8] These failures not only reduce engagement but can also erode trust: a page that feels “broken” or sluggish is unlikely to be perceived as authoritative, regardless of its content quality.[7][8][11] To avoid this, automated generation must conditionally enable heavy motion and 3D only where budgets allow, use static images on mobile for 3D scenes when necessary, and always render meaningful content in HTML before any JavaScript enhancement.[7][8]

### Misleading or Under-Labeled Data Visuals

Another failure mode lies in the realm of data ethics and rhetoric. Hullman and Diakopoulos’s work on visualization rhetoric and the “value of uncertainty visualization” highlights that authors often under-visualize uncertainty or frame data in ways that support particular interpretations.[13] When uncertainty is not shown, readers may overinterpret point estimates as precise and definitive, leading to misplaced confidence.[13] The narrative visualization evaluation survey notes that practitioners are aware of such issues but that systematic uncertainty visualization is not always implemented, sometimes due to perceived complexity or fear of confusing readers.[11][13] Unlabeled or under-labeled charts—those lacking clear axes, units, sources, or uncertainty indicators—are particularly problematic in evidence-dense reports, as they invite misinterpretation.[11][17]

ProPublica’s design principles stress that chart types should match the data, legends should be close to maps, and unnecessary elements should be removed to avoid clutter.[17] When charts are misaligned with data types (e.g., line charts for categorical data) or when legends and labels are missing or distant, readers can misread patterns or trends.[17] For automated systems, generating charts without appropriate labels, units, and uncertainty markers is a critical failure mode. Mitigation requires that the generator understand the semantics of each dataset—units, scales, uncertainty—and incorporate labels, baselines, and, where relevant, confidence intervals or credible ranges into each visualization.[11][13][17] It also requires that narrative text contextualize uncertainty and limitations rather than overstating precision.[11][13]

### Visual Homogeneity and Template Sameness

Visual homogeneity—where pages from the same producer share nearly identical layouts, color schemes, and interaction patterns—can be a subtle but important failure mode, especially in an era of AI-generated content. Readers may perceive templated pages as less authored and less trustworthy, even if content quality is high.[6][11] The narrative visualization survey implicitly touches on this by emphasizing the importance of composition and tailored design for each story, and by noting that overuse of certain patterns, like scrollytelling, can lead to fatigue.[11] ProPublica’s emphasis on aligning visual hierarchy with editorial goals also suggests that each story’s design should be deliberately crafted to highlight its unique “nut,” rather than applying a one-size-fits-all template.[17]

For an automated generator tasked with producing many reports, the temptation to reuse a small set of templates is strong, but doing so risks making pages recognizably machine-generated. To avoid this, the system should incorporate a design-variation layer that adapts layout, typography, color, and motion patterns to the subject matter and perhaps even to the data’s visual affordances.[1][5][17] For example, topics involving temporal evolution might employ horizontally oriented timelines with muted color palettes, while spatial topics might use map-centric layouts with distinct palettes and iconography.[1][5][17] While underlying structural patterns like martini-glass stems and provenance UI can remain consistent for usability and trust, superficial similarity should be minimized, both to maintain a sense of authorship and to avoid user fatigue.[11][17]

### Claims Outrunning Their Sources

Finally, claims that outrun their sources—assertions that are stronger or more general than the evidence supports—constitute a serious failure mode in any evidence-dense report. The YourDataStories framework explicitly includes a “trust” stage that focuses on transparency about privacy, rights, and validity, implying that conclusions should be grounded in data and open to scrutiny.[14] The narrative visualization survey’s credibility and trust heuristics likewise emphasize clear data sourcing and honest representation of uncertainty.[11] Hyperlink and provenance research show that while hyperlinks and AI disclosures can increase perceived credibility, they must correspond to genuine support; otherwise, they risk being perceived as cosmetic.[6][15]

Automated generators are particularly vulnerable to this failure mode if they aggregate multiple sources with differing conclusions or if they extrapolate beyond what the corpus supports. Without careful constraint, a system might produce summary statements that gloss over conflicting evidence or that imply causality where only correlation is present.[11][13][14] Mitigations include explicit requirement that each key claim be backed by identified sources, that conflicts between sources be acknowledged in the narrative, and that hedging language be used when evidence is limited or contested.[11][13][14] The system can also surface metadata about the nature of evidence—e.g., randomized trial, observational study, expert opinion—so that readers can gauge strength.[11][14] Failure to do so not only undermines trust but may also have legal and ethical consequences in sensitive domains.

## Implications for Automated Scrollytelling Report Generation

### Architectural Implications for the Generation Pipeline

Implementing a Claude Code skill that generates single-page, evidence-dense scrollytelling reports from a multi-backend research corpus requires a pipeline that reflects the narrative, motion, provenance, performance, and accessibility constraints discussed above. Conceptually, the pipeline can be divided into content analysis and planning, visual and interaction design, provenance mapping, motion and 3D augmentation, and performance and accessibility validation.[1][11][14] In the content analysis stage, the system must parse the research corpus, identify key themes, claims, evidence clusters, and uncertainties, and classify the topic in ways relevant to visualization (e.g., temporal, spatial, comparative).[1][14] Planning then maps these to a narrative structure—linear, martini-glass, or modular—with discrete panels obeying the “one idea per scroll” discipline.[1][2][11]

Visual and interaction design layers map narrative panels to visual frames and interaction templates: charts, maps, diagrams, text-only sections, and drill-down modules, each with associated GSAP and, where appropriate, CSS scroll-driven animation patterns.[1][5][16][18] Provenance mapping associates each claim and visual with its supporting sources, generating inline tags and detailed source records for later rendering.[11][14][15] Motion and 3D augmentation then decide, based on topic classification and budgets, which panels receive animated transitions, pinned sequences, or Three.js scenes, ensuring that every motion choice serves a narrative function and respects `prefers-reduced-motion` and Core Web Vitals constraints.[4][7][8][9][16] Finally, performance and accessibility validation uses automated tests and heuristics to ensure that the generated page meets LCP, INP, CLS, and key WCAG criteria, falling back to simpler templates when necessary.[7][8][9]

### Embedding Narrative and UX Rules into Templates

For practical implementation, many of the rules derived from research can be encapsulated in parameterized templates. For example, an “explanatory time-series stem” template might present a sequence of panels, each showing a single time-series chart with a highlighted segment and a short paragraph explaining a trend or event, with GSAP used only to animate the highlight and fade-in of annotations.[1][4][5][11] A “martini-glass exploration” template might combine a short linear stem with a section where readers can filter and sort a table or chart, with static content fully accessible and motion reserved for subtle transitions.[1][2][11][14] Templates can also encode provenance and trust patterns: every chart template automatically includes a source label and an expandable “Methods” note; every claim template includes slots for inline citation markers.[11][14][15][17]

The system must also encode negative templates—patterns that are forbidden because they are associated with failure modes. These might include full-page scrolljacking sequences without alternative navigation, heavy parallax effects that cannot be disabled, 3D hero scenes on mobile, or charts rendered without labels or sources.[7][8][9][10][11][17] During generation, the planner should avoid these patterns and, if a narrative concept suggests one (e.g., an initial desire to use parallax), substitute a more accessible and performant alternative (e.g., a pinned scroll with modest translation).[5][7][9][10] Over time, telemetry and user feedback can refine template selection rules, but initial rules should be grounded in the empirical findings summarized here.[3][5][10][11][20]

### Encoding Motion Budgets and 3D Eligibility Tests

Motion budgets and 3D eligibility can be operationalized as constraints in the generator. For motion, each page could have a maximum number of GSAP timelines, a maximum number of animated properties per panel, and an upper bound on total animation duration per scroll depth.[7][16][18] Each animation template can be tagged with a cost estimate, and the planner can ensure that the sum of costs does not exceed the budget for the page, scaling back or disabling non-essential animations when necessary.[7][8] `prefers-reduced-motion` handling should be baked into every animation template, ensuring that content remains accessible without motion.[7][9][16]

For 3D eligibility, topic classification and data type analysis can determine whether Three.js is considered. Only topics with inherently spatial or volumetric data, or where 3D convey significant additional information, should qualify; others default to 2D.[4][8][18] Even when qualified, 3D scenes must be subject to performance budgets: maximum bundle size, maximum textures, maximum draw calls, and frame time limits.[8] If estimated costs exceed budgets on typical mobile hardware, the system should either simplify the scene or replace it with pre-rendered images.[8][18] Static image fallbacks should always be generated and served to devices that indicate limited capabilities or preferences, such as low-power modes or constrained network conditions.[8][9]

### Provenance Management and Automated Citation UX

Provenance management in an automated system requires structured metadata about sources. Each input document and dataset should be ingested with metadata including title, author, publication date, URL, and type (e.g., peer-reviewed article, official documentation, news report).[11][14][15] During analysis, claims extracted from the corpus should be linked to the supporting passages and datasets, forming a provenance graph. The generator can then use this graph to populate inline citations, tooltips, and source sections for each claim and visual.[11][14][15] UI patterns should ensure that provenance is both visible and unobtrusive: e.g., small superscript markers that open on hover or tap, consistent “Source” labels under charts, and a structured “Evidence and Sources” section near the end of the page.[11][15][17]

Importantly, provenance management must handle conflicting evidence explicitly. If multiple sources disagree on a claim, the generator should either avoid making a definitive statement or clearly present the disagreement, perhaps in a dedicated “Conflicting Evidence” panel that explains the differences and their implications.[11][13][14] This not only aligns with ethical reporting practices but also builds trust by signaling epistemic humility.[11][13][14] AI provenance, as discussed earlier, should be represented alongside data provenance, with disclosures about which components of the page were generated automatically and what review, if any, was applied.[6] All of this must be done without overwhelming readers: default views can present high-level source information, with detailed metadata accessible via interaction for those who seek it.[11][14][15]

### Safeguards Against Failure Modes and Template Detection

Finally, the generator must include safeguards against the failure modes identified earlier. These safeguards can operate at generation time and at runtime. At generation time, automated checks can verify that: no essential content is locked exclusively inside scrolljacked sections; static HTML includes all key headings and summaries; every chart has axes, units, and sources; `prefers-reduced-motion` is honored in CSS and JavaScript; and performance budgets are not exceeded.[7][8][9][10][11][17] Pages failing these checks can be automatically simplified or flagged for human review.

To prevent template sameness and preserve a sense of authored specificity, the system can incorporate controlled variation in layout, color, typography, and motion patterns, guided by topic and perhaps by randomly chosen but constrained design parameters.[1][5][17] For example, it might select from a palette of layout grids and typographic systems, mapping them to topics based on semantic cues (e.g., financial topics get one aesthetic, environmental topics another), while preserving core usability and provenance patterns.[1][17] Over time, telemetry can reveal whether certain designs perform better on engagement and comprehension metrics, allowing the system to adapt while maintaining diversity.[11][12][20] By embedding these safeguards into the generation pipeline, the system can produce scrollytelling pages that both leverage the strengths of narrative visualization and avoid the pitfalls that practitioners and researchers have documented.

## Conclusion

Single-page, evidence-dense scrollytelling reports occupy a demanding design space that combines narrative structuring, data visualization, interactive motion, provenance UX, performance engineering, and accessibility. Research on narrative visualization, from Segel and Heer’s early taxonomy to recent systematic reviews, shows that effective stories usually follow hybrid structures like the martini glass, with a tightly authored linear stem that gradually opens into optional exploration, and that each narrative step is best kept conceptually small—one primary idea per scroll state, reinforced through coordinated text and visuals.[1][2][5][11][14] Empirical studies on data storytelling and digital narratives demonstrate that such structures, when combined with multimodal cues and clear messaging, can measurably improve comprehension, recall, and engagement compared with conventional presentations, though these benefits are contingent on careful pacing and disciplined use of motion.[3][11][20]

At the same time, the literature and practitioner experience highlight serious risks. Scrolljacking patterns that override user expectations, overlong and overstructured scrollytelling sequences, and heavy-handed animations can degrade usability, cause discomfort, harm performance, and ultimately prompt abandonment.[7][8][9][10][11] Misleading or under-labeled charts and opaque provenance further erode trust, especially in evidence-dense contexts where readers may seek to verify claims or understand uncertainty.[11][13][17] To counter these risks, practitioners emphasize transparent data sourcing, proximity of labels and legends to data, honest uncertainty communication, and UI patterns that facilitate verification without overwhelming.[11][13][14][15][17] Recent work on AI provenance in journalism suggests that AI involvement does not inherently reduce credibility, but that clear disclosures and accountability structures are essential.[6]

On the implementation side, GSAP and ScrollTrigger provide a powerful motion layer but must be constrained by explicit Core Web Vitals budgets and accessibility rules, while Three.js/WebGL should be reserved for topics where 3D representation materially aids understanding, implemented via progressive enhancement and paired with static fallbacks.[7][8][16][18] Accessibility requirements, codified in WCAG’s 2.3.3 “Animation from Interactions” and related guidance, demand strong support for `prefers-reduced-motion`, predictable scroll behavior, and alternative access paths for keyboard and screen-reader users.[9][10] For automated systems like a Claude Code skill, these constraints can be translated into concrete generation rules and template parameters: narrative planners that enforce one idea per scroll, structural patterns that embed provenance and uncertainty, motion templates that respect performance and accessibility budgets, and variation layers that prevent visual homogeneity while preserving usability and trust.[1][5][7][8][9][11][14][17]

The current state of practice is characterized by craft, innovation, and ad hoc evaluation, with a growing but still incomplete empirical base. Practitioners have developed effective heuristics for composition, reader experience, and credibility, but systematic testing and standardization are limited.[11][12] As automated generation becomes more feasible, the stakes for getting these heuristics right—and for encoding them into systems—rise. The evidence reviewed here supports a set of design principles that can guide such systems toward producing scrollytelling reports that are not only visually compelling but also performant, accessible, trustworthy, and measurably effective at conveying complex information. Continued research, especially controlled studies on specific scrollytelling patterns and motion techniques, will be needed to refine these principles, but the foundations are sufficiently clear to inform robust, rule-driven generation today.[3][5][10][11][14][20]
