# Evidence-Dense Scrollytelling Report Pages: What Measurably Works

---

## Executive Summary

- **(High Confidence)** The canonical structural taxonomy is settled and directly usable as a build rule: Segel & Heer's three genres — **martini glass** (author-driven stem → reader-driven mouth), **interactive slideshow** (interaction bounded within each slide), and **drill-down** (general theme → reader-selected instances) [Segel & Heer, IEEE TVCG, Nov 2010](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/segel-tvcg10.pdf). For an evidence-dense report page, martini glass is the correct default: constrain the argument, then release to a source list / data appendix.

- **(High Confidence)** Scrollytelling's measured advantage is **engagement, perceived clarity and reduced cognitive load — not comprehension**. In a 454-participant between-subjects study, scrollytelling matched plain text, two nutrition-label variants and a standalone interactive visualisation on comprehension accuracy and confidence, beat text on cognitive load and engagement, and produced *statistically inconclusive* differences on transparency and trust [Méndez & Such, CHI 2026, arXiv:2603.04367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04367). Spatial-immersion techniques (depth cues, camera moves) were rated more interesting and persuasive but **no more understandable or trustworthy** [Kim et al., arXiv:2411.18049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18049). Design implication: motion buys attention, never credibility.

- **(High Confidence)** Scrolljacking is the single best-documented failure mode. NN/g's usability testing found *the majority of participants experienced at least mild disorientation*, and on a fully scrolljacked page **every participant who visited was disoriented**; the worst combination is altered scroll rate *plus* altered direction *plus* text to read [Paul, NN/g, 6 Aug 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/). Bostock's earlier engineering argument reaches the same verdict from the mechanics: snapping requires inferring swipes from wheel events, giving hypersensitivity, dropped gestures, loss of the scrollbar, loss of keyboard control and loss of rubber-banding [Bostock, "How To Scroll", 3 Nov 2014](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/).

- **(High Confidence)** The animation evidence is genuinely **contested, and the contest resolves by task**. Heer & Robertson found significant advantages for animated *transitions between chart states* on object-tracking and value-estimation tasks [Heer & Robertson, IEEE TVCG 13(6):1240-7, 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.70539). Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt's review found apparent animation advantages over static graphics were confounds — the animated conditions conveyed more information or added interactivity — and that text-studying participants *improved* after a week while animation-studying participants *declined* [Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt, IJHCS 57(4):247-262, 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf). Rule: animate *between two states of the same encoding*; do not animate to explain.

- **(Medium Confidence)** Citation UX earns trust only in aggregate and only when links go to genuine primaries. Experimental work finds positive effects of hyperlink source-transparency on perceived credibility, but a single transparency feature had almost no effect while multiple indicators together did; and in recall testing only **34%** of participants could correctly identify the journalist and only **26%** could recall a detail from an editorial explanation they had seen [Designing Transparency Cues in Online News Platforms, arXiv:2108.02325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.02325). Hover-preview affordances measurably lower the cost of checking: Wikipedia Page Previews produced a **31% increase in overall page interactions** versus control [Wikimedia, Page Previews 2016 A/B Tests](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Page_Previews/2016_A/B_Tests).

- **(High Confidence)** The performance budget is externally fixed, not a matter of taste: LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms, CLS < 0.1, each at the **75th percentile of field data over 28 days**, all three must pass [Google Search Central, Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals). GSAP ScrollTrigger is main-thread; CSS scroll-driven animations (`animation-timeline`) run off the main thread because browsers scroll on a separate process and deliver scroll events asynchronously [Chrome for Developers, Scroll-driven animations](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/scroll-driven-animations).

- **(High Confidence)** Accessibility floors are legally anchored and asymmetric. **2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide is Level A** — mandatory under ADA Title II/III, Section 508 and EN 301 549 — and covers any auto-starting motion over 5 seconds shown in parallel with other content [W3C, Understanding SC 2.2.2](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/pause-stop-hide.html). **2.3.3 Animation from Interactions is Level AAA** and covers scroll-parallax specifically [W3C, Technique SCR40](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/client-side-script/SCR40). Build to a reduced-first architecture: static baseline, motion added only under `prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference`, so unsupported browsers fail safe.

- **(Medium Confidence)** Template sameness is a measured phenomenon with a measured cause. Web design became *less* homogeneous 2003–2007 then dramatically more so, with **layout similarity distance declining 44% from 2010–2019**, and the strongest correlate was shared use of a small number of frameworks and libraries [Goree, Doosti, Crandall & Su, CHI '21](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445156). <INFERENCE from="Goree et al.'s finding that library sharing correlates with visual similarity; Segel & Heer's genre taxonomy; The Pudding's step-sizing rules">A generator that reuses one motion library, one step-height rule and one sticky-graphic layout across every topic will converge on one look regardless of theming, because the invariant that reads as "sameness" is layout skeleton and motion signature, not palette.</INFERENCE>

## Detailed Findings

### How do practitioners build single-page, evidence-dense scrollytelling web reports, and what measurably works versus fails?

#### (1) Production patterns and narrative structures

The structural vocabulary comes from two complementary sources: a research taxonomy and a newsroom technique vocabulary.

**The research taxonomy.** Segel & Heer's three narrative structures sit on an author-driven ↔ reader-driven axis. The **martini glass** puts the reader on a narrow authored path (the stem) through introduction, observation and question, then opens to a sandbox where they can slice the data themselves; the freedom is initially very narrow and widens at the end [Segel & Heer, IEEE TVCG, 2010](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/segel-tvcg10.pdf). The **interactive slideshow** admits interaction mid-narrative but confines it within each slide — and each such slide is itself often martini-glass shaped; for complex stories the slideshow format gives audiences a clear roadmap for distinguishing segments within a larger story [Segel & Heer, IEEE TVCG, 2010](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/segel-tvcg10.pdf). The **drill-down** presents a theme and lets the reader pick instances; it is the most reader-driven but still requires the author to decide which sub-sections carry detail [Segel & Heer, IEEE TVCG, 2010](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/segel-tvcg10.pdf). Segel & Heer's own prescription is a blend: provide checkpoints within the narrative while letting the reader digress, but not too far.

The same paper names the visual narrative tactics that are still under-used two decades on: highlighting key data points by colour or size, annotations and labels carrying the insight, **progress indicators showing the reader's position in the story**, consistent layouts across steps, "tacit tutorials" that animate an interactive control to teach its use, stimulating default views, and explicit narrative messaging (intro text and summaries) [Segel & Heer, IEEE TVCG, 2010](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/segel-tvcg10.pdf).

**The newsroom technique vocabulary.** Oesch, Renner & Roth (all then at *Neue Zürcher Zeitung*) analysed 50 scrollytelling articles and grouped "scrollers" into five standard techniques: **graphic sequences, animated transitions, panning and zooming, scrolling through movies, and show-and-autoplay**, with a table mapping each technique to its typical configuration of "scrolly-atoms" [Oesch, Renner & Roth, Information Design Journal 27(1):102-114, 9 Dec 2022, DOI 10.1075/idj.22005.oes, CC BY-NC](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.22005.oes). Their stated motive is production cost: scrollytelling remains costly to produce, and a shared vocabulary lets teams implement scrollers faster. This taxonomy has been adopted by the EU's official Data Visualisation Guide [data.europa.eu, Data Visualisation Guide](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-graphic-sequence).

**The "one idea per scroll state" discipline** is not a research finding so much as a convergent production constraint. Bostock's formulation is that scrolling's strength is linearity — one axis, one track, no branching, the reader's only decision being forward or backward [Bostock, 2014](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/). The Pudding's operational version is blunter: keep it "short and sweet" — a few steps to make the point, then exit, because what feels fine on desktop becomes tiring on a small screen [Goldenberg, The Pudding, Responsive scrollytelling best practices](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/). Mittenentzwei et al. observed the same constraint expressed structurally: their ScrollyVis implementation builds text by scrolling **so that at most one text block is shown at a time**, where the PowerPoint/Unity slideshow variants could display multiple text items simultaneously [Mittenentzwei et al., Computers & Graphics 114:229-238, 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.cag.2023.06.011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849323001061).

**Directly convertible build rules.** From The Pudding's responsive guidance, which is the most operational published source:

| Rule | Source rationale |
|---|---|
| Size steps in **`px`** computed from `window.innerHeight` on load and resize — **never `vh`** | Mobile navbars appear/disappear during scroll, changing viewport height and making triggers janky [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/) |
| Sync JS and CSS breakpoints via `window.matchMedia` (example threshold `min-width: 800px`), store as an `enhanced` flag | Prevents CSS/JS branch divergence [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/) |
| Separate chart **setup** from chart **draw** | Resize handlers update rather than rebuild [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/) |
| No steppers, no swipe/tap advance | They override native scroll and browser behaviour; scrollytelling exists so users can just scroll [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/) |
| No hover-only content — use fixed text or annotations | Stray taps during scrolling cause accidental triggers [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/) |
| Pin the graphic with CSS `position: sticky`, not JS | Scrollama deprecated `.onContainerEnter`/`.onContainerExit` in v2.0.0+ in favour of `position: sticky` [Goldenberg, Scrollama README](https://github.com/russellsamora/scrollama) |
| **Stack instead of scroll** when: heavy transitions would hurt performance; steps read as well standalone; mobile needs a different chart type; or the deadline favours a static image | The test for keeping it scrolly is whether transitions are "truly meaningful, and not just something to make it pop" — change over time or spatial movement qualify [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/) |

Bostock's five rules add the interaction contract: prefer scrolling to clicking; allow rapid, incremental, reversible scrolling; provide instantaneous consistent feedback by **always keeping something moving in direct proportion to scroll input** (don't fix everything in place or the page feels dead); avoid unwanted disruptions such as autoplaying media on scroll-into-view; and support standard keyboard controls — arrows, home/end, page up/down, space and shift-space — without breaking browser history [Bostock, 2014](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/scroll/).

#### (2) Empirical findings on comprehension, recall and engagement

This is where the strongest and most decision-relevant evidence sits, and where the popular claims are weakest.

**Format comparisons repeatedly show engagement gains with null comprehension gains.** The largest direct test found scrollytelling matched plain text, two nutrition-label variants and a standalone interactive visualisation on comprehension accuracy and confidence; one nutrition-label variant did marginally better; cognitive load was lower and engagement, willingness to adopt and perceived clarity were higher than text; and differences in perceived understanding, transparency and trust were **small and statistically inconclusive** (N = 454, between-subjects) [Méndez & Such, CHI 2026, arXiv:2603.04367 cs.HC, submitted 4 Mar 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04367). Notably, the *same visualisation shown without narrative scaffolding performed slightly worse*, which is the paper's strongest argument for narrative framing per se.

A comparative onboarding study found the pooled interactive conditions (scrollytelling and chatbot) produced significantly higher guideline-adherence scores and higher engagement ratings than static onboarding, but **comprehension did not differ significantly across conditions**, and scrollytelling and chatbot did not differ from each other (N = 25 UX designers and students) [A Comparative Study of Static, Scrollytelling, and Chatbot Visualization Onboarding Techniques, arXiv:2607.03023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03023).

Distill's synthesis of the layout literature reports two results that cut against scroll: McKenna et al. found participants preferred step- or scroll-based navigation over static articles with **no significant engagement difference between step and scroll**; and **Zhi et al. found slideshow layouts outperformed vertical scroll layouts on comprehension tasks** [Hohman et al., "Communicating with Interactive Articles", Distill, 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/). Both studies used desktop devices; mobile evidence is thin.

**Where animation measurably helps.** Heer & Robertson's two controlled experiments found animated transitions can significantly improve graphical perception, with significant advantages over static transitions for **object tracking and value estimation**; their key design principle was staging — animating scatter→bar by first moving points to their x-coordinates and updating the axis to form a dot plot, then morphing points into bars, rather than direct interpolation [Heer & Robertson, IEEE TVCG 13(6):1240-7, Nov-Dec 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.70539).

**Where animation measurably hurts.** <CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt's review of the learning literature concluded that where animated graphics appear superior, scrutiny reveals a lack of equivalence — the animated condition conveyed more information or added interactivity. Presenting any graphic beat no instruction, but animated-condition students did not outperform equivalent text or static-graphic conditions; in one case animation students finished training faster but tested slower, and after a week the text students' performance *improved* while the animation students' *declined*, attributed to deeper processing of text [Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt, IJHCS 57(4):247-262, 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf). Separately, Robertson et al.'s work on *trend* animation found animation less efficient than static small multiples for time-series analysis though preferred in a presentation context, with users appearing to find the movement confusing even when guided [contrast noted in the Heer & Robertson literature](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.70539). The disagreement is not about whether animation "works" — it is about the unit being animated. Transitions between two states of one encoding are supported; animation as an explanatory medium in place of static graphics is not.</CONFLICTING_EVIDENCE>

**Immersion and 3D specifically.** Kim et al. built four data stories in three variants — static (flat, scrolls as a whole), animated (dynamic layout, planar) and immersive (adds depth cues, cinematic camera, realism) — and ran a within-subjects crowdsourced study rating interest, ease of understanding, persuasiveness, trustworthiness and curiosity. Immersive variants were rated **more interesting and more persuasive**, but **no condition was judged more understandable or more trustworthy** [Kim, Park, Song, Lee, Lee, Rossi, Hoffswell, Koh & Lee, arXiv:2411.18049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18049). Their six identified patterns — cinematic camera shots and transitions, intuitive data representations, realism, naturally moving elements, direct manipulation of camera or visualisation, and dynamic dimension — are the closest thing to a published pattern library for 3D on narrative pages.

**Related mechanisms with positive evidence.** Kim et al. (separate work, via Distill) found visualisation-based prompts effectively trigger self-explanation and improve recall; showing other readers' guesses (social comparison, as in NYT "You Draw It") was **not** shown to improve recall [Distill, 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/). Annotations enhance comprehension, memorability and recall, with clear headlines and colour highlights improving memory recall by reducing visual clutter [A Survey on Annotations in Information Visualization, arXiv:2410.05579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05579). And the underlying narrative-advantage assumption is well supported: a meta-analysis across 75+ samples and 33,000+ participants found stories more easily understood and better recalled than essays, robust to single-study exclusion [Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts: a meta-analysis, PMC8219577](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8219577/).

**What readers actually do.** Chartbeat's analysis of Slate found that of every 161 arrivals ~61 (38%) left immediately; of the 100 engaged readers ~5 never scrolled a pixel (~10% web-wide); **median scroll depth was ~50%** on Slate and ~60% web-wide; and only 25% got past the 1,600th pixel of a ~2,000-pixel article [Manjoo, Slate, Jun 2013, with Chartbeat data](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html). Critically for anyone tempted to treat shares as a proxy for reading: the relationship between scroll depth and sharing was **only very weak**, both at Slate and web-wide [Manjoo/Chartbeat, 2013](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html). Roughly two-thirds of time on page is spent below the fold [Chartbeat via Slate, 2013](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html).

<INFERENCE from="median scroll depth ~50-60%; ~38% immediate bounce; the martini-glass structure; and Segel & Heer's 'narrative messaging' tactic">The load-bearing conclusion of an evidence-dense report page must be legible above the fold or in the first two scroll states. A structure that withholds the finding until the reader has traversed twelve steps is designed for the ~25% who reach the 80% mark.</INFERENCE>

#### (3) Citation, provenance and trust UX

**The theoretical case is strong; the measured effects are ambiguous.** Hyperlinks are understood as a transparent sourcing mechanism giving direct access to raw source material and revealing otherwise covert news-sourcing practice [Finding the news and mapping the links, Information, Communication & Society, 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2018.1477971). Experimental studies have found positive effects of source-transparency elements such as hyperlinks on perceived news credibility (Borah 2014; Curry & Stroud 2021; Karlsson, Clerwall & Nord 2014) [Designing Transparency Cues in Online News Platforms, arXiv:2108.02325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.02325).

But: one study found a single transparency feature had almost no effect on credibility perceptions, while a more recent study found **multiple transparency indicators together** can improve them, and consumers may prefer hyperlinked source material over explanations of framing [arXiv:2108.02325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.02325). The attention problem is the sharpest result: in Koliska's experimental work, only **34%** of participants could correctly identify the journalist, **53%** recalled seeing an editorial explanation but only **26%** could recall a detail from it [arXiv:2108.02325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.02325).

**Practice systematically undercuts the mechanism.** Content analyses reveal limited hyperlink use, with most links pointing to pages on the same website and few to primary sources or background — and "pseudo-external" links pointing to other sites within the same media conglomerate reflect protectionist linking strategies running counter to source-transparency ideals [Information, Communication & Society, 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2018.1477971). Link rot is a documented durability problem, with data journalists reporting that site migrations routinely produce broken links [Evidence and transparency in Open Data Journalism](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/cpo/article/1880/galley/1840/download/).

**The one strong quantitative result on verification affordances** comes from Wikipedia. Page Previews — a card appearing after a hover of more than 500 ms — were explicitly designed to *reduce the cost of exploring a link*. The 2016 A/B tests across Hungarian, Italian and Russian Wikipedias measured average clickthrough of **0.079** for the ON groups; intentional card views (shown ≥1 second without clickthrough or dismissal) of **0.28 / 0.30 / 0.43 per pageview** respectively; and a **31% increase in overall page interactions** in the ON group versus control, taken as confirmation that previews lower the cost of exploring a link [Wikimedia, Page Previews 2016 A/B Tests](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Page_Previews/2016_A/B_Tests). The opt-out rate was very low: settings-cog clicks occurred in **0.02% / 0.034% / 0.016%** of sessions [Wikimedia, 2016](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Page_Previews/2016_A/B_Tests). Qualitatively, 79% agreed previews were easy to use and 76% that they were useful [Wikimedia, 2016](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Page_Previews/2016_A/B_Tests). The 2017–18 English/German follow-up projected over 50 million previews seen per day at 0.26 previews per pageview [Wikimedia, Page Previews 2017-18 A/B Tests](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Page_Previews/2017-18_A/B_Tests).

<INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>Wikipedia's Page Previews metrics cover *article link* previews, not *citation/reference* previews. Wikimedia's "Reference Previews" is a distinct feature and no comparable A/B result for it surfaced. Transferring the 31% figure to citation hovercards is not warranted.</INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>

**Design rules that follow.** Details-on-demand, anchored in Shneiderman's mantra ("Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand"), is the established pattern family, realised as tooltips, expandable StretchText-style prose, and hover preview cards [Distill, 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/). But Tse's rule constrains it: *if you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it — if content is important for readers to see, don't hide it* [Tse, "Why We Are Doing Fewer Interactives", Malofiej 24, 2016](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/blob/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf). <INFERENCE from="Koliska's 26% recall of transparency-feature detail; Tse's rule that hidden content is unseen content; the finding that multiple transparency indicators outperform one; and the documented dominance of same-site links">For an evidence-dense report page the defensible citation architecture is redundant rather than clever: the claim carries a visible inline marker, the marker opens a preview containing source name and date without navigation, *and* the same source appears in a persistent end-of-page registry with a live URL. Any design where the source exists only inside a hover state has, by Tse's rule, no sources.</INFERENCE>

#### (4) GSAP ScrollTrigger and three.js/WebGL production practice

**The externally fixed budget.** LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms, CLS < 0.1 for "good"; "needs improvement" bands are LCP 2.5–4.0 s, INP 200–500 ms, CLS 0.1–0.25 [Google Search Central, Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals). Evaluation is at the **75th percentile of Chrome UX Report field data over a rolling 28-day window**, measured separately per device class, and all three must pass — two-of-three is a fail [Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals). The 200 ms INP threshold was set by taking the 100 ms figure supported by quality-of-experience research and adjusting for achievability, with extra attention paid to lower-end mobile devices [web.dev, How the Core Web Vitals metrics thresholds were defined](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds). INP replaced FID on **12 March 2024** and, unlike FID, reports the worst interaction across the whole visit; it is a pure field metric that Lighthouse cannot measure, though Total Blocking Time correlates [Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals).

**The main-thread question.** Browsers perform scrolling on a separate process and deliver scroll events asynchronously, while main-thread animations are subject to jank — which is why performant scroll-synced animation was historically very difficult or impossible with the classic JS approach. From Chrome 115, Scroll Timelines and View Timelines integrate with WAAPI and CSS Animations and can run **off the main thread**, with no scroll listener and no IntersectionObserver [Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/scroll-driven-animations). The off-main-thread benefit applies to compositor-friendly properties — transform and opacity.

**GSAP's own position is that ScrollTrigger is cheap by design, with specific named hazards.** The docs state it "ONLY watches the scroll position. Period," debounces scroll events, synchronises updates with `requestAnimationFrame`, and throttles resize recalculation until there is a 200 ms gap in resize events, because recalculating is CPU-intensive [GSAP, ScrollTrigger docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/). Start/end values are static and are **not** constantly recalculated; animating a trigger element will not update positions, and `ScrollTrigger.refresh()` is required [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/).

The explicit warnings, all directly convertible into lint rules:

| GSAP-documented hazard | Rule |
|---|---|
| "Don't animate the pinned element itself because that will throw off the measurements" | Animate nested children only [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| Pinning uses `position: fixed` deliberately — transforms caused "annoying visual glitches" because scroll repaints are handled on a different thread | Do not substitute transform-based pinning [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| Ancestor `transform` / `will-change` breaks `position: fixed` — "a browser thing, not a ScrollTrigger thing" | Never place `will-change` on an ancestor of a pinned section [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| `pinReparent: true` is a last resort — "reparenting can be expensive" | Avoid [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| CSS `content-visibility: auto` / `hidden` prevents correct start/end calculation | Exclude from scrollytelling sections [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| Under `scrub`, child tween durations are **proportions of scroll distance**, not real time | To slow an animation, extend `end: "+=4000"`, never the durations [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| Creation order matters, because pinning distance affects later triggers' start/end | Create top-to-bottom; `refreshPriority` is "VERY unlikely" to be needed if you do [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| One ScrollTrigger controls one timeline (recommended); nesting ScrollTriggers in child tweens is discouraged | "It wouldn't be good to have multiple things trying to control the same animation's playhead" [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| `once: true` kills the instance, stops scroll listening, makes it garbage-collectable | Use for one-shot reveals [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| Pin flash on fast scroll | `anticipatePin: 1`, though "in many cases you don't need any" (default 0) [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |
| Overlapping animations from fast scrolling | `fastScrollEnd` (default threshold 2500 px/s) or `preventOverlaps` [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) |

Note the deliberate architectural divergence: ScrollTrigger does **not** use IntersectionObserver, which "constantly 'watches' elements" and "lacks the necessary functionality and compatibility" for scrubbing [GSAP docs](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/) — whereas Scrollama is built precisely on IntersectionObserver "in favor of scroll events" for performance [Scrollama README](https://github.com/russellsamora/scrollama). These are not competing claims about the same job: Scrollama triggers discrete steps; ScrollTrigger additionally scrubs continuous progress.

**CSS scroll-driven animations: support and gotchas.** Two timeline types — Scroll Progress Timeline (scroll container position along an axis) and View Progress Timeline (element position within its scroll container) [MDN, CSS scroll-driven animations](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Scroll-driven_animations). Available in all major browsers except Firefox, where it is fully implemented behind a flag and enabled by default in Nightly; Safari support arrived in Safari 26 [WebKit, A guide to scroll-driven animations with just CSS](https://webkit.org/blog/17101/a-guide-to-scroll-driven-animations-with-just-css/). Three failure modes worth encoding as lint rules: `animation-timeline` is a reset-only value in the `animation` shorthand, so it must be declared **after** the shorthand; `animation-duration` is ignored under a `scroll()` timeline; and `animation-fill-mode: both` is required or the animation snaps back on scroll-up [MDN, animation-timeline](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/Properties/animation-timeline). Progressive enhancement via `@supports (animation-timeline: scroll())` or `CSS.supports('animation-timeline', 'scroll()')` [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Scroll-driven_animations).

**three.js / WebGL.** The controlling constraint is draw calls, not triangles: each `Mesh` produces one draw call per frame, `InstancedMesh` renders all instances of a geometry in a single draw call with per-instance matrices in an `InstancedBufferAttribute`, and geometry merging and texture atlasing cut binds; culling of off-camera objects is automatic and free [three.js docs, InstancedMesh](https://threejs.org/docs/#api/en/objects/InstancedMesh). Two production-specific killers named repeatedly: raycasting against all objects on every mouse move, and failing to `dispose()` removed objects so GPU memory grows linearly until the tab crashes — both survive because they work fine in short-session demos [Three.js Journey, Performance tips](https://threejs-journey.com/lessons/performance-tips). Texture memory is the other lever: KTX2/Basis textures stay compressed on the GPU where PNG and JPEG decompress fully into VRAM [three.js docs, KTX2Loader](https://threejs.org/docs/#examples/en/loaders/KTX2Loader).

<CONFIDENCE:LOW>The commonly cited figure of ~0.1 ms CPU per draw call on mobile GPUs — which yields the practical budget of ≤100 draw calls in a 16.67 ms frame — appears only in commercial optimisation blogs `[SECONDARY: promotional]` and could not be traced to a published benchmark. Treat it as a rule of thumb to be re-derived on target hardware, not as a measured constant.</CONFIDENCE:LOW>

**Progressive enhancement is the only defensible architecture.** 14islands describe a tiered production implementation: a shared global WebGL canvas persisting between page loads, layered from JS-enabled-but-no-WebGL (richer hover states, a dark showreel mode) up to the full WebGL experience. Their honest caveat is the important one: **some browsers claim WebGL support but are too weak to run it well, and there is no robust way to feature-detect powerful devices** — frame-rate measurement and feature combinations all have flaws — so they infer capability from screen size [14islands, Progressive Enhancement with WebGL and React](https://14islands.com/blog/progressive-enhancement-with-webgl-and-react). The architectural principle is that the page's HTML content renders and is crawlable independently, with the 3D layer applied after the critical path.

**<INFERENCE from="Kim et al.'s finding that immersion raised interest and persuasiveness but not understandability or trustworthiness; Tversky et al.'s confound finding; The Pudding's meaningful-transition test; and 14islands' finding that device capability cannot be reliably detected">The test for whether a 3D scene earns its cost has three conjunctive conditions. (a) The subject has a genuine spatial or volumetric structure that a 2D encoding loses — physical geometry, camera position, depth ordering, or a third continuous dimension that carries the argument. #### (5) Accessibility for scroll-driven and animated content

**The criteria that actually apply, with their conformance levels:**

| Criterion | Level | What it covers here |
|---|---|---|
| **2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide** | **A** | Moving/blinking/scrolling content that starts automatically, lasts >5 s, and is presented in parallel with other content must have a pause, stop or hide mechanism unless essential [W3C](https://w3c.github.io/wcag21/understanding/pause-stop-hide.html) |
| **2.3.3 Animation from Interactions** | **AAA** | Motion animation triggered by interaction must be disableable unless essential — explicitly including **parallax effects when scrolling**, where foreground and background move at different speeds or directions [Deque University, WCAG 2.1 SC 2.3.3](https://dequeuniversity.com/resources/wcag2.1/2.3.3-animations-from-interactions) |
| **2.3.1 Three Flashes** | A | Distinct from 2.2.2; covers seizure-inducing flash rather than distraction [Silktide](https://silktide.com/accessibility-guide/the-wcag-standard/2-2/enough-time/2-2-2-pause-stop-hide/) |
| **1.4.13 Content on Hover or Focus** | AA | Hover-revealed content must be hoverable, dismissible and persistent — the criterion that hover-only tooltips fail |
| **2.1.1 Keyboard** | A | Every mouse-reachable action must be keyboard-reachable, including tooltips, filters and legend items |

**Legal weight is asymmetric and matters for a build spec.** 2.2.2 at Level A is mandatory for ADA Title II and Title III, Section 508, EN 301 549 and the European Accessibility Act [TestParty, WCAG 2.2.2 guide](https://testparty.ai/blog/wcag-2-2-2-pause-stop-hide-2025-guide). 2.3.3 at Level AAA is not required for baseline ADA Title III compliance, which typically references Level AA, but is increasingly expected in government, healthcare and education procurement [TestParty, WCAG 2.3.3 guide](https://testparty.ai/blog/wcag-2-3-3-animation-from-interactions-2025-guide).

**The vestibular case.** W3C's own technique notes that if scrolling a page causes elements to move — other than the essential movement associated with scrolling the content, which is under the user's control — it can trigger vestibular disorders [W3C, Technique SCR40](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/client-side-script/SCR40). Affected readers may experience dizziness, nausea, disorientation, migraine or motion sickness, and even subtle parallax or bouncing can be debilitating [Deque University](https://dequeuniversity.com/resources/wcag2.1/2.3.3-animations-from-interactions). <CONFIDENCE:LOW>A widely repeated figure holds that vestibular disorders affect roughly 35% of adults over 40; this circulates through accessibility guides without a traceable epidemiological citation in the sources reviewed, and should not be used as a load-bearing number.</CONFIDENCE:LOW>

**Implementation architecture.** Two approaches exist — motion-first (full animation by default, disabled under `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)`) and **reduced-first/defensive** (static baseline, motion added only under `no-preference`). Reduced-first *fails safely*: an older browser that does not support the query simply ignores the `no-preference` block and leaves the static baseline [Deque University](https://dequeuniversity.com/resources/wcag2.1/2.3.3-animations-from-interactions). For JS-driven animation, W3C Technique **SCR40** covers evaluating the media query in JavaScript [W3C, SCR40](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/client-side-script/SCR40). The preference is set at OS level, not browser level, and browsers relay it. A site-level toggle is a valid supplement, but however motion is reduced it must not prevent access to content.

**A subtlety that catches teams:** a pause control that only appears on hover or focus fails, because users who cannot use a mouse precisely, or who navigate by voice, may never trigger it — controls must be persistently visible or announced to assistive technology [Silktide, 2.2.2](https://silktide.com/accessibility-guide/the-wcag-standard/2-2/enough-time/2-2-2-pause-stop-hide/).

**Screen-reader and keyboard access to scroll-triggered content.** The controlling requirement is that narrative meaning carried by visual "steps" must survive linearisation: provide a logical reading order presenting title, summary, key trends, then detailed data [The A11Y Collective, accessible charts checklist](https://www.a11y-collective.com/blog/accessible-charts/). The established two-tier text alternative is a concise alt-text stating chart type and primary conclusion, plus a structured data table ordered thoughtfully rather than dumped; Amy Cesal's widely used alt-text recipe has four elements — chart type, data presented, main conclusion, and a link to the full dataset [Flourish, accessible visualisation descriptions](https://flourish.studio/blog/accessible-visualization-screenreader-description/). Redundant encoding (colour *and* line style) with keyboard-navigable legend items serves a diverse audience **without a separate "accessible" version** — the principle worth adopting rather than shipping a stripped-down fallback page [A11Y Collective](https://www.a11y-collective.com/blog/accessible-charts/).

Newsroom practice has moved here recently. BBC journalist Johny Cassidy's 2022 Reuters Institute fellowship found blind audiences were missing crucial COVID-19 health information presented visually; the output was new BBC alt-text guidance, published externally and used to train **nearly 800 staff journalists** through workshops, with reportedly positive uptake despite initial workload concerns [Cassidy, Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism, 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/blind-news-audiences-are-being-left-behind-data-visualisation-revolution-heres-how-we-fix). The same work notes The New York Times created an Accessibility Visuals Editor post [Reuters Institute, 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/blind-news-audiences-are-being-left-behind-data-visualisation-revolution-heres-how-we-fix).

#### (6) Documented failure modes

**Scrolljacking.** NN/g's qualitative testing across multiple industries found the majority of participants experienced at least mild disorientation, with some reading the effect as a broken page or a bug; on a fully scrolljacked page **every participant who visited was disoriented**, one "got lost", could not follow the story, and called backtracking "a hassle" — users re-scrolled repeatedly, switched to dragging the scrollbar, refreshed, or abandoned [NN/g, Scrolljacking 101, 6 Aug 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/). Short scrolljacks preserved orientation but produced an *illusion of completeness* — users assumed the page had ended [NN/g, 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/). Task-oriented users were the most annoyed; **mobile amplified every problem**; sticky navigation acted as an escape hatch [NN/g, 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/). NN/g's seven recommendations: only scrolljack to progressively disclose genuinely valuable information; test and tune the scroll rate; mix normal-scrolling sections in; **don't change scroll direction**; **keep text out of scrolljacked sections**; skip scrolljacking on mobile; and if used at all, place it below the fold [NN/g, 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/). The concluding verdict is that the pattern "can do more harm than good" and remains risky even when executed carefully.

Note the definitional line that matters for a build spec: **scrollytelling monitors scroll and changes content; scrolljacking manipulates the browser's scroll mechanics** — the first is the pattern, the second is the anti-pattern, and they are routinely conflated [Goldenberg, The Pudding, How to implement scrollytelling with six different libraries](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-implement-scrollytelling/).

**Mobile performance collapse.** The most-cited engineering observation is Tse's: *getting it to work on all platforms is expensive*, and *you need to redesign your graphic at least 2 or 3 times to make it work on both desktop and mobile* [Tse, Malofiej 24, 2016](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/blob/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf). Contentsquare's cross-web benchmarks report desktop scroll rates averaging 50.5% and mobile 45.2%, with both declining year over year `Contentsquare benchmark (unusable citation URL)`. <CONFIDENCE:LOW>A commonly repeated pass-rate statistic holds that only 48% of mobile and 56% of desktop sites achieve "good" on all three Core Web Vitals per the 2025 Web Almanac; this was relayed second-hand and not verified against the Almanac chapter.[almanac.httparchive.org](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/performance)</CONFIDENCE:LOW>

**Unlabelled or misleading charts.** Pandey et al. tested four distortion techniques — truncated axis (bar), area as quantity (bubble), aspect ratio (line) and inverted axis — across 330 unique participants in the final study. Participants exposed to deceptive visualisations reported interpretations **58.5% to 129.5% larger** than controls; for message reversal via inverted axis, **97.5% of participants in the deceptive group responded incorrectly** [Pandey et al., "How Deceptive are Deceptive Visualizations?", CHI 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/300726103_How_Deceptive_are_Deceptive_Visualizations). Chart familiarity, visual ability and education produced no conclusive protective correlation, with substantial confidence-interval overlap [Pandey et al., CHI 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/300726103_How_Deceptive_are_Deceptive_Visualizations) — meaning reader sophistication cannot be relied on as a mitigation, and axis integrity must be enforced at generation time.

**Visual homogeneity and template sameness.** Goree et al. measured this directly across 2003–2019: web design became *less* homogeneous 2003–2007, then dramatically more so, in both colour and spatial layout, with **layout similarity distance declining 44% from 2010–2019** [Goree, Doosti, Crandall & Su, CHI '21, DOI 10.1145/3411764.3445156](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445156). Their identified cause is expanded use of a small number of frameworks and libraries, with library-sharing strongly correlated to visual similarity; and their warning is forward-looking — these changes may **constrain the perceived repertoire of legitimate designs available to future designers** [Goree et al., CHI '21](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445156). Their qualitative arm interviewed 11 designers with ~15+ years' experience, deliberately avoiding direct questions about homogenisation to prevent confirmation bias [Goree et al., CHI '21](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445156).

**Claims outrunning sources.** This failure mode has weaker direct measurement than the others, but three findings bound it. First, content analyses show most links point to the same website with few to primary sources [Information, Communication & Society, 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2018.1477971). Second, link rot degrades whatever provenance exists [Evidence and transparency in Open Data Journalism](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/cpo/article/1880/galley/1840/download/). Third, the design principle emerging from recent work is that users should be able to trace each fact to its original source, with **every fact displayed alongside its citation and publication year** [arXiv:2108.02325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.02325).

**Fragility as a failure mode.** Distill flags web fragility as a threat to longevity of interactive articles, noting The Parametric Press publishes downloadable WARC archives per article as mitigation — and that accessibility remains unsolved for mixed audio/video/text formats [Distill, 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/). For a self-contained single HTML page this is an argument in favour of the architecture, not against it.

### What is the current state, and what is the strongest supporting evidence for it?

The current state is a mature production practice with a **thin and largely null empirical base for its central claim**. Practitioners have converged on: sticky-graphic + scrolling-text as the layout primitive, `position: sticky` rather than JS pinning, IntersectionObserver for discrete steps and scrubbed timelines for continuous ones, px-based step heights, and a mobile fallback that either preserves scroll or stacks. The strongest evidence for the practice is Segel & Heer's taxonomy (2010, still the reference frame for every subsequent study) [Segel & Heer, TVCG 2010](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/7450/Papers/segel-tvcg10.pdf), Oesch et al.'s technique vocabulary from 50 real newsroom articles [Oesch, Renner & Roth, IDJ 2022](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.22005.oes), and Heer & Robertson's controlled experiments on staged transitions [Heer & Robertson, TVCG 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.70539).

The strongest evidence *against* over-claiming is the consistent pattern of null comprehension results across independent studies with different populations and materials: privacy policies (N=454) [Méndez & Such, CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04367), UX onboarding (N=25) [arXiv:2607.03023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03023), spatial immersion (within-subjects crowdsourced) [Kim et al., arXiv:2411.18049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18049), and Zhi et al.'s finding that slideshows *beat* scroll on comprehension [via Distill, 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/).

### What changed recently, and what is the trajectory?

Four shifts since 2023 change the correct build defaults:

1. **Scroll animation moved into the platform.** CSS scroll-driven animations shipped in Chrome 115 and reached Safari 26; support sits around 85% with Firefox implemented behind a flag [WebKit, Safari 26](https://webkit.org/blog/17101/a-guide-to-scroll-driven-animations-with-just-css/). The off-main-thread property is a genuine architectural change, not an incremental one [Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/scroll-driven-animations).

2. **INP replaced FID on 12 March 2024**, changing the measured object from first-interaction delay to worst-interaction latency across the whole visit [Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals). A scrollytelling page's *last* step is now as load-bearing as its first.

3. **GSAP became free including commercially in April 2025**, with formerly Club-only plugins including ScrollTrigger and SplitText included at no cost, following Webflow's acquisition of GreenSock [GSAP pricing/licence](https://gsap.com/pricing/). This removes a cost argument against ScrollTrigger but not the main-thread argument.

4. **Accessibility moved from advocacy to newsroom process** — BBC alt-text guidance training ~800 journalists, an NYT Accessibility Visuals Editor role [Reuters Institute, 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/blind-news-audiences-are-being-left-behind-data-visualisation-revolution-heres-how-we-fix) — while the EU's Data Visualisation Guide formalised Oesch et al.'s scroller vocabulary as public guidance [data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-graphic-sequence).

**Trajectory.** <INFERENCE from="the off-main-thread property of CSS scroll timelines; the INP measurement change; GSAP's documented main-thread architecture and its explicit non-use of IntersectionObserver; and the 85% support figure">The stable equilibrium is a three-tier motion stack rather than one library: CSS `animation-timeline` for compositor-friendly reveals (off main thread, no JS, degrades to static under `@supports`), IntersectionObserver/Scrollama for discrete step triggering, and GSAP ScrollTrigger reserved for scrubbed sequencing and pinning that CSS cannot express. A generator that reaches for GSAP by default is buying main-thread cost it does not need for the majority of its effects — but a generator that abandons GSAP loses scrub, which is the technique with the strongest comprehension evidence behind it.</INFERENCE>

**Newsroom accessibility standards for scroll-triggered content.** Alt-text standards are published; the harder question — how a screen-reader user should experience a *sequence* of scroll-triggered states — has no published newsroom standard.

**Sources discarded rather than caveated.** Per the source-discipline requirement, the following were reviewed and excluded from the synthesis: a large set of SEO-optimised "best scrollytelling libraries 2026" and "best 3D websites" listicles, no-code platform marketing (Shorthand, Wix Studio, Lovable, scrollytelling.ai), hosting-vendor performance blogs, and Medium reposts of the Segel & Heer paper. Where a promotional source supplied the *only* instance of a load-bearing number, it is retained with a `[SECONDARY: promotional]` label and a LOW confidence tag rather than silently absorbed.

**Interpretive caution on trust.** No source reviewed measured whether readers of an evidence-dense page *actually verified* a claim by following a citation. The Wikipedia data measures link-exploration cost, not verification behaviour; the transparency literature measures perceived credibility, not checking. <INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE>The claim "good citation UX makes readers verify claims" could not be corroborated in either direction. ## Recommended Next Steps

1. **Run the missing motion benchmark before fixing the skill's default stack.** Build one representative report page in three implementations — CSS `animation-timeline`, IntersectionObserver-triggered, and GSAP ScrollTrigger with scrub — and measure INP, long-task count and frame drops on mid-tier Android under CPU throttling. *Rationale:* this is the one decision in the whole build spec where the practitioner literature actively contradicts itself and no primary measurement exists; every other rule in this report can be adopted on published evidence, and this one cannot. The result determines whether GSAP is the default motion layer or the escape hatch.

2. **Retrieve the three papers whose numbers were withheld** — Mittenentzwei et al. (full text), Méndez & Such (full PDF), and the Springer scrolljacking chapter. *Rationale:* Mittenentzwei is the only controlled genre comparison specifically on scroll-vs-slideshow navigation; Méndez & Such is the largest format comparison and the effect sizes determine how strongly the "engagement not comprehension" rule should be stated; the Springer study is the only quantitative test of NN/g's qualitative scrolljacking findings.

3. **Instrument the first published pages with step-level scroll telemetry and publish the funnel.** *Rationale:* the field's central empirical gap is that nobody publishes per-step completion. A producer of many topic pages is unusually well-positioned to close it, and the data directly answers the operational question — how many scroll states a report can carry before the argument stops arriving — which no current source answers. It also converts the ~50% median-scroll-depth figure from a 2013 web-wide average into a figure for this specific format.

4. **Build the sameness detector before building more pages.** Goree et al. measured homogenisation computationally on colour and spatial layout across ~2M pairwise comparisons [Goree et al., CHI '21](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445156); the same method run over the skill's own output corpus gives a quantitative sameness score per new page against all previous ones. *Rationale:* "every page must look genuinely different" is currently an unfalsifiable design goal. Goree's method makes it a gate. Measure layout skeleton and motion signature, not just palette — the evidence says layout is where convergence happens.

5. **Establish the citation-verification baseline the literature lacks.** A small comparative study — inline marker only, marker + hover preview, marker + preview + persistent registry — measuring actual citation follow-through, not perceived credibility. *Rationale:* the trust UX section of this report rests on a mechanism (lower exploration cost) and a proxy (perceived credibility), with the actual behaviour unmeasured in every source reviewed. For a format whose entire premise is evidence density, this is the load-bearing unknown, and it is cheap to test.
