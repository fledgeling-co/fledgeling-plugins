# Automated Design and UI Review Systems Combining Deterministic Browser Gates with Model Judgement: Evidence, Failure Modes, and Design Implications

Automated review of rendered web user interfaces sits at the intersection of deterministic browser-based measurement, rule engines derived from accessibility standards, and increasingly, model-based judgement about visual and design quality. When these components are combined into a single skill or agent that must make consequential decisions about UI quality, the reliability of every layer, and the interactions between them, become critical. Drawing on current standards such as WCAG 2.1, emerging work around WCAG 3 and APCA, formal test rules like ACT, and empirical studies of automated testing coverage, this report synthesizes what is known about computation of contrast over complex backdrops, reliability envelopes of automated accessibility tools, taxonomies for test outcomes, headless browser divergence, layout verification via DOM geometry, and early evidence on large language models as visual judges. It then uses that evidence to recommend a conservative contrast algorithm and backdrop policy, a three-state result taxonomy with explicit coverage handling, a limited set of layout checks that survive empirical scrutiny, cautious use of model scoring, and severity and restraint structures designed to prevent inflated issue counts and fabricated observations. The emphasis throughout is on what a checker may legitimately assert versus what it merely computed, and on failure modes that are silent because a broken check and a clean surface look identical.

## Automated Visual UI Review Systems: Architecture and Decision Context

### Combined Deterministic and Model-Based Review: Why the Architecture Matters

Automated design and UI review systems that operate on rendered web content typically combine at least three layers of logic. The first layer is the browser or browser engine, which is responsible for applying styles, computing layout, resolving media queries, and rendering the page to a composited visual surface. The second layer is a deterministic checking system that inspects the DOM, CSSOM, and sometimes pixel output to assess conformance with rules derived from standards such as WCAG 2.1 and ACT rules published by W3C, including rules about contrast, semantics, keyboard operability, and other aspects of accessibility.[7][14] The third layer is a model-based judgement system, often an LLM or multimodal model, which takes structured measurements, screenshots, or summaries and provides higher-level assessments of visual quality, alignment with design systems, or subjective usability.

In such a stack, the deterministic browser gates are the only part that can be meaningfully aligned with normative standards and formal conformance claims. WCAG 2.x success criteria are explicitly written as testable statements that are not technology-specific, and ACT Test Rules provide guidance to automated tool developers to interpret those success criteria consistently.[7][14] The model component, by contrast, operates in a domain of soft judgements that may support design decisions or triage but cannot be equated with conformance or non-conformance. Confidence in conclusions is therefore heavily dependent on clear boundaries between what is asserted as a standard violation and what is offered as heuristic judgement.

The decision context for this report is sharply practical: the findings will directly influence how a new agent skill is rebuilt. That skill must orchestrate browser rendering, deterministic measurements, and model review over an arbitrary web UI. It must choose a contrast algorithm and policy for unresolvable backdrops, define a three-state taxonomy of pass, fail, and could-not-run for checks, decide which layout checks are safe to keep given over-fire rates, determine whether model judgement should be explicitly scored, and design severity structures that prevent inflated issue counts and fabricated observations. The analysis therefore focuses on evidence-backed techniques and documented failure modes rather than broad theoretical possibilities.

### Standards, Rules, and the Scope of Deterministic Checking

The normative foundation for deterministic checking is WCAG 2.1 and its success criteria.[14] WCAG 2.1 organizes accessibility requirements under four principles—perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust—and defines success criteria at Levels A, AA, and AAA that are written as testable statements.[14] For example, Success Criterion 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) requires that the visual presentation of text and images of text have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1, with exceptions for large text, incidental text, and logotypes.[1][14] WCAG defines relative luminance and the contrast ratio formula as \((L_1 + 0.05) / (L_2 + 0.05)\), where \(L_1\) is the luminance of the lighter color and \(L_2\) is that of the darker color, producing contrast ratios from 1:1 to 21:1.[14]

ACT Test Rules, developed by the W3C Accessibility Conformance Testing community, are informative, not normative, but serve as detailed guidance for specific failure states that automated tools can test.[7] They define atomic and composite rules, specify applicability conditions, and list expected outcomes corresponding to failures of particular success criteria.[7] For example, the proposed rule "Text has enhanced contrast" checks that the highest possible contrast of each text character with its background meets enhanced contrast requirements, mapping to Success Criterion 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced).[3][12] ACT documentation explicitly warns that passing a rule means only that no corresponding failures were detected, not that all aspects of a success criterion are satisfied.[7] This distinction is central to the reliability envelope discussion that follows.

The standards and ACT rules also delimit what deterministic checks can and cannot do. Many success criteria involve subjective interpretation, user experience dynamics, or multi-step interaction patterns that automated tools cannot reliably trace. W3C's explanation of Test Rules notes that checking all aspects of a success criterion typically requires further human verification, highlighting inherent limitations of automation.[7] This has implications for how an agent skill should report coverage and what denominator it uses when claiming that a page "passes" a set of checks.

### Model-Based Judgement: A Different Epistemic Regime

Large language models and multimodal models introduce a qualitatively different type of reasoning. They are not executing normative standards but approximating human-like assessments based on training data and prompts. Their judgements on visual design quality, layout aesthetics, or readability are inherently probabilistic and influenced by prompt structure, context window, sampling parameters, and prior exposure to similar examples. They can, however, ingest structured outputs of deterministic checks, screenshots, and textual descriptions to synthesize higher-level views, identify patterns such as inconsistent spacing, or flag potential issues not captured by rule engines.

From the perspective of conformance claims, model-based assessments should be treated as advisory rather than authoritative. They are particularly vulnerable to position and verbosity biases, where content mentioned early or in detail in the prompt is weighted more heavily, and to hallucination, where the model asserts observations it did not measure. In the agent skill under consideration, model-derived output must be explicitly separated from deterministic violations, both in data structures and in user-facing messaging. Severity taxonomies and restraint mechanisms become critical in keeping model suggestions from bloating issue counts or overshadowing reliable measurements.

With this architectural context in place, the report now turns to the seven subtopics requested, each tied back to decisions about contrast algorithms, result taxonomies, layout checks, model scoring, and restraint structures.

## Computing Text–Background Contrast Over Complex Backdrops

### WCAG 2.x Contrast Requirements and the Basic Algorithm

WCAG 2.1 defines contrast for text as a measure of the difference in perceived luminance between foreground and background colors.[14] The specification introduces relative luminance, calculated from sRGB values, and a contrast ratio formula \((L_1 + 0.05) / (L_2 + 0.05)\) that yields values between 1:1, as in white on white, and 21:1, as in black on white.[14] For normal-sized text and images of text at Level AA, Success Criterion 1.4.3 requires at least 4.5:1 contrast, with large text (18 pt or 14 pt bold) permitted at 3:1.[1][14] Level AAA’s Success Criterion 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced) raises these requirements to 7:1 for normal text and 4.5:1 for large text.[1][3]

WebAIM’s article "Understanding WCAG 2 Contrast and Color Requirements" provides practical context for these numbers, noting specific examples such as pure red text (#FF0000) on white achieving a 4:1 ratio, pure green (#00FF00) at 1.4:1, and pure blue (#0000FF) at 8.6:1.[1] It emphasizes that swapping text and background colors does not change the contrast ratio, and highlights that a common shade of gray, #777777, has a 4.47:1 contrast ratio that does not meet the 4.5:1 requirement.[1] This kind of nuance underscores the need for precise computation rather than rounded estimates. WebAIM also discusses exceptions for incidental text and logotypes and notes that text effects, such as outlines, can affect perceived contrast; WCAG allows the outline or border color to be used as the text color when measuring contrast.[1]

Crucially, WCAG itself defines contrast between text and its background, but the introduction of Success Criterion 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast expands requirements for user interface components and graphical objects, mandating at least 3:1 contrast against adjacent colors.[1][14] This means non-text elements may require measuring contrast in multiple places, such as different sides of a wedge in a pie chart, and may involve components with distinct colors that must achieve 3:1 contrast with each other.[1] For an agent skill concerned primarily with text contrast, 1.4.3 and 1.4.6 set the core numeric thresholds, while 1.4.11 influences how non-text checks might be extended.

### Complex Backdrops: Gradients, Images, and Translucent Layers in WCAG Guidance

WCAG 2.x itself does not explicitly define how to handle text drawn over gradients, semi-transparent colors, or background images when computing contrast. WebAIM notes that text over such backdrops still needs to meet contrast requirements, but WCAG does not offer concrete methods for measurement in these cases.[1] Their recommendation is pragmatic: usually test the area where contrast is lowest.[1] This implies a per-region or per-pixel analysis, rather than relying on a single background color derived from computed styles.

ACT’s proposed rule "Text has enhanced contrast" formalizes a per-pixel approach. It defines "background colors of text" as the colors of all pixels, excluding foreground color pixels, in the bounding box around a visible character.[3] It defines "foreground colors of text" as the colors of all pixels of a visible character that change color when the CSS color property is changed, including anti-aliased pixels.[3] Anti-aliasing is recognized as a technique that blends foreground and background colors to create smooth edges, complicating identification of pure foreground and background pixels.[3] The rule then specifies that for each text character, the highest possible contrast between foreground and background color sets must meet enhanced contrast requirements—4.5:1 for large-scale text and 7.0:1 for normal text—unless the text is purely decorative or non-linguistic.[3]

The rule further clarifies that "highest possible contrast" refers to the highest contrast ratio between either the darkest color in set A and brightest color in set B or the brightest color in set A and darkest color in set B.[3] This approach attempts to guarantee at least some portion of the text has sufficient contrast, but ACT documentation explicitly cautions that passing this rule does not mean the text has sufficient color contrast overall.[3] If all background pixels have low contrast with all foreground pixels, the success criterion is not satisfied, even if some combinations achieve high contrast. Moreover, when some pixels have sufficient contrast and others do not, legibility becomes a nuanced question with no clear method for determination, which is why passing the rule does not necessarily imply conformance with the associated success criterion.[3]

These details are critical for the agent skill’s contrast algorithm: they indicate that standards bodies are moving toward per-pixel analysis for complex backdrops but also recognize that such analysis yields ambiguous cases where some pixels pass and others fail. This is where an "unresolvable-backdrop" policy becomes necessary.

### APCA and WCAG 3: Status and Implications

The Accessible Perceptual Contrast Algorithm (APCA) is a newer method designed to better predict readability contrast, particularly in modern UI contexts and dark modes.[11] APCA reports lightness contrast as an Lc value, ranging from Lc 0 to Lc 106 for dark text on light background and Lc 0 to Lc -108 for light text on dark background, with the minus sign indicating negative contrast for light-on-dark scenarios.[11] APCA is part of the larger S-Luv accessible color system and aims to capture perceived contrast more accurately than the WCAG 2.x formula, which has been criticized for not accounting well for certain color combinations and font weights.[11]

However, APCA’s status within WCAG 3 is currently exploratory rather than normative. Adrian Roselli’s analysis of WCAG 3 contrast developments notes that APCA was marked for removal as exploratory content in early 2023 because it did not gain sufficient working group support within six months, leading to its removal from the July 2023 WCAG 3 working draft.[2] The working group explained that exploratory items not advancing to the next stage are automatically removed, and while APCA’s work is considered promising, it is not part of a final standard.[2] Roselli emphasizes that APCA is draft guidance being actively tested and evaluated, and explicitly warns that no one should call APCA "WCAG 3"; any such labeling is incorrect.[2]

Roselli also highlights that WCAG 3 is years from completion and suggests 2030 at the earliest for a final standard.[2] Any contrast algorithm adopted into WCAG 3 will not be final until then, meaning that tools or agents using APCA now are operating outside normative standards and must handle the risk of mismatch with current WCAG 2.x requirements. Roselli suggests that if teams choose to use APCA, they should either select colors that also conform to WCAG 2 contrast requirements or document cases where APCA-recommended colors fail WCAG 2 and be prepared to respond to possible legal challenges, particularly from automated checkers used by litigants.[2]

For the agent skill under consideration, this implies that APCA can be used as a secondary, advisory metric but WCAG 2.x contrast ratios remain the normative baseline. A conservative design would compute WCAG 2.x ratios and optionally APCA values, reporting both but basing pass/fail decisions on WCAG 2.x thresholds. This is particularly important because many existing automated testing tools, such as axe-core, are aligned with WCAG 2.x success criteria and current legal enforcement tends to reference WCAG 2.0/2.1.

### Per-Pixel Versus Computed-Style Sampling, and Naive Ancestor Walk Failure Modes

Most naive automated contrast checkers are built around the DOM and CSSOM rather than per-pixel analysis. A common pattern is to compute the foreground color from the element’s computed style and then walk ancestors to find a background color—perhaps the nearest ancestor with a non-transparent background-color—and feed these two colors into the WCAG formula. This ancestor-walk approach is straightforward but fails in the presence of gradients, background images, transparent overlays, or composited effects such as mix-blend-mode.

MDN’s documentation of the Window.getComputedStyle() method explains that it returns a CSSStyleProperties object containing resolved values of all CSS properties after applying active stylesheets.[8] However, it warns that returned values can be deliberately inaccurate in certain cases, such as visited links, where browsers may lie about computed styles to avoid CSS history leaks.[8] This means that even a naive approach that trusts computed styles as ground truth is subject to security-driven distortions in specific contexts. Moreover, getComputedStyle returns CSS properties, not actual rendered pixel values. For background images, gradients, or semi-transparent layers, computed styles may indicate that an element has a background-image or gradient but not directly expose the blended color under a particular text glyph.

The ACT rule "Text has enhanced contrast" is essentially a reaction to these limitations. It defines background colors and foreground colors in terms of pixel sets rather than CSS properties.[3] This suggests moving from computed-style sampling to per-pixel sampling, where the checker draws or accesses the rendered canvas and identifies pixels corresponding to text and surrounding background in order to calculate contrast ratios.[3][12] In practice, this often means using browser APIs such as CanvasRenderingContext2D’s getImageData() or headless-browser screenshot capture and offline pixel analysis.

A critical failure mode arises when deterministic tools use computed styles to identify background colors but ignore gradients, images, or semi-transparent overlays. WebAIM notes that text over gradients, semi-transparent colors, and background images must meet contrast requirements, and suggests testing the lowest-contrast area.[1] Yet a simple ancestor-walk algorithm might take a solid base background color from an ancestor element and compute a perfectly acceptable contrast ratio, even though the specific portion of the text in question overlies a darker or lighter region of a gradient or an image that produces far lower contrast. Because WCAG does not define exact measurement procedures for these cases, naive tools may pass content that fails actual contrast requirements.

The documented failure rate of such naive approaches is not precisely quantified in the available sources, but ACT documentation makes clear that when some pixels have sufficient contrast and others do not, legibility can be compromised even though a checker might find a passing combination.[3] OpenAccess’s analysis of automated testing tools warns that they only detect a limited subset of accessibility issues and that there is a risk of misinterpretation when tools report "0 issues"; this does not mean there are no issues, only that none were detected within the tool’s scope.[15] This general observation applies strongly to naive contrast measurement, which may simply not test the visually lowest-contrast regions.

For the agent skill’s contrast algorithm and unresolvable-backdrop policy, the evidence suggests several high-confidence conclusions. First, WCAG 2.x contrast ratios must be computed using accurate foreground and background colors, which in complex backdrops require per-pixel or at least multi-region sampling rather than relying solely on computed styles.[1][3][14] Second, gradients, images, and semi-transparent layers necessitate measuring contrast in areas known or suspected to have lowest contrast; WebAIM’s recommendation and ACT’s pixel-based definitions point in this direction.[1][3] Third, because standards bodies themselves recognize that passing pixel-based rules does not guarantee legibility, the agent must adopt a policy for declaring certain backdrops "unresolvable" or requiring human review when measured contrast varies widely across pixels or when any portion of text falls below threshold despite some pixels passing.

A reasonable policy is to define the deterministic contrast gate as follows. For each text element, sample multiple regions, including areas under each glyph or a grid across the bounding box, and compute contrast ratios using WCAG 2.x formula. If the minimum region-level contrast is below the required threshold, mark the element as failing. If the minimum exceeds threshold, mark as passing. If contrast varies so widely that some pixels pass and others fail, and the failing regions cover more than a threshold proportion of glyph area or occur near the center of glyphs where reading occurs, mark the element as failing. Where sampling is technically infeasible, such as dynamic video backgrounds, mark the check as "could not run" and refrain from asserting pass or fail. This three-state taxonomy, discussed further below, ensures that unmeasurable or ambiguous cases do not produce false assurances.

## Reliability Envelopes of Automated Accessibility Testing

### Empirical Coverage: Axe-Core, Deque Studies, and Competing Evidence

Automated accessibility testing tools, particularly those built around rule engines like axe-core, have been widely studied for their coverage and reliability. Deque Systems, the maintainers of axe-core, conducted a large study analyzing anonymized data from over 2,000 audits across various industries, covering over 13,000 pages and nearly 300,000 issues.[4] They found that, on average, 57 percent of accessibility issues were completely covered by automated testing using their axe suite of tools.[4] This result challenges a widely held belief that automated testing provides only 20–30 percent coverage. Deque argues that measuring the total volume of issues detected by severity and impact is more meaningful than simply counting success criteria covered.[4]

In a separate "Automated Accessibility Coverage Report," Deque looks at coverage in terms of WCAG 2.1 success criteria.[13] They report that automated testing found issues for 16 out of 50 Level AA success criteria, supporting the 20–30 percent automated coverage claims when coverage is defined as "success criteria for which automated tools can detect at least one issue."[13] This distinction clarifies an apparent contradiction. When coverage is measured as the percentage of issues found across all criteria, automated tools may detect a majority of issues—57.38 percent in Deque’s study of first-time audits—because many high-frequency issues are in categories that automation can handle well.[13] Yet when coverage is measured as "how many success criteria are touched by automation," the fraction is closer to 32 percent (16 out of 50).[13]

Deque’s report identifies seven categories that accounted for over 80 percent of total issues recorded: Language of Page (3.1.1), Parsing (4.1.1), Contrast (Minimum) (1.4.3), Bypass Blocks (2.4.1), Non-Text Content (1.1.1), Name, Role, Value (4.1.2), and Info and Relationships (1.3.1).[13] Many of these are structural or semantic properties that automated tools can reliably inspect via DOM and attribute analysis, which explains why a concentrated set of criteria can produce a large portion of total issues.

Other studies present more conservative estimates of automated coverage. A summary of AI accessibility tools statistics notes that research consistently shows automated testing tools, including AI-enhanced ones, detect approximately 30–40 percent of WCAG violations.[6] The UK Government Digital Service study, widely cited in the industry, found automated tools catching only about 30 percent of issues across tested sites.[6] This report also states that automated tools generate false positive rates averaging 25–35 percent, meaning roughly one-quarter to one-third of flagged issues are not genuine accessibility violations and require human review to dismiss.[6] False negatives, especially for complex issues involving user experience, cognitive accessibility, or interaction patterns, can reach approximately 60 percent, meaning the majority of issues in these domains remain undetected by automation.[6]

OpenAccess’s analysis further emphasizes that automated tests only detect a limited subset of accessibility issues and warns specifically against misinterpreting a "0 issues" result.[15] Such a result only means the tool found no issues among the tests it can perform; many issues may exist outside its scope.[15] OpenAccess recommends tools like axe DevTools, noting their philosophy of providing zero false positives, but still insists that manual verification is required, particularly for tools that are more "sensitive" and prone to raising false positives or warnings, such as IBM’s Equal Access Accessibility Checker.[15] This underscores that even within the automated tool landscape, approaches to false positives differ.

Reconciling these studies suggests that automated tools have high coverage within the subset of issues they can detect, particularly structural and semantic defects, but low coverage across all possible accessibility issues, especially dynamic and experiential problems. It also highlights that false positive rates are tool-dependent, with some vendors explicitly optimizing for high specificity.

### Axe-Core and ACT Implementation: Criteria Reach and Consistency

W3C’s documentation of axe-core as an ACT implementation provides a more granular view of its rule coverage.[5] Axe-core implements numerous ACT rules, covering both WCAG 2 rules and proposed rules, and W3C assesses its consistency against curated examples.[5] For many rules, such as "Role attribute has valid value," "ARIA state or property has valid value," and "ARIA attribute is defined in WAI-ARIA," axe-core is marked as fully consistent, meaning it correctly reports outcomes across all examples and maps failures to appropriate success criteria.[5] For instance, the rule "Button has non-empty accessible name" is implemented fully consistently, covering all 17 examples and correctly reporting which success criteria are failed.[5]

Some rules have partial coverage due to technical limitations. For "ARIA state or property has valid value," axe-core covers 20 of 21 examples; in one example it cannot determine the outcome.[5] W3C notes that such limitations often arise from technical constraints in tools and that ACT Rules do not account for accessibility support issues, such as differences among assistive technologies.[5] This indicates that even within the set of structural checks, edge cases exist where automation cannot confidently determine pass or fail.

Deque’s coverage report notes that, across the analyzed pages, automated issues were found for 16 of the 50 Level AA success criteria, and they list success criteria where automated issues and manual issues both occurred.[13] Success criteria such as 1.3.5 Identify Input Purpose, added in WCAG 2.1, are validated by automated tests checking autocomplete attribute values.[5][13] However, many criteria, especially those related to user interaction, cognitive load, and subjective content clarity, show few or no automated issues and are primarily covered by manual testing.[13][14] This supports the conclusion that criteria involving timeouts, complex pointer gestures, animation from interactions, and content on hover or focus cannot be fully assessed by static analysis of DOM and CSS.[14]

### False Positives, False Negatives, and Criteria Automation Limits

The evidence suggests that automated tools have reasonably high sensitivity for a subset of structural and semantic criteria but limited reach into interaction or experiential criteria. Deque’s studies highlight that automated testing can find a majority of issues when measured by total issue count, but OpenAccess and other reports point to significant false negative rates for complex issues and non-trivial false positive rates for some tools.[4][6][13][15] The UK Government Digital Service study, cited by TestParty, places automated detection at around 30 percent of issues, reinforcing that coverage depends heavily on what is counted and how.[6]

Certain WCAG criteria are particularly resistant to automation. Success Criteria related to keyboard traps, focus order, and keyboard-inaccessible functionality require dynamic interaction that static automated tools cannot fully simulate; TestParty notes that automated testing struggles with keyboard accessibility, achieving only about 45 percent accuracy in identifying keyboard traps and focus order issues, implying a high false negative rate.[6] Success Criteria such as 2.1.2 No Keyboard Trap, 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide, 2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold, and 1.4.13 Content on Hover or Focus involve dynamic behaviors that are difficult to assess without interactive testing and user perspective.[14] WCAG 2.1’s own descriptions for criteria like 1.4.13 emphasize conditions such as dismissibility, hoverability, and persistence, which require simulating hover and focus transitions and evaluating whether additional content behaves correctly.[14]

Even for contrast, which is structurally defined, per-pixel analysis is required for complex backdrops, and ACT rules acknowledge that passing a test does not guarantee overall legibility due to anti-aliasing and varying contrast across pixels.[3] Automated tools that rely on computed styles and simple ancestor searches cannot fully capture the nuance required for robust contrast evaluation in modern designs.

For the agent skill, these findings imply a conservative use of automated checks. They can be treated as high-confidence detectors for structural violations in criteria that map well onto DOM and attribute analysis (for example, language of page, role, name, value, non-empty titles), but as low-confidence or non-existent coverage for interaction-heavy criteria. This distinction should be reflected both in internal coverage metrics and in user-facing explanations. The skill should avoid implying that a page "passes WCAG" on the basis of automated checks; at most, it can say that no failures were detected among the subset of criteria it tests.

## Result Taxonomies, Unmeasurable Populations, and Coverage Denominators

### Three-State Taxonomy: Pass, Fail, and Could-Not-Run

Mature test and verification tooling often distinguishes between a check that passed, a check that failed, and a check that could not be run because required conditions were not met. This three-state taxonomy is crucial for avoiding silent failures and misinterpreted "all clear" results. ACT Rules and W3C’s description of them implicitly acknowledge such distinctions by noting that rules are informative and that content may fail a rule yet still conform due to alternate versions.[7] They also stress that passing a rule means only that no corresponding failures were detected, not that the success criterion is fully satisfied.[7]

Although the ACT documentation does not explicitly formalize a "could-not-run" state, its discussion of limitations, applicability conditions, and examples where tools cannot tell the outcome points directly to such a category.[5][7] For example, W3C’s assessment of axe-core implementation of "ARIA state or property has valid value" notes that in one example the implementation cannot determine the outcome due to technical limitations.[5] In a robust reporting system, such cases would be classified as "inapplicable" or "not run" rather than pass or fail.

For the agent skill, a clear definition of three states is recommended. A "pass" indicates that the check was applicable, executed successfully, and found no violation within its scope. A "fail" indicates that the check was applicable, executed successfully, and detected a violation. A "could-not-run" or "not applicable/indeterminate" indicates that the check either did not apply to the content at hand, or could not execute because required data or behaviors were unavailable or unmeasurable, such as pixel sampling in a video-backed section or keyboard trap detection in a highly dynamic script context.

### Reporting Unmeasurable Populations and Coverage Denominators

The denominator used in coverage metrics has significant impact on how stakeholders interpret automated testing results. Deque’s distinction between issue coverage (percent of issues found) and success criteria coverage (number of criteria touched by automated issues) illustrates this.[4][13] A system that reports "we have 80 percent coverage" without clarifying whether this refers to issues within automated scope or success criteria overall invites misinterpretation.

OpenAccess emphasizes that when an automated tool reports "0 issues," it only means no issues were detected among the tests the tool can perform.[15] This is a clear warning against conflating the tested population with the entire set of potential issues. In a three-state taxonomy, checks marked as "could-not-run" or "inapplicable" must not be silently treated as passes. Instead, the agent should explicitly report how many checks were applicable and executed, how many could not be run, and how many success criteria are outside automated scope altogether.

A robust coverage denominator might be defined as the number of checks that were applicable and executed, divided by the number of checks implemented in the agent, with a separate indicator for the proportion of WCAG success criteria represented by those checks. For example, if the agent implements checks for 20 out of 50 Level AA success criteria but only runs 18 of them on a particular page due to applicability conditions, it should state that 18 checks were run, 2 were inapplicable or could not be run, and that these checks cover 20 of 50 success criteria in the standard. This level of transparency reduces the risk of shipping defects that exist in untested criteria.

Evidence that conflating pass and could-not-run causes shipped defects comes indirectly from the observed false negatives and limited criteria coverage in automated tooling. TestParty’s report that false negative rates reach approximately 60 percent for complex issues implies that many defects are shipped when organizations rely on automated tools alone.[6] OpenAccess’s discussion of misinterpretation of "0 issues" and Deque’s findings that automated issues exist for only 16 of 50 success criteria further suggest that without explicit coverage reporting, teams may assume broader safety than is warranted.[13][15] While explicit case studies of shipped defects due specifically to conflated result states are not in the provided sources, the combination of limited automated scope and optimistic interpretations provides strong circumstantial evidence.

Given this, the agent skill should enforce a strict policy: no inference from "could-not-run" to "pass," and no aggregation that counts not-run checks as passing when computing overall pass rates. The skill must also treat "pass" as "no violation detected within test scope" rather than "criterion fully satisfied." Labeling, such as "No automated failures detected for these criteria," can help maintain this nuance.

## Headless-Browser Engine Divergence as Measurement Hazard

### Divergent Behaviors in getComputedStyle and Rendering Engines

Automated design and accessibility checks often rely on headless browsers to render pages and measure styles and geometry. However, different rendering engines—Blink (Chromium), Gecko (Firefox), WebKit (Safari)—and headless variants can diverge in how they compute and report styles. A Playwright cross-browser testing tutorial emphasizes that distinct layout engines interpret HTML, CSS, and JavaScript differently, leading to UX inconsistencies and functional discrepancies across browsers.[9] It notes that V8 (Chrome), SpiderMonkey (Firefox), and JavaScriptCore (Safari) process scripts differently, requiring validation that logic works in all environments and that CSS support and layout behavior are consistent.[9]

MDN’s documentation of Window.getComputedStyle provides a specific example of divergence driven by security policy. To prevent CSS history leaks, browsers may lie about computed styles for visited links, returning values as if the user never visited the linked URL.[8] This means that an accessibility checker running in a browser may read color or text-decoration properties that do not reflect the actual rendered appearance for visited links, potentially missing low-contrast or other issues specifically affecting visited states.

A Chromium bug report highlights another divergence: getComputedStyle() returning parent element styles for non-existent pseudo-elements rather than an empty string.[16] In this reported case, the expected behavior was to return an empty string for computed styles of non-existent pseudo-elements, but the actual behavior was to return specified styles for the parent element.[16] Automated tools relying on pseudo-element detection via getComputedStyle could interpret such values as real measurements, incorrectly concluding that styles exist where they do not.

These examples show that computed-style APIs are not perfectly reliable across engines and that tool authors must account for idiosyncrasies and security-driven distortions. For an agent skill using headless browsers, this presents a measurement hazard: a checker might record a style value that is either fabricated for privacy reasons or inherited from a parent due to implementation quirks, and treat it as an accurate representation of the rendered UI.

### Mitigation Patterns: Longhand vs. Shorthand Reads, Capability Probing, and Feature Detection

Several mitigation patterns can reduce the impact of engine divergence. First, when reading styles, tools can prefer longhand properties and avoid inferring values from shorthands that may be normalized differently across engines. For example, reading color, background-color, and font-size as individual computed properties rather than parsing the shorthand font or background may minimize discrepancies.

Second, capability probing and feature detection should precede assertions. Before relying on getComputedStyle for pseudo-elements, a tool can test known scenarios to see how the engine behaves, such as querying styles of deliberately non-existent pseudo-elements and verifying whether the result is empty or inherited.[16] If the engine exhibits known bugs, the tool can avoid using those paths or apply engine-specific workarounds. Similarly, for visited links, a checker might avoid relying on computed styles of :visited states or treat them as unreliable and mark contrast checks in visited states as "could-not-run."

Third, cross-engine comparison can be used to validate critical measurements. Playwright allows running the same tests across Chromium, Firefox, and WebKit, and the tutorial demonstrates diagnosing a layout failure isolated to Firefox.[9] For a high-stakes agent skill, particularly when measuring contrast or layout for conformance claims, using multiple engines can identify discrepancies and prevent reliance on a single engine’s idiosyncratic behavior.

Finally, rendering to a pixel buffer or capturing screenshots via standardized APIs may be more robust than relying solely on computed styles. Since visual defects and contrast issues are fundamentally about pixels, headless browsers that provide accurate screenshots can feed deterministic pixel-based analyses that are less susceptible to engine-specific computed-style anomalies. ACT’s pixel-based contrast rules implicitly assume access to reliable pixel data.[3][12]

The agent skill should thus incorporate feature detection and capability probing for critical APIs, avoid interpreting zero or default values as meaningful measurements without verification, and prefer pixel-based measurement for contrast and layout where feasible. It should also maintain engine-aware code paths and treat engine-dependent results as less reliable, potentially requiring multi-engine confirmation for certain checks.

## Layout Integrity and Visual Defect Detection via DOM Geometry

### Formal Verification of Layout Accessibility: VizAssert and Visual Logic

Layout integrity and visual defect detection can be approached via pixel comparison or via DOM geometry and formal layout models. Traditional methods like Selenium, Sikuli, and Raven use automated pixel-by-pixel comparison engines to detect layout differences across versions, but they typically consider a subset of rendering parameters and are not sufficient to ensure accessibility.[10] A key challenge is verifying accessibility guidelines that involve relationships among visual elements, such as ensuring that labels are correctly aligned with inputs or that hit areas are large enough.

VizAssert, introduced in a 2018 paper, presents a tool for verifying that assertions about visual layout hold for web pages using formalization of the W3C browser rendering algorithm.[10] VizAssert employs a "visual logic" language to express visual layout properties, including 14 accessibility and usability guidelines such as those in their Table 1.[10] It uses a formal model of browser rendering to determine all possible renderings of a page across variations in rendering parameters and checks whether assertions hold in all cases.[10] This approach moves beyond pixel comparison under fixed parameters to reasoning about layout invariants under diverse conditions.

VizAssert’s contribution includes an efficient formalization of crucial components of the browser rendering algorithm and demonstration that it can verify that realistic web pages satisfy accessibility and usability guidelines.[10] It verifies assertions such as "the main content must be visible within a viewport of a given size without overlapping critical elements" or "interactive elements must maintain minimum size and spacing under zoom." While the paper’s detailed results are not fully described in the provided excerpt, the abstract indicates that VizAssert was able to verify certain accessibility guidelines for web pages in a sound manner.[10]

From this, we can infer that certain classes of layout defects are provably computable via DOM geometry and formal rendering models, particularly those involving positional relationships, ordering, overlap, and size constraints. For example, verifying that a skip-to-content link appears before the main content in DOM and is within the visible viewport, or that headings follow a semantically structured order, can be done via DOM and CSS box model analysis. Similarly, verifying that elements do not overlap or occlude one another under given viewport sizes can be done by computing bounding rectangles and checking for intersections.

### Over-Fire Rates and Practical Limitations of Geometry-Based Checks

The challenge in applying DOM geometry checks in real-world automated systems lies in over-fire rates—false positives where geometry appears to violate a guideline but actual user experience is acceptable. The evidence base for over-fire rates is limited in the provided sources, but some relevant insights can be drawn.

VizAssert’s formal approach attempts to avoid over-fires by modeling all possible renderings and proving assertions hold universally.[10] In practice, however, most automated tools use simpler heuristics: they check bounding boxes at a few viewport sizes and attempt to infer overlap or misalignment. Such heuristics are vulnerable to false positives in cases such as deliberate overlapping design motifs, legitimate use of fixed-position overlays, or responsive designs that shift content based on interaction.

Deque’s coverage reports indicate that some layout-related success criteria, such as 1.4.10 Reflow (content can be presented without loss of information or functionality, and without requiring scrolling in two dimensions at specified viewport sizes), are challenging for automation.[13][14] WCAG 2.1’s description of 1.4.10 Reflow specifies conditions involving reflowed content at widths equivalent to 320 CSS pixels and heights equivalent to 256 CSS pixels, and makes exceptions for parts of content that require two-dimensional layout for usage or meaning.[14] Automated tools can, in principle, simulate these viewport sizes and examine layout, but determining whether reflow causes loss of information or functionality, or whether two-dimensional layout is essential, requires more than simple geometry. This creates potential for over-fires in cases where two-dimensional layout is legitimately necessary.

Similarly, Success Criterion 2.4.1 Bypass Blocks, which requires a mechanism to bypass blocks of content repeated on multiple pages, can be partially assessed via DOM presence of skip links or landmarks, but whether layout makes these mechanisms discoverable and usable is not fully captured by geometry alone.[13][14] Tools might over-fire by flagging absence of a particular pattern even when alternate patterns serve the same purpose.

Given these limitations, the agent skill should adopt a narrow set of geometry-based layout checks that have strong empirical support and low over-fire rates. These include verifying that elements with ARIA roles or landmarks are within visible viewport bounds, that elements marked as buttons or links have sufficient size to be easily tappable, and that content does not overlap or occlude critical interactive elements under standard viewport sizes. More subjective layout issues, such as design consistency, aesthetic alignment, or optimal spacing, should be left to model-based judgement or human review.

It is advisable to treat geometry-based checks as structural detectors rather than definitive UX assessments. When a geometry check flags a potential issue, the agent can record it as a structural anomaly and, optionally, ask the model component to assess whether the anomaly likely degrades usability. This layered approach can reduce over-fires by combining deterministic detection with model-based context, while maintaining clear separation between structural violations and heuristic judgements.

## LLM-as-Judge for Visual and Design Quality: Empirical Findings and Limitations

### Inter-Rater Agreement and Position/Verbosity Bias

Empirical evaluation of large language models as judges for visual and design quality is still emerging, and the provided sources do not include formal studies specific to this question. However, based on the broader literature on LLM-as-evaluator and multi-modal models’ performance, several patterns can be outlined with moderate confidence.

Inter-rater agreement between models and human evaluators tends to be reasonably high when tasks are well-defined and criteria are objective, such as rating grammar correctness or adherence to instructions. For subjective domains like aesthetics and design quality, agreement is lower and more sensitive to prompt framing. Models can approximate consensus opinions on what constitutes clean or cluttered design, sufficient spacing, or legible typography, but their judgements are influenced by training data biases and the examples they have seen.

Position and verbosity bias are particularly salient. Models often weight information presented earlier in a prompt more heavily, and content described in greater detail can attract more attention. In an agent skill where model judgement is fed by deterministic measurements and textual descriptions of the UI, ordering and emphasis of issues can affect the model’s overall assessment. If the prompt mentions 47 buttons and discusses them in detail, the model may infer that buttons are a primary concern and amplify any issues related to them, even if the actual UI has fewer or less problematic buttons.

Models may also exhibit bias toward more symmetric and minimalist designs, as these often align with design guidelines learned from training data. They might rate dense dashboards or specialized UIs more harshly, not fully accounting for domain-specific needs. This makes them less reliable as sole judges of design quality in specialized contexts.

### Model Consensus and Validation of Subjective Judgement

One proposal in the literature is to use model consensus—aggregating judgements from multiple models or from the same model under varied prompts—to validate subjective assessments. The idea is that if multiple models independently agree that a design is cluttered or that contrast is low, this consensus might serve as a proxy for human agreement. In practice, consensus can reduce random variation but does not eliminate systemic bias. If all models share similar training data and architecture, their judgements may correlate strongly even when they diverge from human preferences.

For the agent skill, model consensus can be used as a secondary signal, but deterministic standards and human review remain primary. For example, if deterministic contrast checks pass but multiple models consistently flag a region as "hard to read," this may indicate that WCAG’s numeric thresholds do not fully capture perceived readability, and APCA or other perceptual metrics might suggest an adjustment.[11] Conversely, if deterministic checks fail and models rate the design as "fine," conformance must still be considered violated.

Given the limited direct evidence in the provided sources, assertions about specific inter-rater agreement figures and detailed bias patterns should be treated with lower confidence. This uncertainty reinforces a cautious approach: model-derived scores should not be used as primary conformance indicators but rather as advisory signals, and any numeric ratings should be clearly labeled as heuristic.

## Prompt and Instruction Design for Review Agents: Severity, Restraint, and Anti-Fabrication Structures

### Severity Taxonomies to Reduce False Positives

Severity taxonomies categorize findings by impact, such as blocking defects, major issues, minor issues, and informational observations. In accessibility and design review, severity classification helps prioritize remediation and prevent minor stylistic concerns from equating to critical violations. OpenAccess notes that one risk in manual testing is engaging practitioners who raise significant numbers of false positive issues, filling audit reports with "issues" that are not genuine accessibility barriers.[15] This illustrates that even human auditors can over-report when severity structures are weak or absent.

For an agent skill combining deterministic and model-based checks, severity taxonomies must reflect both standards-based impact and uncertainty in detection. Deterministic violations of WCAG success criteria directly affecting perceivability or operability, such as missing alt text on critical images or non-contrast-compliant text, should be classified as high severity. Issues arising from ambiguous measurements or model judgements should be classified as lower severity, often informational or advisory.

LLM prompts should explicitly instruct models to differentiate between standards violations and subjective critiques. For example, the agent can present determined violations separately from model input and ask the model to provide optional commentary on user experience impact for each violation, while also inviting additional design improvement suggestions that are clearly labeled as non-conformance issues. This prevents models from reclassifying minor design suggestions as critical accessibility defects.

### Restraint Mechanisms Against Finding-Count Inflation

Finding-count inflation occurs when tools or agents over-report issues, either due to aggressive heuristics or model eagerness. This can overwhelm teams and dilute attention from genuine defects. TestParty’s report of false positive rates averaging 25–35 percent for automated tools suggests that many tools already raise more issues than warranted.[6] OpenAccess stresses the importance of selecting tools with low false positive rates and manually verifying issues from more "sensitive" tools.[15]

In LLM-based review, prompts can inadvertently encourage inflation by asking models to "find as many issues as possible" or by rewarding verbosity. To counter this, prompts should emphasize precision and restraint, instructing models to report only issues supported by evidence or measurements provided. They can also require the model to reference specific elements or coordinates for each reported issue, such as "Button with text 'Submit' at coordinates (x,y)" or "Heading level 2 'Features' at top of page," thereby tying findings to concrete entities.

Another mechanism is limiting the maximum number of issues the model can report per category, such as "List at most five accessibility concerns and at most five design suggestions, prioritizing those with highest impact." While we must avoid list formatting in this report, in the agent implementation, numerical limits can be enforced in structured outputs. This encourages models to prioritize and avoid enumerating minor concerns purely to satisfy a perceived need for output.

The deterministic layer can also enforce restraint by controlling which issues are passed to the model. For example, the agent might only feed model-interpreted structural anomalies that have ambiguous severity, asking the model to help classify them. Clear-cut deterministic violations need not be reviewed by the model at all, reducing opportunities for the model to "discover" additional issues not backed by measurements.

### Structures to Prevent Fabricated Observations

Fabricated observations, such as "I audited 47 buttons" when no button count measurement was performed, are a known hazard in LLM-based systems. To prevent this, agent architectures should separate observation and analysis roles. A deterministic collector module can generate a structured inventory of elements—buttons, links, headings, images—complete with counts and identifiers. The model then receives this inventory and is instructed to base its analysis strictly on the provided data and any screenshots, not on assumptions.

Prompts should explicitly forbid invented counts or elements. For example, they can declare "Do not infer the existence of elements not listed in the inventory. If you have no data about a type of element, say so explicitly rather than assuming its presence or absence." Agent logic can then validate model outputs against the inventory, rejecting any claims that mention elements not present or report counts that do not match. This validation step can be deterministic: if the model says "47 buttons" but the inventory shows 9, the agent flags the output as inconsistent and either discards that portion or instructs the model to correct itself.

Another structural safeguard is requiring the model to include matching identifiers from the inventory for each reported issue. If the inventory labels buttons as "button_1," "button_2," and so on, the model must reference these labels. This eliminates the possibility of hallucinated elements and provides a direct mapping from model output to DOM elements for downstream remediation.

By combining deterministic inventories, strict prompts, and output validation, the agent skill can effectively prevent fabricated observations from influencing reported defects, thereby preserving trust in its analysis.

## Synthesis and Design Recommendations for the Agent Skill

### Contrast Algorithm and Unresolvable-Backdrop Policy

The evidence supports a conservative contrast algorithm grounded in WCAG 2.x ratios. The agent should compute contrast using the normative formula \((L_1 + 0.05) / (L_2 + 0.05)\) with thresholds of 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text under Level AA, and optionally 7:1 and 4.5:1, respectively, for enhanced contrast under Level AAA.[1][3][14] Foreground colors should be derived from pixel samples corresponding to text glyphs, including anti-aliased pixels, and background colors from surrounding pixels in the bounding box as defined in ACT’s "Text has enhanced contrast" rule.[3] For simple solid backgrounds, computed style background-color may be sufficient, but for gradients, images, and semi-transparent layers, per-pixel sampling is necessary.

The agent should adopt a policy that declares certain backdrops unresolvable when pixel sampling reveals highly variable contrast or when technical limitations prevent accurate measurement, such as dynamic video backgrounds or GPUs that do not expose pixel buffers. In such cases, the contrast check should be marked as "could not run," and no pass or fail should be asserted. Where sampling reveals mixed contrast—some pixels above threshold, others below—the agent should consider legibility and proportion. If failing pixels cover significant portions of glyph interiors or occur centrally where reading happens, the element should be marked as failing, consistent with the spirit of WCAG’s minimum contrast requirements.[1][3]

APCA can be integrated as an advisory metric. The agent may compute APCA Lc values using tools such as APCA’s contrast calculator, reporting them alongside WCAG ratios.[11] However, pass/fail decisions must remain anchored in WCAG 2.x thresholds due to APCA’s non-normative status and its removal from exploratory status in WCAG 3 drafts.[2][11]

### Three-State Taxonomy and Coverage Reporting

The agent should implement a three-state taxonomy for each check: pass, fail, and could-not-run. A check passes when it is applicable, executed successfully, and finds no violation within its defined scope. It fails when executed successfully and detects a violation. It is marked as could-not-run when either not applicable to the content or technically infeasible to execute, such as when critical data such as pixels or event sequences cannot be obtained.[5][7][15]

Coverage reporting must distinguish between checks implemented in the agent, checks applicable to a page, and checks actually executed. The agent should report the number of checks run, the number failed, and the number not run, and separately indicate how many WCAG success criteria are represented by these checks, using Deque’s findings that automated issues exist for 16 of 50 Level AA success criteria as a baseline for expectations.[13] Customer-facing messaging should avoid language such as "WCAG compliant" based solely on automated checks and instead use phrasing like "No automated failures detected for the following criteria; manual and user testing are required for comprehensive assessment."[7][14][15]

### Layout Checks That Survive Empirical Scrutiny

Geometry-based layout checks must be carefully chosen to minimize over-fire rates. The agent may safely implement checks for overlap and occlusion of interactive elements under standard viewport sizes, ensuring that buttons, inputs, and critical menus are not covered by other elements or off-screen.[10][14] It can verify that elements with ARIA roles or landmarks are within visible viewports and that their DOM order roughly matches visual order, to support keyboard navigation and assistive technology expectations.[5][10][14]

Checks that require subjective determination of essential two-dimensional layout, semantic grouping beyond DOM structure, or complex interaction sequences should either be omitted from deterministic gates or implemented with very conservative thresholds and low severity classification. Formal approaches like VizAssert can guide future expansion, but until such formal models are embedded in the agent, geometry-based checks should focus on clearly definable conditions such as intersection of bounding boxes, minimum size of tappable areas, and presence of skip links or landmarks.[10][13][14]

### Model Judgement: Scored or Advisory

Given the limited empirical evidence on LLM-as-judge for visual and design quality and the known biases in model reasoning, model judgement should not be scored in the same way as deterministic checks. Rather than giving models numeric scores that imply precision, the agent should treat model output as qualitative advisory commentary. When models identify potential issues, the agent should record them as suggestions, with severity not exceeding medium unless corroborated by deterministic checks.

In internal metrics, model-derived suggestions should be tracked separately from deterministic violations. Over time, correlation between model suggestions and human review outcomes can be measured to refine trust. Until robust data demonstrates high alignment, model scores should not drive automated pass/fail decisions, but they may inform triage of manual testing.

### Severity and Restraint Structures to Prevent Inflation and Fabrication

The agent must enforce severity taxonomies that distinguish deterministic, standards-based violations from heuristic suggestions. Deterministic violations of critical success criteria such as 1.1.1 Non-Text Content, 1.3.1 Info and Relationships, 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), and 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value should be classified as high severity.[1][13][14] Model suggestions about spacing, typography, or aesthetic alignment should be classified as low or informational severity.

Restraint in finding counts can be achieved by limiting the number of findings the model may propose per category, instructing it to prioritize impact, and validating its outputs against deterministic inventories of elements. The agent should prohibit models from inventing element counts or types and should validate every reported issue against known DOM elements, rejecting inconsistent claims.[15]

In data structures, each finding should include a source field indicating whether it came from deterministic measurement or model judgement, an evidence field referencing the measurement or screenshot involved, and a severity field determined by deterministic criteria or guided by model suggestions but subject to agent rules. This structure will prevent conflation of model speculation with hard violations and support downstream analytics that separate structural issues from design feedback.

## Conclusion

The evidence from WCAG 2.1, WebAIM, ACT rules, Deque’s studies, OpenAccess’s analysis, MDN documentation, Chromium bug reports, Playwright tutorials, and the VizAssert paper converges on a set of practical design principles for an automated agent skill that reviews rendered web UI. Contrast must be measured using WCAG 2.x ratios with per-pixel sampling for complex backdrops, and APCA can be used only as an advisory metric. Naive computed-style ancestor-walk approaches are insufficient and prone to silent failure in modern designs. Automated accessibility tools like axe-core provide high coverage of structural issues within their scope but reach only a subset of WCAG success criteria and cannot detect many interaction or experiential problems. False positives and false negatives are significant, requiring careful tool selection and manual verification.

A three-state result taxonomy distinguishing pass, fail, and could-not-run is necessary to avoid silent failures and misinterpretation of coverage. Coverage denominators must be explicit, indicating how many checks were run and which success criteria they cover. Headless-browser engine divergence introduces measurement hazards that must be mitigated via feature detection, longhand property reads, cross-engine validation, and pixel-based analysis.

Geometry-based layout checks can safely detect overlap, occlusion, and basic positional relationships but must be limited to avoid over-fire in subjective design areas. Formal tools like VizAssert demonstrate that some layout properties are provably computable, but practical implementation remains constrained. Large language models can provide valuable advisory judgements on visual and design quality but should not be treated as normative judges; their output must be structurally constrained to avoid fabricated observations and inflated issue counts.

The agent skill should therefore be built around deterministic browser gates that align with WCAG and ACT rules, with a cautious and clearly delineated layer of model-based commentary. Severity taxonomies, restraint mechanisms, and validation structures must ensure that only genuine, evidence-backed defects are treated as violations while heuristic suggestions are presented as optional improvements. By grounding decisions in the strongest available evidence and explicitly acknowledging limitations and uncertainties, the resulting skill can deliver reliable automated UI review without overclaiming its capabilities or obscuring the need for human and user testing.
