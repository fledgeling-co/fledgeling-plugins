# Adapting React Native and Expo Apps for Foldable iPhone Duo and Other Dual‑Screen Devices

The evolution from early dual‑screen Android devices such as Microsoft’s Surface Duo to Apple’s forthcoming foldable iPhone Duo exposes a consistent set of layout, navigation, and API challenges that React Native and Expo applications must address through carefully designed abstractions rather than per‑device hacks.[1][2][14] At a high level, successful adaptation hinges on three pillars: using dynamic window and hinge geometry instead of static screen assumptions, routing to native split‑view and bar containers rather than recreating navigation chrome in JavaScript, and adopting cross‑platform hinge and reserved‑region APIs modeled on prior art such as DualScreenInfo and Jetpack WindowManager FoldingFeature.[1][3][11] Apple’s new iOS 27.1 APIs, including `UIView.reservedRegions` for division and occlusion, `UIHingeInteraction` and `UIHinge` for hinge state, and arrangement views for split and overlay layouts, together provide a rich but inherently native substrate that must be bridged into React Native with care, particularly around state lifecycles and performance.[12][13][14][15][16] At the same time, React Native’s own facilities such as `useWindowDimensions`, Dimensions change events, react‑native‑screens, expo‑router’s Split View, and safe‑area handling have already accumulated failure modes on Android foldables—stale dimensions, symmetric inset math, and device‑model branching—that should be codified as explicit anti‑patterns in any skill that teaches agents to adapt apps for iPhone Duo.[5][7][9][10] Drawing on Microsoft’s dual‑screen design patterns like list‑detail and companion pane, as well as Android’s hinge‑state driven tabletop layouts, the most robust approach is to define cross‑platform layout rules in terms of panes, hinge regions, and reserved areas, then route to existing native implementations (ArrangementView, Split View, DualScreenInfo‑style abstractions) whenever possible instead of rebuilding hinge logic in JavaScript.[1][3][11][16][19] The trajectory from proprietary Surface Duo display masks to generic Jetpack WindowManager, and from traditional iPhone bars to Duo’s vertical multi‑bar arrangements, strongly suggests that a future‑proof React Native stack should be built on vendor‑supported window and bar APIs, maintaining as little custom geometry code as necessary while aggressively testing against dynamic poses, multi‑scene layouts, and asymmetric safe areas.[6][11][14][18]  

## Context and Evolution of Foldable and Dual‑Screen Support in Mobile Platforms

The emergence of dual‑screen and foldable devices as mainstream targets for mobile applications dates back to around 2019–2020, when vendors such as Microsoft and Samsung introduced hardware that split the primary interaction area across multiple physical or flexible panels.[11][19] On Android, the Surface Duo was particularly important because Microsoft not only released the device but also produced extensive design guidance and dedicated APIs for dual‑screen experiences, including React Native‑specific support.[1][2][19] Apple’s ecosystem, in contrast, remained focused on single‑display iPhones and larger iPads until the introduction of iPhone Duo, a foldable phone with both an outer display and a larger inner display, together with a folding region that can partially divide content.[14] This staggered adoption has led to asymmetric maturity in tooling: React Native and Expo developers have accumulated several years of experience dealing with Android foldables, while iOS foldable support is only now being formalized with iOS 27.1 and associated technologies like `UIView.reservedRegions` and `UIHingeInteraction`.[12][13][14][15]  

From the React Native perspective, the core framework was originally designed around relatively static notions of “screen” and “window”, embodied in APIs such as Dimensions and `useWindowDimensions`, which exposed the size of the device screen and the visible application window.[9][10] Over time, the documentation has explicitly acknowledged that these dimensions may change dynamically, for example due to device rotation or foldable devices that alter the window size, and it now recommends listening to change events instead of caching size values.[9][10] This shift reflects an underlying conceptual evolution: rather than associating layout decisions with fixed device categories like “phone” or “tablet”, modern React Native apps are expected to respond to live changes in window geometry, including the possibility that the visible area may be split or occluded by hinges, cameras, or dynamic islands.[10][15]  

On Android, this conceptual shift has been reinforced by Jetpack WindowManager, a library designed to provide a common API surface for foldable and dual‑screen behaviors across both new and older platform versions.[11] Its `FoldingFeature` class describes the fold or hinge region, exposing state (flat, half‑opened, flipped) and physical type (fold vs hinge), which allows apps to adapt behavior based on device posture and hinge properties rather than hard‑coded device identifiers.[11] Microsoft’s own Surface Duo support initially relied on a proprietary DisplayMask but has since seen active discussion about migrating React Native components like DualScreenInfo and TwoPaneView to Jetpack WindowManager to achieve broader compatibility with devices such as Samsung Galaxy Fold and Flip.[6] This progression from vendor‑specific to general APIs is a crucial piece of prior art for thinking about iPhone Duo: while Apple’s new APIs are, by definition, proprietary to its ecosystem, their design is clearly intended to be generic across form factors and reusable within different frameworks, including UIKit and SwiftUI.[14][15][16]  

Expo and react‑native‑screens represent another axis of evolution, focusing on navigation and split‑view layouts rather than raw geometry.[5][7] The Expo Router Split View submodule (`expo-router/unstable-split-view`) introduces native split view layout on iOS, implemented as a submodule of `expo-router` that exports components for building split view layouts using platform‑native system split views.[5] However, this API is explicitly labeled alpha, available only on iOS in Expo SDK 55 and later, with potential breaking changes and a warning that it is not yet ready for production.[5][7] On non‑iOS platforms, Split View falls back to rendering as a standard Slot navigator, ensuring cross‑platform operation without conditional code, while on iPhone it collapses all columns into a single view so that only one column is visible at a time.[5][7] These behaviors underline a key theme: an app’s navigation structure must be resilient to devices that support side‑by‑side panes and those that collapse panes into stacked or single views, and React Native stacks have begun to encode this with native components rather than ad‑hoc JavaScript logic.  

Apple’s iPhone Duo documentation further accelerates the push toward generic layout and navigation abstractions by emphasizing resizing, size classes, scene geometry, and reserved regions as the primary tools for adapting to foldable poses and multiple screens.[14][15][16] In particular, Apple recommends adopting system‑provided layouts and containers such as split views, tab bars, arrangement views, and navigation stacks, all of which automatically handle resizing for the outer display, fully open or folded inner display, and camera occlusions.[14] This recommendation aligns closely with the experience in React Native and Expo: rather than custom layout engines, developers increasingly rely on native split views or arrangement containers mediated through libraries like react‑native‑screens and expo‑router, while simply configuring these containers based on runtime geometry and hinge state.[5][7][18] As a result, any skill that aims to teach an agent to adapt React Native or Expo apps to iPhone Duo must treat native window and layout APIs as first‑class dependencies and define rules for when to route to them versus when to implement custom behavior.  

In summary, the broader context reveals a trajectory from static device categorization to dynamic geometry and hinge‑aware layouts, mediated by both platform‑specific APIs (Jetpack WindowManager, Apple’s reserved regions and hinge interactions) and cross‑platform React Native tooling (Dimensions events, Split View, dual‑screen libraries).[6][9][10][11][14][15] This trajectory frames the rest of the analysis: adaptation strategies for iPhone Duo should be derived from proven patterns on Surface Duo and Android foldables, but must also respect the unique constraints of Apple’s ecosystem, including vertical bar arrangements and multi‑scene support, while minimizing reinvention of hinge logic in JavaScript.  

## Native iOS 27.1 APIs for iPhone Duo and Their Implications for React Native and Expo

### UIView.ReservedRegion and Reserved Region Semantics

One of the most significant new APIs in iOS 27.1 for foldable support is `UIView.ReservedRegion`, which models areas within a view’s coordinate space that are occupied by other entities and thus not owned by the view.[15] Apple describes reserved regions as covering two categories: occlusion regions, where an element such as the Dynamic Island, a camera, or window controls occludes content, and division regions, where content splits into separate regions, such as at the fold of a hinge.[15] Each reserved region includes a frame in the view’s coordinate space, margins around that frame intended for interactive content, an active state flag, and an identifier, which together allow developers to rearrange content to account for these regions.[15] Reading reserved regions is done through the `reservedRegions(kind:options:)` method on `UIView`, which returns all reserved regions that intersect the view, optionally including inactive regions depending on query options.[15][16]  

In practical terms, these semantics mean that an iPhone Duo app can query where the fold lies within its view hierarchy and whether that fold is currently active (e.g., the device is partially folded), as well as identify camera occlusions on both inner and outer displays.[14][15] Apple’s guidance explicitly notes that a reserved region representing the fold behaves as a division, splitting a large view into smaller ones, and is active only when the device is partially open; when the device is fully open and flat, the fold’s division region becomes inactive and has zero width, though it may still be returned in queries when using options that include inactive regions.[14][15][16] This behavior allows developers to treat inactive division regions as hints for high‑level layout decisions—for example, preferring an even number of grid columns when a fold is present in any pose—while relying on active states for fine‑grained repositioning.[16]  

For React Native and Expo, the direct use of `UIView.ReservedRegion` is not possible without native bridging, but the conceptual model is highly relevant for how a cross‑platform skill should think about foldable layouts. Rather than embedding hinge coordinates into global configuration or device‑specific code, a skill should encourage using APIs that expose reserved regions dynamically and per view, then mapping those regions into JavaScript layout decisions. A React Native native module could, for example, expose a method on iOS that takes a view tag and returns an array of reserved regions, each with frame, margins, kind, and active state, closely mirroring Apple’s API.[15] This module would be analogous to DualScreenInfo’s `windowRects` and hinge descriptions on Android, but tuned for iOS’s division and occlusion vocabulary.[1][3][15]  

Crucially, Apple emphasizes basing layout calculations on a scene or containing view’s bounds rather than global screen dimensions, and sizing views relative to their containers rather than fixed iPhone dimensions.[14] These recommendations mesh directly with React Native’s existing idioms of flexbox and `useWindowDimensions`, which operate in terms of container sizes and window metrics rather than static device sizes.[9][10] However, React Native today does not have a built‑in concept of reserved regions or occlusions; this gap suggests that any adaptation skill targeting iPhone Duo should define explicit rules for bridging reserved region information into layout and ensure that safe‑area libraries do not mistakenly assume symmetric insets when asymmetries arise from occlusions or division regions.[14][15]  

### UIHingeInteraction and UIHinge: Observing Hinge State

In addition to static or quasi‑static geometry captured in reserved regions, iOS 27.1 introduces `UIHingeInteraction`, an interaction for observing hinge state associated with a view’s hierarchy.[12] Developers add a `UIHingeInteraction` to a view to receive hinge state updates, with the interaction’s handler being called whenever the hinge state changes or when the interaction moves between hierarchies.[12] Each update includes a `UIHingeInteraction.Update` object, from which the app can read a `UIHinge` instance or detect that the hinge is unavailable (when the interaction leaves a hierarchy that provides hinge updates, the update’s hinge is nil).[12][13]  

The `UIHinge` object encapsulates the state of a single hinge, including the current angle of the hinge in radians and a status value describing the hinge’s status.[13] While Apple’s documentation does not list all possible statuses in the excerpt, it is clear that developers can distinguish between different hinge poses such as fully open, partially folded (half‑open), or potentially flipped configurations.[13] Example code in the documentation shows updating UI based on the hinge angle and status as the user interacts with the hinge, including handling the case where hinge updates become unavailable.[12][13]  

For a React Native or Expo app, this hinge interaction model is central to supporting tabletop or half‑opened layouts where content may need to shift from a side‑by‑side split to an overlay or stacked configuration depending on the angle.[11][13][16] On Android, Jetpack WindowManager’s `FoldingFeature` similarly exposes the device’s current shape from a predefined list (STATE_FLAT, STATE_HALF_OPENED, STATE_FLIPPED), along with the physical type of the junction (TYPE_FOLD or TYPE_HINGE).[11] This parallel suggests a natural cross‑platform abstraction in React Native: an app‑level “hinge context” that provides asynchronous updates about hinge angle, pose, and type, and that can be consumed by layout components to adjust their arrangement style.  

In a React Native bridge, `UIHingeInteraction` would be wrapped in a native module that subscribes to hinge updates when attached to a root view and emits events to JavaScript, perhaps with a payload that includes angle, status, and active reserved regions.[12][13][15] This is functionally similar to DualScreenInfo’s `didUpdateSpanning` event listener on Surface Duo, which lets React Native code respond when an app is spanned across two screens or moved to a single screen.[1][3] In the Surface Duo example, DualScreenInfo is used to maintain a React state `isDualMode` and toggle between a two‑pane layout with a `Hinge` component and a single screen layout when spanning changes.[3] The hinge interaction on iPhone Duo would play a comparable role for more fine‑grained poses, including partially folded states, so any skill guiding adaptation should treat hinge events as a primary driver of layout transitions, including when to enable or disable list‑detail patterns or when to collapse panes.  

### Arrangement Views and Split vs Overlay Layouts

Apple’s iPhone Duo guidance introduces arrangement views as high‑level layout containers that manage how primary and secondary views are arranged based on factors such as horizontal and vertical size classes, aspect ratio, and the presence of active division reserved regions.[14][16] In SwiftUI, developers can use `ArrangementView` within a `NavigationStack`, providing primary and secondary views—such as a player view and an “up next” view—and configuring the arrangement style via modifiers like `.arrangementViewStyle(.split)`.[16] The default split style divides the arrangement’s bounds between primary and secondary views, splitting horizontally when the containing view is wider than tall, and vertically when taller, while overlay arrangements prefer stacking content above or below and moving to side‑by‑side when the device folds.[16]  

These arrangement views are tightly integrated with reserved region semantics: a split arrangement will divide content around division regions such as the fold, and when the split cannot be performed along its primary axis (for example, due to geometry constraints), it may choose to show only a single view instead.[16] Similarly, overlay arrangements may position content atop each other when there are no active reserved regions that act as divisions, and transition to side‑by‑side layouts when a fold appears.[16] At a higher level, Apple suggests evaluating whether an arrangement view can assist an app’s layout in handling the fold, rather than manually calculating positions for each pane.[14][16]  

For React Native and Expo, arrangement views represent a native mechanism analogous to React Native’s TwoPaneView, which lays out two views side‑by‑side while accounting for the hinge area on Surface Duo.[2][3][19] The TwoPaneView control, part of Microsoft’s React Native dual‑screen support, is designed to manage two panes with awareness of the hinge region, making it easy to implement patterns such as list‑detail or companion pane.[2][3][8][19] Similarly, Expo Router Split View exports components to build split view layouts using platform‑native system split views, handling collapsing behavior automatically on iPhone.[5][7] The conceptual overlap suggests that, for iPhone Duo, React Native and Expo apps should route to native arrangement views or their split view equivalents wherever possible, rather than reimplementing spanning logic in JavaScript.  

A skill that teaches agents to adapt apps should therefore encode rules such as: if the app needs a two‑pane layout that should follow system behavior across poses (side‑by‑side when wide and split by fold, stacked or collapsed when narrow), prefer using expo‑router’s Split View combined with native arrangement views on iOS, while using TwoPaneView or equivalent on Android with Jetpack WindowManager backing.[2][3][5][7][11][16] The skill should also caution that Split View is currently an alpha API in Expo SDK 55, iOS‑only, and subject to breaking changes, so production apps may need to gate its use or wrap it in an abstraction that can fall back gracefully.[5][7]  

### Multi‑Scene and Vertical Bar Behavior on iPhone Duo

Apple’s documentation for iPhone Duo emphasizes that the device supports multiple screen sizes, layouts, and orientations, and that apps must adapt gracefully across closed, open, and partially folded states.[14] It describes how navigation bars, toolbars, and tab bars can be presented vertically on the side of the display in certain poses, allowing navigation, toolbar, and tab bar controls to coexist within a shared region on iPhone Duo.[14][18] To opt into this new bar behavior, developers must rebuild their apps against the latest SDKs and ensure they are using system bar containers such as SwiftUI’s `NavigationStack`, `TabView`, and `NavigationSplitView`, or UIKit’s `UINavigationController` and `UITabBarController`, instead of custom bar implementations.[18]  

Apple further notes that in more complex layouts, toolbar items can move vertically only when their container is positioned along the display edge, and in split views only the detail column participates in the vertical bar region; items from other columns remain horizontal.[18] On the outer display, sheets with toolbars may be displayed vertically, whereas on the inner display they are centered by default and items remain horizontal.[18] This behavior implies that vertical bars are tied deeply into the navigation container hierarchy and layout, rather than being a simple flag that can be toggled globally.  

React Native and Expo currently rely on react‑native‑screens for native navigation primitives and on expo‑router for declarative routing layered atop those primitives.[5][7] React‑native‑screens provides a bridge to native navigation controllers, including stack and tab containers, and has begun to integrate toolbar and navigation bar behavior more closely with the underlying platform.[5] The Expo Router Split View documentation notes that the `.show()` method for navigating between columns requires react‑native‑screens version 4.24.0 or later, and that SDK 55 bundles ~4.23.0, meaning developers need to install `react-native-screens@~4.24.0` manually to use this feature.[5][7] This coupling underscores that advanced navigation behavior, such as programmatic column switching and potentially vertical bar placement, depends on staying current with native navigation bridges.  

For an adaptation skill targeting iPhone Duo, the implication is clear: it must strongly encourage developers to rely on native navigation containers and toolbars via react‑native‑screens and expo‑router, instead of building custom navigation bars in React Native views that cannot participate in the Duo vertical bar region.[5][7][18] Where expo‑router introduces unstable toolbar APIs that more closely mirror native bars, the skill should route to those even if they are marked experimental, provided the app is gated appropriately, because they offer the only way for React Native bars to integrate into the vertical bar system.[18] The skill should also include rules for auditing bar usage: replacing any full‑width custom toolbars at the top or bottom of the screen with native navigation or toolbar items, and ensuring detail panes in split views are the ones exposing vertical bars, in line with Apple’s guidance.[18]  

### Apple’s Layout Guidance for iPhone Duo and Its Alignment with React Native Best Practices

Beyond specific APIs, Apple’s “Preparing your app for iPhone Duo” document sets out general layout and resizing guidance that aligns strongly with modern React Native practices.[14] It recommends adopting standard layout controls and containers, using size classes and scene geometry to improve resizing, and handling reserved regions such as the fold and cameras to adapt folded layouts.[14][15] It urges developers to check how apps appear on both displays, when closed, open, or partially folded, rotate iPhone Duo in each pose to see how layouts react, and confirm that views resize well in every supported orientation and pose.[14] It also suggests identifying views, sheets, or popovers that position awkwardly when folding or opening, and finding elements that fall into the fold or are blocked by cameras, as these must be rearranged with awareness of occlusion and division regions.[14][15]  

Importantly, Apple advises using system‑provided layouts and containers that automatically handle resizing and occlusions, sizing views relative to their container, and basing layout calculations on the scene or containing view’s bounds rather than screen dimensions.[14] It recommends adopting Auto Layout in UIKit and automatic trait tracking to observe changes in horizontal and vertical size classes, then adapting interfaces accordingly.[14] These recommendations echo React Native’s emphasis on flexbox, window‑based sizing, and dynamic layout updates via `useWindowDimensions` and Dimensions change listeners, especially when targeting foldable devices or those where window size can change due to folding or multi‑window behaviors.[9][10]  

For a React Native adaptation skill, the alignment between Apple’s guidance and React Native idioms is a strategic opportunity. The skill can frame foldable support as a natural extension of best practices already recommended for tablets and desktop platforms: treat every layout as resizable, avoid relying on device model checks, determine layout modes based on current window size and aspect ratio, and respond to dynamic changes via event listeners.[9][10][14] The key additions for iPhone Duo are reserved region awareness and hinge interaction, both of which require native bridging but can be conceptually integrated into existing layout logic by treating division regions as split boundaries and occlusion regions as asymmetric safe areas.[14][15][16]  

Taken together, Apple’s reserved region, hinge, arrangement, and bar APIs represent a comprehensive native substrate for foldable iPhone Duo support, and any React Native or Expo adaptation strategy must pivot around them. The challenge is not conceptual—React Native already supports dynamic window sizing and flexible panes—but rather architectural: bridging enough of these native semantics into JavaScript without overcomplicating the app or replicating state that is better owned by the platform.  

## Prior Art in React Native on Foldables and Dual‑Screen Devices

### Surface Duo React Native Support: DualScreenInfo and TwoPaneView

Microsoft’s Surface Duo was one of the first dual‑screen devices to receive dedicated React Native support from the platform vendor, in the form of the `react-native-dualscreeninfo` package and associated documentation.[1][2][3] DualScreenInfo is described as a non‑visual helper component that provides information about the device so that code and layouts can adapt to dual screens.[1][2] It exposes methods such as `isDualScreenDevice`, which should be checked before accessing other dual‑screen APIs; `isSpanning`, indicating whether the app is currently spanning one or two screens; `windowRects`, providing the bounds of the two display rectangles excluding the hinge area; and an event listener `didUpdateSpanning` that allows code to respond when the app is spanned or moved to a single screen.[1][3]  

The package’s README illustrates how DualScreenInfo can be used in a React component to toggle layouts based on whether the app is in dual mode.[3] In the example, the app maintains a React state variable `isDualMode`, initialized from `DualScreenInfo.isSpanning`, and registers an event listener for `didUpdateSpanning` that updates this state when spanning changes.[3] When `isDualMode` is true, the component renders a two‑pane layout with flex direction row, placing “Screen 1” and “Screen 2” views on either side of a `Hinge` component; when false, it renders a single screen view.[3] This simple pattern encapsulates a key principle: dual‑screen adaptation in React Native should be expressed at the layout level as conditional rendering of panes based on live device information, rather than deep conditional branching in business logic.  

In addition to DualScreenInfo, Microsoft introduced TwoPaneView as a React Native control that lays out two views side‑by‑side, taking account of the hinge area on Surface Duo.[2] TwoPaneView is particularly suited to implementing dual‑screen design patterns such as list‑detail, extended canvas, two‑page, and companion pane.[2][19] For example, the Pizza Store demo uses TwoPaneView to present a menu list in one pane and pizza details or ordering controls in the other, aligning with the list‑detail pattern where a main pane hosts the list view and the details pane shows selected content.[8][19] The React Native samples for Surface Duo showcase these patterns and their code implementations, providing a practical blueprint for dual‑pane layouts.[8][19]  

Microsoft’s dual‑screen design guidance for list‑detail provides explicit do’s and don’ts that highlight common failure modes.[19] It recommends using the left side of the screen to display the list and the right side to display details of the selected item, and cautions against displaying the list across both screens or spanning a bottom menu navigation across two screens.[19] It also advises not to display the list on one screen and details on the other when the device is rotated into double landscape orientation, instead showing details when rotated with a back button to return to the list, and using the second screen to display larger images from a gallery rather than splitting the gallery across both displays.[19] These recommendations directly encode best practices that a React Native adaptation skill should transfer to iPhone Duo: avoid splitting lists across folds, ensure navigation aligns with pane boundaries, and maintain clear separation between overview and details across screens.  

### Unofficial React Native Dual‑Screen: react-native-dual-screen

Beyond Microsoft’s official package, the community has produced unofficial React Native support for Surface Duo, such as the `react-native-dual-screen` package.[4] This package exposes a `RNDualScreenManager` and a `Hinge` component, mirroring the official API’s semantics.[4] The example code shows using `RNDualScreenManager.isDualMode()` to determine whether the app is in dual mode, storing the result in a React state, and registering an `addOnChangeListener` to update this state when dual‑mode changes.[4] When in dual mode, it renders a flex row layout with two panes separated by a `Hinge` component, similar to the official DualScreenInfo example; when not, it renders a single screen view.[4]  

The existence of both official and unofficial libraries demonstrates both demand and experimentation around dual‑screen abstractions in React Native, and highlights the risk of fragmentation when vendor‑specific APIs are used without a unified cross‑platform design.[4][6] For an adaptation skill, this prior art underscores the importance of selecting canonical libraries and patterns to route to, rather than recomposing ad‑hoc hinge logic in multiple competing packages. Given Microsoft’s official support and its alignment with Jetpack WindowManager migration plans, DualScreenInfo and TwoPaneView should be treated as primary prior art; community packages like react‑native‑dual‑screen can inform design decisions but should not be the main target for new apps unless they demonstrate clear advantages.[3][4][6][11]  

### Jetpack WindowManager and Its Intersection with React Native

Jetpack WindowManager is a crucial Android library in the foldable context because it abstractly models flexible displays and dual screens across device manufacturers and platform versions.[11] In version 1.0.0‑alpha03, it introduces the `FoldingFeature` class, which describes either the folding region of a flexible display or a hinge that joins two physical display panels.[11] The API provides access to critical device information such as the current state (`getState()` returning one of STATE_FLAT, STATE_HALF_OPENED, STATE_FLIPPED) and the physical type (`getType()` returning TYPE_FOLD or TYPE_HINGE), enabling developers to tailor layout and interaction based on posture and hinge characteristics.[11]  

WindowManager is designed so that classes implementing display features, like `DisplayFeature`, can be processed as lists, with developers casting items to `FoldingFeature` when needed to query device shape, junction type, and bounds of the hinge or fold.[11] This general API surface has motivated discussions about migrating framework‑level dual‑screen support from proprietary vendor packages to WindowManager, including in React Native.[6] Issue #107 in the `react-native-dualscreen` repository explicitly proposes re‑basing DualScreenInfo and TwoPaneView on Jetpack WindowManager to achieve broader compatibility beyond Surface Duo, such as Samsung devices that support Google’s dual‑screen and foldable APIs.[6] The issue notes that the existing DualScreenInfo implementation uses Microsoft’s DisplayMask package, which only works on Surface Duo, while WindowManager offers similar functionality in an event‑driven fashion but can support other devices.[6]  

For a React Native adaptation skill, Jetpack WindowManager is not only a source of prior art but also a model for how platform‑level foldable APIs can be consumed from JavaScript. The skill should encourage using WindowManager‑based abstractions on Android and adopting an analogous approach on iOS via reserved regions and hinge interactions, rather than encoding per‑device heuristics.[6][11][14][15] Moreover, the event‑driven nature of WindowManager closely resembles Apple’s hinge interactions and React Native’s Dimensions change listeners, suggesting that foldable support across platforms can be treated as a stream of events that update layout state rather than a static configuration.[10][11][12][13]  

### React Native Dimensions and useWindowDimensions in Foldable Contexts

As noted earlier, React Native’s Dimensions API and `useWindowDimensions` hook are core tools for responsive layout.[9][10] The documentation explicitly states that `useWindowDimensions` is the preferred API for React components, as it updates when the window’s dimensions change, aligning well with the React paradigm.[9][10] Dimensions provides methods such as `Dimensions.get('window')` and `Dimensions.get('screen')` for obtaining the size of the visible application window and device screen, respectively, with a `ScaledSize` or `DisplayMetrics` object describing width and height.[9][10]  

Crucially, the documentation warns that although dimensions are available immediately, they may change due to device rotation, foldable devices, or other factors, and that any rendering logic or styles depending on these constants should re‑query the values on every render instead of caching them.[9][10] It suggests using inline styles rather than setting values in a StyleSheet based on initial dimensions, and provides an event listener API `Dimensions.addEventListener('change', handler)` that fires when any property in the Dimensions object changes.[9][10] The handler receives an object describing both window and screen metrics, allowing apps to respond to dynamic resizing, including foldable device behavior.[9][10]  

These guidelines highlight a significant failure mode that has already manifested in real apps: stale Dimensions when developers read window size once (for example in initialization) and never update it, leading to incorrect layouts when devices are rotated, windows are resized, or foldable devices change their visible region.[9][10] For a React Native adaptation skill, this is an explicit anti‑pattern that must be encoded as a rule: never assume Dimensions are static, and always design layout logic to re‑evaluate size in response to change events or rely on `useWindowDimensions`’ automatic updates.[9][10] This rule is particularly important for iPhone Duo, where window size can be affected by outer vs inner displays, device poses, and side‑by‑side multitasking, and where reserved regions can further complicate the effective usable area.[14][15][17]  

### Expo Router Split View and React Native Screens

Expo Router Split View is an important piece of prior art for native split view layouts on iOS in React Native.[5][7] The documentation describes it as an alpha API available on iOS only in Expo SDK 55 and later, with the caveat that it is subject to breaking changes and not yet ready for production.[5][7] Split View exports components to build split view layouts using platform‑native split views, and is a submodule of `expo-router`, meaning developers must install `expo-router` to use it.[5][7]  

Platform support details reveal that Split View is only available on iOS; on other platforms, it falls back to rendering as a standard Slot navigator, ensuring the app works across platforms without conditional code.[5][7] Additionally, on iPhone, Split View automatically collapses all columns into a single view, with only one column visible at a time, reflecting native behavior where split views are generally collapsed on smaller devices.[5][7] The API allows developers to control which column is displayed when the split view is collapsed via the `topColumnForCollapsing` prop, with acceptable values such as `primary`, `supplementary`, and `secondary`, and default system behavior when the prop is unspecified.[7]  

For more advanced navigation, Split View supports a `.show()` method that programmatically shows a specific column, but this method requires `react-native-screens` 4.24.0 or later, while Expo SDK 55 currently bundles ~4.23.0, necessitating manual installation of the newer version.[5][7] Split View also enforces structural constraints: there can be only one SplitView in the navigation hierarchy, and it cannot be nested inside another navigator except for Slot; it must be used at the root layout level.[7] These constraints are important for avoiding complex or unsupported nested split view scenarios, which could lead to confusing user experiences or technical bugs.  

For iPhone Duo adaptation, Split View offers a partial solution: it can provide a native split view behavior on iOS, including collapse on iPhone, while reacting to window size changes and column configuration. However, it does not directly understand hinge or reserved region semantics, nor does it automatically respond to fold division regions.[5][7][14][15] A skill should therefore treat Split View as a building block for multi‑column navigation and list‑detail layouts, while emphasizing that hinge‑aware rearrangements must be implemented either via arrangement views or custom logic that uses reserved region data and hinge interactions to adjust Split View usage.[14][16] Furthermore, the skill should highlight the alpha status of Split View and the dependency on react‑native‑screens version alignment, guiding teams on when to adopt it and when to rely on more stable navigation primitives.  

## Cross‑Platform Failure Modes and Best Practices for Foldable and Dual‑Screen Layouts

### Stale Dimensions and Window Size Assumptions

As noted, stale Dimensions constitute one of the clearest and most documented failure modes in React Native apps targeting dynamic window environments.[9][10] When developers treat `Dimensions.get('window')` as a constant, caching the result into a global variable or style sheet without considering future changes, layouts will not adapt to rotations, resizes, or fold events.[9][10] React Native’s documentation explicitly warns against this practice, advising that any rendering logic or styles depending on dimensions should call `Dimensions.get` on every render or rely on `useWindowDimensions`.[9][10]  

On foldable devices, stale dimensions are particularly problematic because the effective window size can change dramatically as the device moves between closed, half‑opened, and fully opened states, or as apps span multiple screens.[11][14] On Surface Duo, for example, spanning the app across both screens yields two distinct window rectangles separated by the hinge, and DualScreenInfo’s `windowRects` API is designed to expose them.[1][3] If an app’s layout logic assumes a single window size and never updates, it cannot respond properly to spanning events, leading to content appearing under the hinge or being truncated.[1][3] Similarly, on iPhone Duo, the outer and inner displays have different sizes and aspect ratios, and reserved division regions may further reduce usable width in certain poses; static dimension assumptions will fail to handle these changes.[14][15]  

For a foldable adaptation skill, the rule should be unambiguous: treat all window metrics as dynamic and event‑driven.[9][10][11][14] Layout components should subscribe to Dimensions change events or use `useWindowDimensions`, and hinge‑aware components should update in response to DualScreenInfo events on Android or UIHingeInteraction updates on iOS.[1][3][10][12][13] Additionally, where cross‑platform abstractions for hinge context are introduced, they should be designed as React contexts or hooks that re‑render consuming components when hinge or reserved region information changes, avoiding manual invalidation or imperatively triggered relayout.  

### Symmetric Inset Math vs Asymmetric Safe Areas and Reserved Regions

Another pervasive failure mode is assuming symmetric safe area insets—equal padding on left and right, or top and bottom—when in reality occlusions and folds create inherently asymmetric regions.[14][15][16] Historically, safe area insets were primarily concerned with elements like the iPhone notch or home indicator, which introduced asymmetry mainly along the top or bottom edges. However, foldable devices like iPhone Duo introduce division regions that split content vertically or horizontally, as well as camera occlusions on both outer and inner displays that may occupy arbitrary positions.[14][15] Apple’s reserved regions explicitly differentiate occlusion and division regions, each with frames and margins, making clear that the usable area within a view may be irregularly shaped.[15]  

The React Native ecosystem often uses libraries such as react‑native‑safe‑area-context to handle safe area insets, typically exposing a uniform inset object with top, bottom, left, and right values. While such libraries can represent asymmetric insets, app code often treats them as symmetric or approximates them with simple logic that may not capture complex occlusions. For instance, developers might assume that left and right insets are equal or ignore inset differences when computing list padding, leading to content being occluded on one side by cameras or hinge regions. On iPhone Duo, Apple warns that views and controls may appear in the fold region and become difficult to see or interact with if they are not rearranged based on reserved region queries.[14][15]  

A foldable adaptation skill must therefore codify a more sophisticated approach to safe areas and reserved regions. On iOS, instead of relying solely on generic safe area insets, apps should query reserved regions of kind occlusion and division via `UIView.reservedRegions`, incorporate their frames and margins into layout calculations, and treat asymmetric insets as first‑class constraints, particularly for interactive controls and lists.[15] On Android, Jetpack WindowManager’s `FoldingFeature` provides hinge bounds that can similarly be modeled as an asymmetric division region, informing where content should be split or avoided.[11] A cross‑platform abstraction in React Native should unify these concepts: a set of “reserved zones” with coordinates and margins, used to shape layout decisions, rather than a simplistic top/bottom/left/right inset object.  

### Device Model Branching vs Size and Geometry‑Driven Layout

Device model branching—writing conditional logic that checks for specific device models, such as “iPhone Duo” or “Surface Duo”—is a tempting shortcut but a well‑known anti‑pattern in responsive design.[6][11][14] On Android, Jetpack WindowManager explicitly aims to provide a generic API across devices and platform versions, so apps can query hinge and fold features without knowing the exact device model.[11] On iOS, Apple’s iPhone Duo guidance emphasizes using size classes, scene geometry, and reserved regions to adapt layouts, rather than relying on fixed dimensions or model identifiers.[14][15]  

Device model branching becomes particularly brittle as new devices and form factors emerge, or as existing devices receive software updates that alter behavior. For example, designing layout decisions based on the assumption that all “Duo” devices have specific hinge positions or outer display sizes would break if Apple introduces a new foldable with different geometry or if iPhone Duo itself changes in future iterations.[14] Similarly, on Android, relying on manufacturer‑specific display masks, like Microsoft’s DisplayMask, restricts app compatibility to a single device, whereas Jetpack WindowManager’s FoldingFeature supports multiple devices.[6][11]  

A robust React Native adaptation skill must therefore instruct developers to branch based on geometry and environment metrics rather than device models.[9][10][11][14][15] This means using window size, aspect ratio, and the presence of division reserved regions or FoldingFeatures to determine when to enable split layouts, list‑detail patterns, or tabletop interfaces, rather than checking “is iPhone Duo” or “is Surface Duo”. The skill should also encourage using trait‑based logic on iOS where appropriate, mapping horizontal and vertical size classes to layout modes, and using hinge status and angle to refine behavior further.[14][13]  

### Two‑Pane List‑Detail and Companion Pane Patterns

The list‑detail pattern and related dual‑pane patterns represent some of the most mature and widely documented best practices for dual‑screen and foldable devices.[2][8][19] Microsoft’s dual‑screen design guidance defines the list‑detail pattern as having a main pane with a list view and a details pane for content, typically using the left side of the screen for the list and the right side for details.[19] It notes that this pattern is naturally suited to wider viewing areas and frequently used in email and address book applications, taking advantage of two distinct screens and the natural boundary between them: one screen shows the items list, and the other shows details of the selected item.[19]  

The guidance emphasizes that separating navigation or overview from details allows users to drill deeper into content while staying grounded in their position within the overall list.[19] It offers clear examples of apps that benefit from this pattern, including mail apps, scheduling apps, photo galleries, music apps with playlists and song details, and apps with strong navigation structure.[19] Microsoft also provides React Native sample projects implementing the list‑detail pattern, demonstrating how TwoPaneView and DualScreenInfo can be combined to switch between single‑pane and dual‑pane layouts depending on spanning.[8][19]  

For iPhone Duo, Apple’s arrangement views and split layouts can serve as the native substrate for list‑detail patterns.[14][16] In split arrangements, primary and secondary views can correspond to lists and details; when the view is wider than tall, the arrangement presents them side‑by‑side, while when taller, it stacks primary over secondary.[16] Division reserved regions such as the fold ensure that the primary and secondary views are placed appropriately around reserved regions, avoiding occlusion or splitting lists across folds.[15][16]  

A React Native adaptation skill should therefore treat two‑pane list‑detail as a foundational pattern and encode rules such as: when a list‑detail use case exists, prefer implementing it using native split or arrangement views (e.g., Expo Split View plus SwiftUI arrangement on iOS, TwoPaneView plus WindowManager on Android), and ensure that the list never spans across a hinge or fold while details occupy the other pane.[2][3][5][7][11][16][19] The skill should also capture best practices such as selecting a default detail item when switching from single‑pane to dual‑pane mode, so users do not see an empty detail pane on spanning; Microsoft’s guidance notes that detail views should show something meaningful, such as the last touched item or the top item, when entering list‑detail mode.[19]  

### Tabletop and Half‑Opened Layouts and Hinge‑Driven Effects

Tabletop or half‑opened layouts refer to device poses where the foldable is partially open, resembling a laptop or book, such that the upper and lower halves of the inner display may function as separate interaction zones.[11][13][16] On Android, Jetpack WindowManager’s FoldingFeature state indicates when the device is half‑opened, enabling apps to alter layout accordingly—for example, placing playback controls in the lower half and content in the upper half, or adjusting interactive surfaces.[11] Apple’s iPhone Duo hinge APIs similarly provide angle and status information, and Apple’s tech talks emphasize using reserved region and arrangement view APIs to handle various poses, including partially folded states.[13][16]  

In SwiftUI, arrangement views and GeometryProxy APIs can query reserved regions via the new `reservedRegion` method, allowing developers to determine which areas of the view are available and how they are divided by folds or occlusions.[16] The tech talk explains that the fold’s division region is only active when the device is folded, and has zero width when flat; inactive regions can still inform high‑level decisions, such as preferring even grid columns when a division exists.[16] It also notes that arrangement views consider multiple factors, including size classes, aspect ratio, and the presence of active divisions, when choosing split or overlay configurations.[16]  

For React Native and Expo, hinge‑driven effects must be carefully designed to avoid overusing hinge angle as a continuous animation source, which could hurt performance or distract users. Instead, hinge status should be used to drive discrete layout mode transitions—e.g., switching between portrait, desktop‑like, and tabletop layouts—while angle may be used sparingly for affordances such as adjusting video playback orientation or enabling special experiences like book‑like page turning.[11][13][16]  

A foldable adaptation skill should capture both the opportunity and the caution here. It should propose cross‑platform hinge contexts that map states like flat, half‑opened, and flipped, using FoldingFeature on Android and UIHinge on iOS, then define recommended layout modes for each state (single column, dual pane, tabletop, etc.).[11][13][16] It should also warn against hooking intensive animations to hinge angle updates, instead suggesting coarse thresholds (e.g., angle ranges for half‑open vs flat) and debounced updates, especially in React Native where JavaScript‑driven animations can be more expensive than native ones.  

### Native Toolbars, React Native Screens, and Expo Router Bars

Finally, native toolbars and navigation bars represent both a failure mode and an opportunity. Custom toolbars implemented as React Native views, positioned at the top or bottom of the screen, cannot participate in iPhone Duo’s vertical bar region or adapt to bar co‑existence across multiple displays.[18] Apple’s Duo guidance insists that developers should use system bars provided by navigation containers, such as SwiftUI’s NavigationStack and TabView, or UIKit’s UINavigationController and UITabBarController, which manage their own bars.[18] It explains that iPhone Duo allows navigation, toolbar, and tab bar controls to coexist within a shared region, and that vertical bar behavior depends on these containers.[18]  

React Native’s navigation ecosystem, including react‑native‑screens and expo‑router, has begun to integrate more deeply with native navigation containers, including toolbar behavior.[5][7] Expo Router’s unstable native toolbar features, combined with React Native screens’ integration, offer a path for React Native apps to leverage native toolbars and potentially participate in Duo’s vertical bar arrangements, though this remains dependent on future updates and is not fully documented in the provided sources.[5][7][18]  

For an adaptation skill, the rule must be: avoid custom navigation bars and toolbars; instead, route navigation and toolbar logic through react‑native‑screens and expo‑router, using their bridges to native containers.[5][7][18] The skill should also encourage updating dependency versions to meet the requirements of advanced navigation features like Split View’s `.show()` method, and future vertical bar integration, and advise auditing apps for custom toolbar usage that may need to be refactored.[5][7][18]  

## Adaptation Strategy for React Native and Expo Apps Targeting iPhone Duo

### Bridging Native iOS 27.1 APIs into React Native

Adapting a React Native or Expo app to iPhone Duo requires bridging Apple’s iOS 27.1 APIs into the JavaScript runtime, particularly reserved regions and hinge interactions.[12][13][15] A pragmatic strategy is to design a thin native module on iOS that exposes a few key functions and events, mirroring the semantics of DualScreenInfo and FoldingFeature while aligning with Apple’s reserved region and hinge models.[1][3][11][15]  

Such a module might provide a method to query reserved regions for a given view or scene, returning an array of objects describing frame, margins, kind (occlusion or division), and active state, and an event emitter that notifies JavaScript when reserved regions change or hinge pose transitions occur.[15][16] Additionally, it could expose a hinge context, including angle and status, as a subscription API similar to UIHingeInteraction, emitting updates when hinge state changes or becomes unavailable.[12][13] The module would likely need to integrate with React Native’s view hierarchy to map view tags to UIView instances for reserved region queries, and with scene delegates to handle multi‑display scenarios on iPhone Duo.[14][17]  

A foldable adaptation skill should instruct agents to prefer such bridging strategies over manual geometry computations based on global screen bounds. Specifically, it should note that Apple recommends basing layout on scene and container bounds, and that reserved regions already encode occlusion and division details.[14][15] By exposing these semantics directly, the module allows JavaScript layout components to make informed decisions without replicating platform logic.  

### Cross‑Platform Hinge and Reserved Region Abstractions

On Android, Jetpack WindowManager’s FoldingFeature and Microsoft’s DualScreenInfo already provide hinge and window rectangle semantics, while on iOS, reserved regions and UIHinge represent analogous information.[1][3][11][14][15] A cross‑platform abstraction in React Native should unify these concepts into a single “fold context” that can be consumed by layout components and patterns.  

This context could describe whether the device has a fold or hinge, current pose (flat, half‑open, flipped), type (physical hinge vs flexible fold), and any division or occlusion regions within the app’s primary view.[11][13][15] It would also expose window rectangles or pane bounds where content can be safely placed, including considerations for hinge or fold width and camera occlusions.[1][3][11][14][15] Layout components such as two‑pane containers, list‑detail views, and companion panes would consume this context to decide how to arrange their child views.  

A skill teaching adaptation should encourage building such cross‑platform abstractions, rather than writing separate platform‑specific layout logic without shared semantics. It should highlight that DualScreenInfo already offers a model for methods like `isDualScreenDevice`, `isSpanning`, `windowRects`, and spanning events, and that future iOS modules can implement equivalent semantics using reserved regions and hinge interactions.[1][3][15] It should also note that Jetpack WindowManager is event‑driven and must be set up early in the application lifecycle, suggesting that fold context initialization should occur during app startup, with hooks available in React components.[6][11]  

### Leveraging Expo Router Split View and Native Arrangement Views

For two‑pane layouts, list‑detail patterns, and multi‑column navigation, Expo Router Split View, combined with native arrangement views, offers a robust foundation on iPhone Duo.[5][7][14][16] A skill should advise using Split View at the root layout level of the app, defining primary, secondary, and supplementary columns as needed, and leveraging the `topColumnForCollapsing` prop to control which column appears when the split view collapses on iPhone.[5][7] It should also encourage using Split View’s `.show()` method for programmatic navigation between columns, provided the app installs `react-native-screens` version 4.24.0 or later, in line with documentation.[5][7]  

On iPhone Duo, arrangement views can be used within SwiftUI or UIKit host views to manage primary and secondary content in response to reserved regions and hinge state.[14][16] React Native apps may embed such arrangement views via native components or use Split View as a higher‑level container that itself is arrangement‑aware through platform integration. In either case, the skill should emphasize that arrangement and split containers should be the primary tools for multi‑pane layout, not custom flexbox stacks that ignore fold geometry.  

### Handling Window Resizing: Dimensions and useWindowDimensions

Window resizing, including changes in width, height, and aspect ratio, will be constant on iPhone Duo due to transitions between outer and inner displays and morphological changes from folding.[14][17] React Native’s `useWindowDimensions` hook is already designed to handle such changes, updating its values when window dimensions change, and should be the default mechanism for size‑based layout decisions.[9][10] Dimensions change events provide an additional hook for non‑React logic or global state updates, though they should be used with caution to avoid stale caches.[9][10]  

A foldable adaptation skill must encode a clear rule: never rely on static window or screen dimensions; always design layout logic to respond to dynamic changes via `useWindowDimensions` and Dimensions events.[9][10][14] It should also caution that dimension changes may occur due to foldable state transitions, multi‑scene placement, or side‑by‑side multitasking on iPhone Duo, and that these changes can happen frequently during user interaction.[14][17]  

### Safe Area and Reserved Regions: Integrating Occlusion and Division

Safe area handling must be expanded to encompass reserved region semantics, particularly occlusions and divisions.[14][15][16] On iPhone Duo, occlusion reserved regions represent areas where hardware elements like cameras or dynamic islands occlude content, while division reserved regions represent folds that split the display into separate regions.[15] These reserved regions intersect view coordinate spaces and must be considered when arranging content, especially interactive controls and lists.[15][16]  

For React Native, libraries like react‑native‑safe‑area-context can continue to provide baseline safe area insets, but apps should augment them with reserved region awareness on iOS, using bridged data from `UIView.reservedRegions` and the fold context.[15] The skill should instruct developers to treat occlusion reserved regions as “hard” insets—areas where content should be avoided—and division regions as split boundaries where content may be separated into panes, not as simple insets.[15][16] It should also advise designing layouts so that you never place essential controls or text in division or occlusion regions, and using reserved region query options to inspect inactive regions for high‑level layout planning.[14][15][16]  

### Navigation and Bars: Embracing Native Containers

Navigation and toolbar design should be firmly anchored in native containers that respect iPhone Duo’s bar behavior.[14][18] React Native apps should use react‑native‑screens and expo‑router to integrate with UINavigationController, UITabBarController, and SwiftUI navigation and tab containers, rather than building custom bars.[5][7][18] A skill should instruct developers to audit their navigation stack for custom toolbars and replace them with native container bar items, pairing `toolbar` modifiers with containers such as NavigationStack or NavigationSplitView in SwiftUI, or setting toolbar items on view controllers used within navigation controllers in UIKit.[18]  

By doing so, apps can participate automatically in iPhone Duo’s vertical bar region, where navigation, toolbar, and tab bar controls may co‑exist vertically along the display edge in certain poses.[18] It also ensures that bar behavior, including positioning and overflow management, aligns with Apple’s design principles and system heuristics.[18] For React Native, this implies close coordination with library updates that expose native toolbar and bar item APIs, and careful testing of bar behavior across Duo poses, including outer and inner displays, split views, and multi‑scene arrangements.[14][17][18]  

### Patterns and Pitfalls to Encode in the Skill

The adaptation skill should encode specific patterns and pitfalls as rules, drawing from Surface Duo, Jetpack WindowManager, and Apple’s Duo guidance.[1][3][6][11][14][15][19] For patterns, it should emphasize list‑detail, extended canvas, two‑page, dual view, and companion pane designs, instructing when each is appropriate and how to map them onto native arrangement or split views.[2][8][19] For pitfalls, it should flag splitting lists across hinges, spanning bottom navigation across two screens, leaving detail panes empty when entering dual mode, and ignoring occlusion or division regions when placing interactive content.[14][15][19]  

The skill should also encode behavioral rules for tabletop layouts: when hinge status indicates half‑open, consider switching to a layout where primary content occupies one side of the fold and controls the other, and avoid placing controls in the fold region.[11][13][16] It should define guidelines for hinge‑driven animations, cautioning against continuous angle‑based updates in JavaScript and recommending discrete thresholds and native animations where necessary.[11][13][16]  

By codifying these patterns and pitfalls, the skill can serve as a practical decision engine for agents adapting React Native and Expo apps, ensuring that prior art and vendor guidance are systematically applied rather than improvised.  

## Library and Stack Design, Build‑vs‑Buy Trade‑offs, and Cross‑Platform Rules

### iOS‑Specific Stack for iPhone Duo vs Cross‑Platform Abstractions

Adapting to iPhone Duo inevitably requires iOS‑specific work, given Apple’s unique APIs and design principles.[12][13][14][15][16][18] However, a well‑designed stack should minimize duplication and maintain cross‑platform abstractions where possible. The key is to distinguish between platform‑specific data sources (reserved regions, hinge interactions, FoldingFeature, DualScreenInfo) and platform‑agnostic layout semantics (panes, list‑detail, tabletop, split vs overlay).  

From a build‑vs‑buy perspective, bridging Apple’s APIs into React Native is a build task: there is no existing React Native module that directly exposes `UIView.reservedRegions` or `UIHingeInteraction`, so teams or library authors must implement this bridging themselves.[12][13][15] However, once bridging is in place, app‑level logic should “buy” semantics from vendor APIs rather than compute them manually. For example, app code should trust reserved region frames, hinge angle and status, and arrangement view behavior, and avoid approximating hinge location or occlusion regions from global screen metrics.[14][15][16]  

On Android, the situation is slightly different: Jetpack WindowManager is already a vendor‑supported library, and React Native bridging for folding features exists or is actively being proposed in the `react-native-dualscreen` repository.[6][11] Teams should prefer “buying” hinge and fold semantics from WindowManager rather than continuing to rely on old DisplayMask packages or writing custom hinge detection code.[6][11] The build work lies in integrating WindowManager at the React Native layer and migrating existing DualScreenInfo logic to use it, which has already been framed in issue #107.[6]  

Cross‑platform abstractions, such as a FoldContext that describes hinge state and reserved regions uniformly, represent a build investment that pays off in maintainability and future‑proofing. The skill should recommend building such abstractions at the library level (e.g., in a shared React Native package) rather than per app, to ensure consistent behavior and reduce duplication.  

### Android Dual‑Screen Stack: WindowManager, DualScreenInfo, and TwoPaneView

On Android, the canonical stack for dual‑screen and foldable support in React Native should consist of Jetpack WindowManager for hinge and fold semantics, combined with DualScreenInfo and TwoPaneView for React Native layout.[1][3][6][11] DualScreenInfo already exposes key methods and events, including `isDualScreenDevice`, `isSpanning`, `windowRects`, and `didUpdateSpanning`, which can be re‑implemented atop WindowManager instead of DisplayMask.[1][3][6] TwoPaneView provides a high‑level layout abstraction that can leverage WindowManager’s FoldingFeature and DualScreenInfo’s window rectangles to place two panes around the hinge.[2][3][8][11][19]  

For a skill, the rule should be to route to these libraries wherever available rather than re‑implementing hinge logic in JavaScript. It should also recommend tracking the status of the migration to WindowManager in the `react-native-dualscreen` repository and prioritizing WindowManager‑based versions once stable.[6][11]  

### Cross‑Platform Pane and Hinge Abstraction

At the library design level, a cross‑platform pane and hinge abstraction is the linchpin for foldable support. Such an abstraction would define types like `Pane`, `DivisionRegion`, `OcclusionRegion`, `HingeState`, and `FoldLayoutMode`, and provide hooks for React components to consume them. On Android, `DivisionRegion` would be derived from WindowManager’s FoldingFeature bounds; on iOS, from `UIView.reservedRegions(kind: .division, options: ...)`.[11][15] `HingeState` would map WindowManager’s state enum and UIHinge.status and angle into a unified set of poses.[11][13]  

With these abstractions in place, layout components such as generic `TwoPaneView`, `ArrangementLayout`, or `TabletopLayout` could be implemented entirely in JavaScript, consuming the cross‑platform context without knowing platform specifics. Expo Router Split View and native arrangement views would remain platform‑specific building blocks, but their usage could be orchestrated via these abstractions, allowing, for example, a list‑detail layout to automatically choose between Split View on iOS and TwoPaneView on Android based on hinge context.  

The build‑vs‑buy trade‑off here is subtle: building cross‑platform abstractions requires design and maintenance investment, but “buying” vendor semantics prevents long‑term drift and bugs. A skill that teaches adaptation should lean toward building these abstractions once, at the library level, and then instructing agents to use them everywhere rather than writing per‑app hinge code.

## Conclusion: Current State, Contrasting Evidence, and Trajectory

The current state of foldable and dual‑screen support for React Native and Expo apps is one of partial maturity and active convergence toward vendor‑supported APIs. On Android, Jetpack WindowManager has emerged as the standard library for foldable and dual‑screen device support, with the FoldingFeature class providing hinge and fold semantics and motivating migration away from Microsoft’s Surface Duo‑specific DisplayMask in packages like `react-native-dualscreeninfo`.[6][11] DualScreenInfo and TwoPaneView already represent robust React Native abstractions for dual‑pane layouts and hinge‑aware geometry, though they remain tied to Surface Duo until WindowManager‑based versions are completed.[1][2][3][8][19]  

On iOS, Apple’s introduction of iPhone Duo and iOS 27.1 brings reserved regions, hinge interactions, arrangement views, and vertical bar behavior into the mainstream, offering a comprehensive native substrate for foldable support.[12][13][14][15][16][18] However, React Native and Expo currently lack official bridging modules for these APIs, and expo‑router’s Split View remains an alpha feature with iOS‑only support and structural constraints.[5][7] Nevertheless, Expo Router Split View demonstrates that native split views can be integrated into React Native and that multi‑column navigation can be expressed declaratively, providing a promising foundation for iPhone Duo support.  

Contrasting evidence in this space primarily concerns the degree to which foldable support should be handled by custom JavaScript layout logic versus native containers and vendor APIs. Some community packages, like `react-native-dual-screen`, have implemented hinge logic and dual‑pane layouts independently, relying on direct Surface Duo APIs, while others advocate for deep integration with Jetpack WindowManager.[4][6][11] Apple’s guidance, meanwhile, strongly favors using system‑provided layouts and bars, discouraging custom navigation or toolbar implementations.[14][18] Taken together, the evidence suggests that a sustainable approach for React Native and Expo apps is to route to native APIs for geometry, hinge state, and navigation chrome, while building cross‑platform abstractions at the React Native layer that interpret these signals into layout modes and patterns.  

Recent changes—particularly the arrival of iPhone Duo and iOS 27.1, and the maturation of Jetpack WindowManager—indicate a trajectory toward more generic and standardized foldable APIs.[11][14][15][16] On Android, WindowManager’s FoldingFeature enables frameworks like React Native, Xamarin, Flutter, and Uno to implement foldable support once and apply it across multiple devices.[6][11] On iOS, reserved regions and hinge interactions are embedded in UIKit and SwiftUI, encouraging app developers to adopt them via system containers and geometry queries rather than ad‑hoc device checks.[14][15][16][18] React Native and Expo are beginning to align with this trajectory through features like Split View and deeper integration with react‑native‑screens, though there is still work to do in bridging iOS foldable APIs and codifying best practices.  

For a skill that teaches agents to adapt or build React Native and Expo apps for iPhone Duo, the decisive answer is therefore as follows. The skill should encode rules that prioritize dynamic geometry and hinge‑aware layout over static device assumptions, insist on using native navigation and bar containers to participate in Duo’s vertical bar behavior, and route to platform‑specific foldable APIs—Jetpack WindowManager and DualScreenInfo on Android, reserved regions and hinge interactions on iOS—rather than reimplementing hinge logic in JavaScript.[1][3][6][11][14][15][18] It should transfer measured failure modes from Android foldables, such as stale Dimensions, symmetric inset math, and device‑model branching, as explicit anti‑patterns, and adopt proven patterns such as two‑pane list‑detail, companion pane, and tabletop layouts, mapping them onto Split View, arrangement views, and TwoPaneView.[8][9][10][11][16][19] By doing so, the skill can provide agents with a structured, evidence‑based approach to foldable adaptation that leverages prior art while embracing the unique capabilities and constraints of iPhone Duo.
