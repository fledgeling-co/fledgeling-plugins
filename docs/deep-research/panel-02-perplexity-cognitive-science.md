### Cognitive Science Frameworks for Multimedia Explainers

The cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML) is the most directly applicable framework for structuring AI-generated ELI5 explanations that combine text, visuals, and interactivity.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> According to CTML, meaningful learning from multimedia arises when learners engage in active cognitive processing across dual channels—verbal and visual—each characterized by severely limited working memory capacity, and when they successfully select relevant information, organize it into coherent representations, and integrate it with prior knowledge.[1][18]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> These assumptions imply that any explainer artifact that synthesizes prose, diagrams, animations, and interactive widgets must be engineered to minimize unnecessary cognitive load (extraneous processing) while allocating capacity for essential and generative processing that supports understanding and transfer.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH>[1][18]

Mayer’s work details evidence-based design principles that operationalize CTML, including the multimedia, coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, voice, personalization, and image principles.[1][18]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> For an ELI5 skill, several of these are particularly critical. The coherence principle requires removing irrelevant text, images, and decorative elements—so-called seductive details—that can distract attention and consume working memory without contributing to learning.[1][10][18]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> The signaling principle emphasizes the use of visual and textual cues such as highlighting, arrows, or labels to direct the learner’s focus to important elements; in an interactive HTML artifact, this could manifest as subtle animations that draw attention to changing variables or as bolded key phrases aligned with specific visual components.[18]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> The segmenting principle advocates breaking content into manageable chunks and allowing learner control over pacing, which meshes naturally with interactive artifacts where users can click or drag to reveal successive layers or to step through dynamic processes.[1][18]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH>

CTML further distinguishes three types of cognitive processing demands—essential, extraneous, and generative—that map closely to design choices in an AI explainer architecture.[1]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Essential processing concerns the mental work needed to represent the core material itself; in an ELI5 explanation of backpropagation, for example, this would include understanding the idea of “adjusting weights to reduce error,” but not the full formalism of partial derivatives.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH><INFERENCE from="[1][18]"> Extraneous processing arises from poor design, such as split-attention layouts where text and corresponding visuals are far apart, unnecessary animations, or overly complex controls; minimizing such processing is crucial to prevent cognitive overload, particularly for novice or young learners.[1][9][10][18]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Generative processing refers to cognitive effort devoted to making sense of the material, such as mentally simulating a process, self-explaining, or connecting new ideas to prior knowledge; interactive simulations and analogy-driven reasoning are primary tools for fostering this kind of processing.[1][8][11]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH>

The implications for Claude Code’s ELI5 skill are substantial.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Rather than simply “simplifying” a complex topic, the system must structure the explanation and its interactive components such that essential processing is prioritized, extraneous processing is minimized, and generative processing is invited through thoughtfully designed tasks, questions, or manipulable elements.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH><INFERENCE from="[1][3][8][9][18]"> For instance, an explainer of a sorting algorithm might show a small set of items and provide controls that let learners step through each comparison, accompanied by concise, conversational narration aligned with the visuals, rather than displaying a large dataset or overwhelming code listings.<CONFIDENCE:MEDIUM><INFERENCE from="[1][8][9][18]"> Similarly, a physics explainer could leverage simplified PhET-like simulations where learners manipulate a few key variables, rather than presenting a full-featured simulation environment that demands high extraneous processing to navigate.[8]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH>

Beyond CTML, research on conceptual understanding and representations in mathematics and science underscores the importance of students’ ability to move between different representations and to understand how these representations relate.[7]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Wedman’s synthesis identifies five aspects that describe a mathematical concept—definition and properties, typical and atypical objects, hierarchical and non-hierarchical relations, processes and procedures, and representations—highlighting that grasping a concept entails linking these aspects through multiple representational forms.[7]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Duval and others argue that the ability to transfer between representations, such as from symbolic to graphical, is essential for conceptual understanding.[7]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> For ELI5 artifacts, this means that explanations should not rely solely on a single visual or textual representation but should scaffold transitions: for example, from a story-based analogy to a simplified diagram to a more formal representation, each layer carefully connected to the previous one.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH><INFERENCE from="[1][7][11][13]">

Studies in science education also emphasize that conceptual understanding is often mediated by representations such as diagrams, physical artifacts, or interactive models and that different representations can make certain properties of a concept more salient.[7]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> In a rational numbers context, for instance, Jäder and colleagues show that students’ conceptual considerations span multiple properties and that their ability to shift between representations plays a crucial role in their understanding.[7]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Translating this to complex systems like neural networks suggests that explainer artifacts should offer multiple representational windows—such as a high-level cartoon of “neurons as buckets of water,” a simplified network diagram, and a more concrete representation of weights and activations—while explicitly helping learners see how these windows relate.<CONFIDENCE:MEDIUM><INFERENCE from="[1][4][7][11]">

Finally, recent research on seductive details and extrinsic motivation provides nuanced insight into when decorative or interesting but irrelevant elements harm or help learning.[10]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Wesenberg and colleagues find that seductive details impair learning when external regulation (for example, high extrinsic motivation or pressure) is high, but are less harmful or even beneficial when external regulation is low, suggesting that the impact of such details is moderated by motivational context.[10]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> For an ELI5 skill, this implies that occasionally embedding curiosity-sparking visuals or micro-stories may not always be detrimental, but their use must be carefully calibrated, and by default, the design should err on the side of coherence, especially in contexts where users are under time pressure or performance demands.<CONFIDENCE:MEDIUM><INFERENCE from="[1][10][18]">

### Progressive Disclosure and Layered Explanations

Progressive disclosure is a UX pattern in which advanced information or functionality is deferred to secondary interfaces, with the primary interface focusing on essential content and actions.[2]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> This approach aims to show users what they need when they need it, thereby improving usability for both novice and experienced users and reducing cognitive load.[2]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Designers commonly use UI patterns like modal windows, accordions, and expandable sections to hide advanced features or details, while keeping essential information visible and easy to access.[2]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> The method relies on clear signifiers (e.g., icons, arrows, labels) that indicate where additional information is available, and often employs onboarding tutorials and contextual help to teach users how to access hidden features.[2]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH>

### Analogy Mapping Constraints and Metaphor Design

Analogy and metaphor are central tools for ELI5 explanations because they can connect abstract or unfamiliar targets to familiar, concrete analogues, thereby reducing intrinsic cognitive load and enabling learners to recruit existing schemas.<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> In science education, analogies such as “the cell is like a factory” or “electricity is like flowing water” have been widely used to help students visualize complex concepts.[11]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Analogy is typically understood as a mapping between two domains: the analogue (or source), often drawn from everyday life, and the target concept, which is often abstract, invisible, or complex.[11]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH> Structure-mapping theory, developed by Gentner and colleagues, posits that analogical mapping primarily involves aligning relational structures rather than individual attributes; successful analogies help learners perceive systems of relations in the target by mapping them to known relational systems in the source.[4][11]<CONFIDENCE:HIGH>
